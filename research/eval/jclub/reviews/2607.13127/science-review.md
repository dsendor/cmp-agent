A. Must address

1. The dimensionless interlayer tunneling in Supp. Tables 5, 7 and 8 appears to be too small by a factor of about 12.5–12.6. That makes the modeled tunneling an order of magnitude weaker relative to Coulomb than the stated meV values. Supp. Table 5 lists "t⊥ × l_B/d (e²/εl_B)" as 0.0023 (Bernal, 10 meV), 0.00041 (ATG, 1.78 meV) and 0.00025 (HTG, 1.09 meV), with d = 3.35 Å and ε_r = 12.5. The column does not depend on field, so it can only mean t⊥/(e²/εd). With e²/(4πε₀) = 1.44 eV·nm, the physical ratio t⊥ε_r d/e² is ≈ 0.029, 0.0052 and 0.0032. The tabulated values equal t⊥/(e²/4πε₀d) with no dielectric constant (10 meV / 4.30 eV = 0.0023), which is the same as dividing the correct value by about 12.57 ≈ 4π. The TMD entries follow the same pattern. For WS₂ (6.32 meV, ε = 14.4, d = 6.229 Å), t⊥ε_r d/e² ≈ 0.0394, against 0.00313 listed (0.0394/4π). Supp. Table 8 (0.0015 ↔ 3.026 meV for WS₂) uses the same conversion. For every compound, the ratio of the tabulated value to t⊥d/e² tracks ε_r/12.5. The divisor is therefore a constant of about 12.5–12.6, not ε_r. This is what one gets from writing the Coulomb scale as e²/(ε₀ε_r l_B) in SI units instead of e²/(4πε₀ε_r l_B). A physical check points the same way. At the full 10 meV, t⊥ ≈ 0.4 e²/εl_B at d/l_B ≈ 0.07, where the Bernal SILC-to-liquid boundary sits in Fig. 3a. Overcoming a tunneling gain of order 2t⊥ there would need a correlation advantage of nearly 1 e²/εl_B, which is implausible in the lowest Landau level. With the tabulated value the advantage needed is a few 10⁻² e²/εl_B, consistent with the figure. (At 10 T, e²/εl_B ≈ 14 meV, so 10 meV is not a small perturbation.) If the code used the tabulated numbers, the modeled tunneling is effectively about 0.8 meV for "Bernal" and 0.14 meV for ATG. ATG and HTG at their stated t⊥ would then lie on the more tunneling-dominated side of the panel now labeled Bernal (Fig. 3a,e). That would overturn the central claim that twisting moves SILC out of the accessible field range, and the B axes of Fig. 3, Supp. Figs. 1 and 4 and the quoted fields (ν_L = 1/2 at B ≈ 3 T for ATG and ≈ 1 T for HTG; B ≲ 8 T at 1/7) would not correspond to the stated materials. The authors should state exactly how t⊥ enters the energy in units of e²/εl_B, including the meaning of the "× l_B/d" label and the definition of the Coulomb unit used in the code, and give the dimensionless t⊥/(e²/εl_B) used at each d/l_B. If the factor is an error, they should recompute Fig. 3, Supp. Figs. 1 and 4 and every quoted field window. They should also check the V₁–V₃ entries, which carry the same unit label, the same way. If it is not an error, they should show how the tabulated numbers follow from the meV values.

2. The twist enters only as a smaller, momentum-local, field-independent, nearest-neighbour intravalley t⊥, and neither the model nor its values are derived for twisted stacks. Yet the conclusion amounts to "SILC loses once t⊥ is small enough", so it is only as good as the mapping from θ to an effective zeroth-Landau-level tunneling. In Eq. (1), H_Tunnel = −t⊥ Σ ∫ ψ†_{l,σ}(r) ψ_{l+1,σ}(r) carries no momentum transfer. In a large-angle twisted pair, however, the K points of adjacent layers differ by ΔK = 2K sin(θ/2). Projected onto the n = 0 Landau level, a tunneling term carrying momentum ΔK has a matrix element of order exp(−|ΔK|² l_B²/4). That is vanishingly small for θ ≳ 10°, where ΔK l_B ~ 10² at 10 T. The residual coupling therefore comes from moiré umklapp and commensurability processes, with its own B and θ dependence, not from a reduced constant. Specific problems: (a) t⊥ = 1.78 and 1.09 meV equal W/4 for the listed bandwidths W = 7.12 and 4.36 meV (Supp. Table 5). The HTG value and bandwidth have no source, and for ATG the mapping from the band structures of refs. 43–44 to an effective zLL hopping is not shown. (b) Ref. 43, cited for ATG, reports magic momenta and three-dimensional Landau levels. That is evidence of strong, non-trivial interlayer coupling, which a uniform nearest-neighbour t⊥ does not capture. (c) In ATG, layers l and l+2 are aligned with no momentum mismatch, so next-nearest-layer tunneling cannot simply be dropped. In Bernal graphite the analogous γ₂ ≈ −20 meV largely sets the zLL dispersion. (d) For HTG "slightly less than 30°" (Supp. Note 3c), the K–K and K–K′ mismatches between neighbouring layers become comparable, so the neglect of intervalley tunneling (Methods) is not justified. (e) The robustness test (Supp. Note 3b) varies only V₁–V₃, and ε_r is fixed at 12.5 with no discussion of interband or anisotropic screening in a graphitic stack. The authors should derive the effective n = 0 interlayer coupling for each stacking, including its B dependence and the l, l+2 terms for ATG, or at least justify the W/4 identification and source the HTG value. Most usefully, they should present the phase boundary at each ν_L as a continuous function of t⊥/(e²/εd) (correctly normalized by ε_r) and d/l_B, with Bernal, ATG, HTG and the TMDs marked, and give the t⊥ range over which the Halperin windows survive below 20 T. They should also show the sensitivity to ε_r and d, which Supp. Table 8 does only partly, and only for TMDs.

3. The metallic competitor is evaluated at a less correlated level than the liquids it is compared with. That biases every SILC–liquid boundary toward the liquids, and this is exactly where the twist acts in Fig. 3. The SILC energy (Supp. Note 2.3, Eqs. S14–S31) is that of an uncorrelated Slater determinant, a filled one-dimensional Fermi sea, evaluated analytically. It contains exchange but no correlation energy. The generalized Halperin liquids and composite-fermion crystals carry Jastrow correlations and are sampled. Correlation energy in a partially filled, dispersing Landau band is not small, and the size of this bias directly sets the critical t⊥ of concern 2. Related omissions point the same way. (a) No correlated metallic competitor is included: a Jastrow-dressed SILC, composite fermions forming a Fermi sea along k_∥, or interlayer-coherent CF states. (b) The k_z density-wave instability of the partially filled one-dimensional band, which the Introduction itself highlights (Fig. 1c, refs. 13–14), is not among the candidates. The staged states are a different, layer-commensurate construction. (c) The gapped liquids gain no second-order tunneling energy (of order −t⊥²/Δ), while SILC gains first-order tunneling energy. (d) The two families carry different finite-size systematics. SILC uses N̄ = 48 with Richardson/Shanks/Euler-Maclaurin acceleration of the layer sum (Eq. S32), while the liquids use Monte Carlo with a 1/N_L fit. The authors should include at least one correlated metallic or density-wave competitor: a Jastrow-correlated SILC, a Hartree-Fock k_z density wave of the one-dimensional band, or at least an RPA-level estimate of the SILC correlation energy. Alternatively, they should argue quantitatively why its correlation gain cannot close the gap. They should also treat the SILC energy at finite N_L in the same way as the liquids, to check that both extrapolate consistently. Without this, the claimed suppression of metallicity is an upper-bound comparison that favours the liquids.

4. The phase diagrams (Fig. 3, Supp. Figs. 1 and 4) are drawn as sharp boundaries showing only the winning state. No energies, energy differences, Monte Carlo errors or extrapolation uncertainties are reported, so the boundaries cannot be assessed. Several details are missing or limited. (a) Methods and Supp. Note 6 state N̄ = 48 particles per layer, 10⁶ samples and a least-squares 1/N_L → ∞ extrapolation, but do not give the N_L values used or the fit quality. A (3111) state couples third neighbours and needs N_L ≥ 7 to avoid wrapping onto itself. (b) There is no extrapolation in particles per layer, only a √(N_φ ν/N̄) density correction, although competing states have different shifts (S = 1 to 9) and therefore different finite-size corrections. (c) Crystal states are assigned N_Φ = N̄/ν̄ − 2p − 1 to match the liquids (Supp. Note 2.2). This is a matching convention, not a thermodynamic choice. (d) The cutoff M_eff (Supp. Table 9) is calibrated only on decoupled 1/3 Laughlin stacks, not on interlayer-correlated states. (e) Supp. Table 9 lists cutoff parameters only for d/l_B ≥ 0.05, about 15 T. The Halperin windows quoted at 1–3 T (d/l_B ≈ 0.013–0.023), and the bars in Fig. 3e–g that reach B = 0, are therefore either computed on an unstated grid or extrapolated. The claimed phases sit where the comparison is most delicate. At d/l_B ≲ 0.06 the layers are nearly coincident, the problem approaches an SU(2N_L)-symmetric limit and many candidates are close to degenerate. At ν_L = 1/7 and 1/9, liquids and Wigner or CF crystals in 2D differ by about 10⁻³ e²/εl_B. That is comparable to typical single-size finite-size errors, and liquid–crystal competition at these fillings is known to be size-sensitive on the sphere. The authors should plot E/N against d/l_B, with error bars that include extrapolation, for the top three to five candidates at ν_L = 1/2, 1/7 and 1/9 in Bernal, ATG and HTG stacking. They should give the winning margin at, for example, 5, 10 and 20 T, and tabulate energies per particle with uncertainties for the winner and its two closest competitors along the boundaries in Fig. 3e–g. They should show the 1/N_L extrapolations, and repeat the key comparisons at other N̄ (for example 24, 36 or 64) or extrapolate in 1/N̄. Finally, they should list the d/l_B grid actually computed, mark the lowest field computed, and mark where boundaries are interpolated.

5. The energies of the winning states come from approximate formulas that are not validated. Every interlayer-coherent winner in Fig. 3 is a product and/or particle-hole conjugate, for example (3111)⊕PH(3111), (3110)⊕(3110) and (3111)⊕(3111). These energies are not sampled directly. They are assembled from seed energies through Eq. (S36) for products and Eq. (S39) for PH conjugates, both marked "≈" with no error estimate. Staged-state energies come from capacitor-like relations (Eqs. S41–S47) that assume d ≪ R and unchanged intralayer correlations. The decisive energies therefore rest on stacked approximations of unknown accuracy, at energy scales where the competition is tight. The authors should evaluate at least the winning product and PH states, and their closest competitors, by direct Monte Carlo on the full wavefunction. They should compare the results with Eqs. (S36), (S39) and the staging formulas and report the discrepancy against the energy margins of concern 4.

7. The winning phase at ν_L = 1/2 needs a consistency check against the SU(2N_L) ferromagnet limit. ν_L = 1/2 is integer total filling per layer, since (3111)⊕PH(3111) gives 1/9 + 8/9 = 1. 9. The candidate space is truncated where the claimed physics occurs. Interlayer correlations stop at third neighbours with the monotonic hierarchy m ≥ n ≥ o ≥ p (Eq. 2, Supp. Note 2). In the emphasized regime, d/l_B ≈ 0.013–0.06 (about 1–20 T), the interlayer Coulomb interaction is nearly as strong as the intralayer one over l_B/d ≈ 20–75 layers. There is little reason to expect optimal correlations to stop at three layers, so longer-range or non-monotonic generalized Halperin states, and genuinely 3D liquids or crystals, are natural competitors. 10. The low-field regime, where the claims are strongest, is where spin polarization and the neglect of Landau-level mixing are least safe. The model assumes spin degeneracy is "completely lifted" by a "sufficiently strong magnetic field" (Results, p. 3). Yet it quotes stability at B ≈ 1–3 T, and Fig. 3f,g show the ν_L = 1/7 and 1/9 windows reaching toward B → 0. At 1–3 T the bare Zeeman energy is about 0.1–0.35 meV. That is far below e²/εl_B (≈ 4.5√B meV, about 4–8 meV) and t⊥, and comparable to the energy differences between competing correlated states. Each graphene layer's zLL has four spin-valley flavours, so spin-unpolarized and spin-valley-entangled Halperin states, spin-singlet CF states, spin textures and canted orders are relevant competitors, and none is among the 862 states. Landau-level mixing (n = ±1) and disorder broadening also grow relative to the gaps. The authors should include spin in the candidate set, at least for ν_L = 1/7 and 1/9 at the lowest fields, or bound its effect. They should estimate the Landau-level-mixing parameter and explain why the phases persist as B → 0 at fixed ν_L, given that t⊥/(e²/εl_B) grows like l_B. They should also qualify or restrict the "as low as 1 T" statements to fields where spin polarization is established.

11. Incompressibility and gap size are not established. The paper calls the winning states fractional quantum Hall phases and proposes them for experiment, but computes no transport or neutral gap. For infinite-layer K matrices the gap can be small when the smallest eigenvalue of K(q) is small. For (3111) the minimum of m + 2n cos q + 2o cos 2q + 2p cos 3q is about 0.37. 12. The Bernal graphite reference, the paper's main control, is a strongly simplified model. The zero-energy Landau band of Bernal graphite is the bilayer-like n = 0, 1 manifold, with orbital as well as valley degeneracy, γ₁ ≈ 0.4 eV, γ₂/γ₅-controlled k_z dispersion and layer-sublattice structure (ref. 45). 15. The experimental realizability of a uniform fractional filling across many layers is not discussed. The proposal needs every layer of a stack of 20 or more layers held at the same ν_L ≈ 1/7–1/9 per valley, about 10¹⁰–10¹¹ cm⁻² per layer.
