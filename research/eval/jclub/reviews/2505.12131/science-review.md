A. Must address

1. The T-linearity claim rests on three-parameter fits over about a factor of 3 in temperature, and these may be picking up an inflection point rather than a genuine power law. Equation 1 is fitted with ρ0, A and n all free over T_c < T < 7.5 K. In practice this is about 2.3-7.5 K (Figs. 1e, 2, 3e-h), roughly half a decade, with about 8-10 points. In this range ρ0 and n are strongly covariant. At 50 T the fitted ρ0 (about 40-43 μΩ cm, Fig. 2d) is an extrapolation about 12 μΩ cm below the lowest data point (Fig. 1e against Fig. 2d). In every high-field panel of Fig. 2 (45, 50, 55, 60 T), and in the 50 T cut of Fig. 1e, the 8.9 K and 10 K points fall well below the extrapolated fit, so ρ(T) bends downward just above the window. The window is also not justified by the zero-field data: Fig. 1a puts the end of T² behaviour at T_Dev ≈ 6 K, below the 7.5 K cut-off. This mixing of regimes is presumably why n = 1.8 rather than 2 at 10 T. In the PPM state the coherence scale is presumably lower still. A curve that goes from convex (Fermi liquid) to concave (approaching a resistivity maximum) passes through a nearly linear stretch at its inflection point, and a free-exponent fit there returns n ≈ 1 whether or not the scattering is T-linear. A minimum of n would then simply mark where that crossover scale is lowest, with no strange metal required. The authors should: (i) show how n(50 T, 36°) and n at the other angles change as the upper limit moves from 5 to 9 K and the lower limit from T_c to 3 K, including a reduced window such as 2.3-5 K; (ii) show dρ/dT against T and the logarithmic derivative d ln(ρ − ρ0)/d ln T at 50 T and at neighbouring fields and angles, with ρ0 fixed independently (for example from the lowest-T normal-state data or from fields where SC3 is absent), so readers can see whether there is a T-independent slope or only an inflection; (iii) report the fit covariance between n and ρ0; (iv) report the temperature T_max of the resistivity maximum against angle and field, and check whether the minimum in n follows it; (v) where SC3 can be avoided (angles just outside the SC3 window, fields above H_c2^SC3 near 44°, or a small a-axis tilt), extend the cuts below 2 K to test whether linearity continues as T → 0 or a Fermi-liquid regime returns at a suppressed T_FL(H), as a conventional metamagnetic crossover would predict. Without this, the result should be described as a minimum in the effective exponent, not an established T-linear state.

2. The fixed-field cuts sit on the steep, temperature-dependent tail of the resistivity peak at the first-order metamagnetic transition, which can produce an apparent T-linear ρ(T) by itself. In Figs. 1c, 2 and 3a-d, ρ(H) has a large peak at H* (about 45 T at 36°) followed by a steep negative magnetoresistance. The peak height, width and position change with temperature, and above the critical end-point temperature the first-order step becomes a crossover. The T-linear cut at 50 T (Figs. 1e, 2d) and the H* + 5 T cuts (Figs. 3e-h, 4b,e) lie on this tail, and Fig. 1d places the minimum of n at about 48 T, close to the peak. A fixed-H slice therefore samples a different distance from the peak at each temperature and picks up a contribution (dρ/dH)·(dH*/dT) that has nothing to do with inelastic scattering in a homogeneous phase. The effect is specific to the key angle. At 36° the tail is steepest and the peak largest, and the authors' own earlier work (ref. 28) shows the critical end point moving towards zero temperature with tilt, so the end-point temperature or the crossover broadening may fall inside the 2-7.5 K fit window exactly at the angles of interest. The strong angle dependence of n at H* + 5 T (3.0 at 25°, 1.0 at 36°) could then reflect how the peak shape and end-point temperature change with angle. Defining the red points from a single H* per angle does not deal with this. The authors should: report H*(T) and the peak width against T for each angle; repeat the exponent extraction at fixed H − H*(T), in reduced field H/H*(T), and at several offsets (for example H* + 2, 3, 5, 8, 10, 15 T) at each angle; state the critical end-point temperature at each angle relative to the fit window; and estimate the (dρ/dH)·(dH*/dT) term from the measured dρ/dH at 50 T and dH*/dT, showing that n ≈ 1 persists far enough from the peak that this term cannot account for the linear behaviour and does not track the steepness of dρ/dH.

3. The sample temperature during the pulse, just above a first-order metamagnetic transition, is not established. The n ≈ 1 region lies a few tesla above H*, so every isotherm has crossed, milliseconds earlier, a first-order transition with a magnetization jump of about 0.5 μB/U and a large entropy change. In a quasi-adiabatic pulse, magnetocaloric heating or cooling, latent heat and hysteretic losses at H*, plus eddy-current heating in a metallic sample at up to 62 T, can shift the sample temperature by an amount comparable to the 0.5-1 K spacing of the isotherms. The shift would be largest at the lowest bath temperatures, where the heat capacity is smallest. If the true sample temperature at 50 T is above nominal for the 2-3 K curves, ρ is overestimated at the low-T end of every cut. That reduces the curvature and lowers the fitted n, preferentially just above H*, exactly where n ≈ 1 is found. Because each ρ(T) cut is assembled from separate pulses at different starting temperatures, such an error enters n directly. The main text does not state the sweep direction used, whether up- and down-sweeps agree, the pulse duration and dB/dt, or how the temperature was known during the pulse. The Supplementary Materials, said to contain the method, are not in the submitted PDF. The authors should: compare up-sweep and down-sweep ρ(H) at the same nominal T for θ = 36°, and the n(H) extracted from each, including whether the falling-field data match at 50 T; show that n at 50 T does not change with pulse length, dB/dt or peak field (for example pulses stopping below and well above H*); where possible add a DC-field cross-check at 36° below about 45 T, or a thermometer on the sample; and estimate the magnetocaloric temperature change at H* from the known magnetization jump and specific heat, showing that it is small compared with the isotherm spacing. If these checks are in the Supplement, the main text should summarize them.

4. The data do not support the claim that the strange metal "encompasses" SC3 or is confined to its strongest region. (a) At 36°, n is 1.5 at 55 T (Fig. 2e) and returns to about 1.9 by 60 T (Figs. 1d, 2f), yet SC3 at this angle extends to about 65-73 T (Fig. 1b; ref. 26), and Fig. 1c shows zero resistance at 0.5 K up to about 58 T. At 44°, SC3 is clearly present (zero resistance near 55 T, Fig. 3d), yet n = 1.6 (Fig. 3h). T-linearity is therefore confined to a narrow band a few tesla above the lower, metamagnetic boundary of SC3, not spread over the dome. That fits a link to the metamagnetic transition at least as naturally as a link to SC3. (b) The SC3, SC2 and PM boundaries (white lines) in Figs. 1b and 4a,d are schematic boundaries from the literature (refs. 21, 22, 26-29), not boundaries measured on these samples, even though the 0.5 K traces in Figs. 1c and 3c,d contain that information. (c) The heatmaps interpolate between about seven or eight measured angles (vertical lines in Fig. 4a: about 15°, 18°, 25°, 32°, 36°, 36.5°, 37°, 44°), three of them clustered at 36-37°, with nothing between 37° and 44° or between 25° and 32°. The "bright yellow region centred around 35°", its shape and its apparent coincidence with the dome are mostly set by the undescribed interpolation. (d) The exponent was effectively sampled only along the line H* + 5 T and at 10 T. Because the red points are defined as H* + 5 T, they lie inside SC3 by construction for 30-40° and outside it elsewhere, so the angle of minimal n and the SC3 window are not independent tests. The authors should: determine T_c^SC3(θ, H) from their own data (for example the onset of zero resistance at 0.5 K and the higher isotherms) and overlay the measured boundaries; show the n and A maps without interpolation, or mark the measured (θ, H) grid explicitly, and describe any interpolation; add angles between 25° and 32° and between 37° and 44°; give full n(H) curves like Fig. 1d at every measured angle, covering the full SC3 field range; and plot n against H − H* for all angles on one graph. The title, abstract and Fig. 4 title should then be reworded to match what is shown, for example "T-linear resistivity near H* at the low-field edge of SC3, near its optimal angle", rather than strange metallicity that "encompasses" or "underpins" SC3.

5. The Planckian estimate (α = 1.3 ± 0.6) cannot be assessed from the submission, and its inputs appear ill-suited to the polarized paramagnetic state. The calculation is described only qualitatively (DFT bare and renormalized plasma frequencies, "fittings of the SC3 domain" and a coherence length), and all details are in Supplementary Materials that are not part of the submitted PDF. The DFT plasma frequencies come from the low-field Fermi surface (refs. 31-35). Above H*, UTe2 has crossed a first-order transition with a magnetization jump of about 0.5 μB/U, and ρ0 rises from about 1 μΩ cm (Fig. 1a) to about 40-50 μΩ cm at 50 T (Figs. 1e, 2d), a jump in resistivity of more than 40 μΩ cm that signals a strong change in carriers or scattering. A Fermi-surface reconstruction or a change in effective mass across H* is therefore likely, and it would change the plasma frequency entering α. Taking an effective mass from H_c2 or the coherence length of SC3 also assumes an orbital limit and a clean-limit relation, which are questionable for a field-reentrant, putatively triplet state. The uncertainty in the geometric factor of the small pulsed-field samples enters the slope as well. The quoted ±0.6 (about 45-50%) allows α anywhere from about 0.7 to 1.9, so "occurs at the Planckian rate" is not tightly constrained. The authors should: include the full derivation in the paper, stating which quantities enter α, which were fitted and which are independent, and which are taken from low-field data; show how α depends on each input; propagate the uncertainty from a possible Fermi-surface change across H* (for example a range of plausible n/m* in the PPM state) and from the geometric factor; and cross-check against any independent measure of the PPM-state Fermi surface or mass, such as the Sommerfeld coefficient above H* from the literature, the Kadowaki-Woods ratio from the T² coefficient at 60 T, or Hall or quantum-oscillation data above H*. If α cannot be pinned down, it should be presented as a conditional, order-of-magnitude estimate, and "Planckian" should be softened in the title and abstract.

6. Superconducting fluctuations above T_c^SC3 may affect the lowest-temperature points and bias the fitted exponent. At about 35-36° and 50 T, T_c^SC3 reaches about 2-2.4 K (text, p. 6), which is the lowest temperature included in the fits (Figs. 1e, 2d). Paraconductivity just above T_c lowers ρ at the low-T end, which is where it most affects the fitted ρ0 and n, and biases n downwards. The bias would be largest at the angles and fields where SC3 is strongest, which would artificially correlate the exponent anomaly with SC3. The authors should exclude points within about 1 K of the local T_c^SC3(H, θ) measured on their own samples (or raise the lower fit bound to about 1.5-2 T_c), show that n is unchanged, and compare with cuts at the same field just outside the SC3 angular range.

7. The A-coefficient map is biased by construction. Figs. 4d-f and the text (p. 8) use "the initial increment of the fitted resistivity between 0 K and 1 K", which is ρ(1 K) − ρ0 = A·1^n, numerically just A. It is read from a region (0-1 K) below T_c, below the lowest data point at about 2.3 K, where there are no normal-state data. For n = 1 this increment is the slope; for n = 2 it is small by construction. For a fixed rise across the fit window it is mechanically larger when n is smaller, and the n-A covariance makes it depend strongly on n. The claimed "order of magnitude" enhancement in Figs. 4d,e may therefore partly restate the drop in n; the large error bars at 32° and 37° in Fig. 4e already hint at this. The units of A (μΩ cm K^-n) also differ from point to point. The authors should map a dimensionally consistent, model-independent quantity against θ and H for both field sets, such as dρ/dT at a fixed temperature inside the window (for example 4 K), (ρ(T1) − ρ(T2))/(T1 − T2) over the measured window, or ρ(5 K) − ρ(T_c⁺).

8. The number of samples, the angle calibration and the misalignment are not given in the main text. The main text does not say how many crystals were measured, which angles came from which sample, whether each angle is a separate sample or mounting, or whether the n ≈ 1 result at about 36° was reproduced on more than one crystal. 9. Conventional explanations for the apparent T-linearity are not discussed adequately. Near a metamagnetic transition in heavy-fermion systems the coherence scale is commonly suppressed, and over a factor-of-3 window the crossover from incoherent to coherent transport can produce an apparently linear ρ(T) without a quantum critical point; the zero-field deviation from T² at about 6 K (Fig. 1a) shows the coherence scale is already low. The Lifshitz scenario is dismissed using the simplicity of the low-field Fermi surface (ref. 33), which does not apply above H*. The novelty claim, that T-linear resistivity has "not previously been associated with a spin-triplet superconductive state", should be weighed against field-reentrant superconductivity in URhGe and UCoGe, where an enhanced A coefficient and non-Fermi-liquid transport near the reorientation field accompany reentrant, putatively triplet pairing. The authors should address these alternatives explicitly and say what would distinguish them, for example magnetostriction, specific heat or Hall data over the same (θ, H) window.
