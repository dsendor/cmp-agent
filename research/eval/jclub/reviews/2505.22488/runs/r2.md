Review outline:

**1. Significance and novelty**

The manuscript reports a large difference between the two cross-circular Raman configurations (LR and RL) for the E_g phonons of single-crystal NiTiO3, a centrosymmetric, non-magnetic ferroaxial crystal (point group -3). The sign reverses between the front and back faces of a single-domain crystal, and spatial maps of g_ROA follow the ferroaxial domains of a multidomain crystal. The authors support this with a symmetry analysis, DFT phonons and a tight-binding model of the Raman susceptibility. Similar cross-circular intensity differences have already been seen in 1T-TaS2, which also has point group -3, and in NiCo2TeO6. The new elements are a clean, bulk, insulating three-dimensional ferroaxial system, domain imaging, and an attempt at a microscopic mechanism. The experimental observation is convincing and useful for domain imaging. The mechanistic claims are stated more strongly than the analysis supports.

**2. Potential reasons for acceptance**

- The effect is large (|g_ROA| of about 1 for E_g(1) at 785 nm) and robust. The sign reverses between the front and back of the same crystal (Fig. 3, Table S4, Fig. S6) and between domains in the high-resolution line scan (Fig. S10). An instrumental origin therefore cannot explain the dominant signal.
- Internal checks support the selection rules: E_g modes appear only in the cross-circular channel and A_g modes only in the parallel-circular channel (Fig. 2c, Fig. S7). The A_g modes show no LL/RR difference.
- The Stokes and anti-Stokes signals have the same sign, which is consistent with a time-reversal-even origin.
- The Raman domain map of the multidomain crystal (Fig. 5c) is qualitatively similar to electrogyration imaging (Fig. 5d). This gives a practical, contact-free probe of ferroaxial domains with micrometre resolution (Fig. S10).
- The symmetry derivation of the circular-basis Raman tensors (Sec. S3) is explicit and reproducible.

**3. Concerns**

**A. Must address**

1. **Absorption is required for the effect, and the paper never says so. This undercuts the claimed distinction from earlier mechanisms.**
   - *What the paper says.* Page 8 states that the static ferroaxial distortion "renders 1E_g and 2E_g inequivalent, leading to |R1| ≠ |R2|". The abstract and summary present the effect as intrinsic electron–phonon coupling "within the electric dipole approximation". The introduction contrasts this with the earlier attribution of the 1T-TaS2 effect to "complex Raman tensors owing to optical absorption" (Ref. [4]).
   - *What the symmetry actually allows.* In -3, the in-plane E_g Raman tensor pair has the forms (c d; d -c) and (d -c; -c -d). The D3d (non-ferroaxial) case has d = 0.
     - Comparing with Eq. (2) gives R1 = (c − id)/2 and R2 = (c + id)/2.
     - It follows that |R1|² − |R2|² ∝ Im(c d*).
     - Ferroaxial order is needed for d ≠ 0, but this alone gives no ROA. A relative phase between c and d is also needed.
   - *Where that phase comes from.* In the transparent, non-resonant limit the Raman tensor of a time-reversal-invariant crystal is real, and the ROA vanishes. The authors' own model shows exactly this: in Fig. 4, Ī_LR = Ī_RL = 0.5 for all ω below the JDOS onset. Their own data show it too: g ≈ 0 at 633 nm, where absorption is "minimal" (Table S4).
   - *Why it matters.* The mechanism is therefore "ferroaxial-allowed off-diagonal component plus an absorption- or resonance-induced phase". That is essentially the ingredient invoked in Ref. [4]. The claims that the mechanism was previously "unknown" and is now established need to be reframed. The phrase "even within the electric dipole approximation" is also not a surprise: the d component is allowed in electric-dipole Raman scattering by -3 symmetry alone.
   - *What the authors should do.*
     - State the Im(c d*) condition explicitly.
     - Explain the statement in Sec. S3 that resonance "does not change the selection rules" against the fact that resonance is what makes the ROA non-zero.
     - Revise the comparison with Refs. [4–6] accordingly.

2. **The model calculation is not connected quantitatively to NiTiO3, and it does not address the mode dependence that the data show.**
   - *What the model is.* Fig. 4 and Sec. S8 use:
     - spinless p orbitals on a triangular two-sublattice lattice;
     - hopping parameters chosen by hand (Eq. S50), including t_ax = 0.1;
     - ω in arbitrary units;
     - a phenomenological phonon vertex X_Q (Eqs. S47–S48) that is the same for every E_g mode.
   - *What the experiment involves.* The resonance invoked is a local, parity-forbidden Ni2+ d–d transition (3A2g → 3T1g), not a p-band interband transition.
   - *What the data show that the model cannot address.*
     - g_ROA varies strongly between modes and changes sign: E_g(1) +0.96, E_g(2) −0.16, E_g(3) +0.21, E_g(4) −0.01, E_g(5) +0.47 (front face, 785 nm).
     - The model predicts that the sign of Ī_LR − Ī_RL itself changes with ω (crossings near ω ≈ 1.38 and 1.42 in Fig. 4). The sign at a single excitation energy is therefore not a prediction the model can confirm.
   - *The one mode-specific argument.* The only mode-specific explanation is qualitative: E_g(1) has the largest Ni displacement (page 9).
   - *Why it matters.* The abstract states that the calculations "reveal" that the ROA "originates from the ferroaxial order". The model shows only that a ferroaxial term is a necessary parameter (Eqs. S64–S65), and that conclusion already follows from symmetry.
   - *What the authors should do.*
     - Tabulate the Ni displacement amplitude, and ideally the projected Ni/O content, of each E_g eigenvector from the DFT calculation (Fig. S3) against the measured g_ROA, including its sign.
     - Better, compute resonant Raman tensors (c, d and their phase) for the five E_g modes in a d-orbital or ab initio framework.
     - Otherwise, tone down the claim to "a minimal model consistent with the symmetry".

**B. Should address**

3. **The instrumental baseline and polarization purity are not established, and an offset is visible in the data.**
   - *The offset.* The front and back values in Table S4 do not reverse symmetrically. Averaging front and back gives about −0.04 (E_g(1)), −0.025 (E_g(2)), −0.03 (E_g(3)), −0.03 (E_g(4)) and 0.00 (E_g(5)). This is a common offset of roughly −0.03, larger than the quoted ±0.02 error. E_g(4) does not change sign at all (−0.01 front, −0.05 back).
   - *Why the stated control does not work.* The paper offers g ≈ 0 off-sample and near cracks (page 10) as evidence against instrumental offsets. Where there is no Raman signal, g is the ratio of two noise values, so this is not a control.
   - *Polarization purity at 633 nm.* The 633 nm cross-circular spectrum (Fig. S5) clearly contains the A_g(3) line at about 398 cm⁻¹. This line should be forbidden in that channel, so the polarization at 633 nm is imperfect. A wavelength-mismatched or imperfect quarter-wave plate mixes the LR and RL channels and reduces g. The near-zero ROA at 633 nm and the small ROA at 532 nm (the resonance evidence) could then be partly instrumental.
   - *What the authors should do.*
     - State whether a separate quarter-wave plate was used for each wavelength.
     - Quantify the circular-polarization extinction at each wavelength, for example the A_g leakage ratio in the cross channel.
     - Measure a null reference under identical conditions. The best choice is a non-ferroaxial corundum (D3d) crystal that absorbs at the excitation wavelength, for example ruby (Cr:Al2O3) with Cr3+ d–d absorption. Its E_g modes must give g = 0, which would separate "absorption alone" from "absorption plus ferroaxial order".
     - Subtract the measured instrumental offset.

4. **The resonance assignment rests on three wavelengths and an absorption spectrum from the literature.**
   - *What is shown.* Absorption at 785, 532 and 633 nm is described from Ref. [25], which concerns NiTiO3 nanocrystallites, not this crystal. No absorption or penetration-depth data are shown for the 60 µm single crystal along c. The 532 nm data exist only for the front face (Table S4), so the sign of the 532 nm signal cannot be separated from the offset in concern 3. All 532 nm values are positive, including E_g(2) and E_g(4), which are negative at 785 nm.
   - *Why it matters.* The model predicts that both the magnitude and the sign of the ROA vary across the absorption band (Fig. 4). Three points cannot test this.
   - *What the authors should do.*
     - Show the c-axis absorption spectrum of the measured crystal.
     - Report front and back g_ROA at 532 nm.
     - Ideally, measure g_ROA for E_g(1) with a tunable source (for example Ti:sapphire, 700–850 nm) across the 3A2g → 3T1g band, and compare the profile with the absorption line shape.

5. **The local-symmetry argument on page 9 is incorrect as stated.**
   - *The claim.* The text says the ferroaxial distortion "further lowers the local symmetry from 3m to 3" at the Ni site.
   - *The problem.* In the corundum structure R-3c, the cation site (Wyckoff 12c) already has site symmetry 3, not 3m. In R-3, the Ni site (6c) also has symmetry 3. The ordering transition changes cation occupancy and removes the glide, not the point symmetry of the cation site. Sec. S3 itself states that the Ni site symmetry is 3.
   - *Why it matters.* This is part of the argument for why the d–d resonance couples selectively to the ferroaxial order.
   - *What the authors should do.* Correct the statement and reformulate the argument in terms of the loss of the c-glide and the resulting d ≠ 0 tensor component, rather than in terms of site symmetry.

6. **The comparison between the domain images is qualitative, and the absolute sign is not assigned.**
   - *The image comparison.* Fig. 5(d) is adapted from Ref. [23] and "inverted from the original". The text does not say whether it shows the same crystal and the same field of view as Fig. 5(c), or why the inversion is justified.
   - *The sign assignment.* The paper never states which sign of g_ROA corresponds to A+ and which to A−. That assignment is needed if Raman scattering is to serve as a domain probe.
   - *The magnitude in the multidomain crystal.* g_ROA in that map is only about ±0.3–0.4 (Fig. 5c), against about ±0.8 in the single-domain crystal (Fig. 5a). In the line scan of Fig. S10 it is about ±0.7. Possible reasons are not discussed: through-thickness domain overlap within the confocal depth, a different numerical aperture or spot size, or local strain.
   - *The single-domain claim.* The single-domain state is "confirmed by electrogyration", but no data are shown in this manuscript.
   - *What the authors should do.*
     - Register the Raman and electrogyration images on the same region.
     - Give a pixel-by-pixel correlation or sign-agreement fraction.
     - Explain the inversion of the electrogyration image and the reduced amplitude in the multidomain crystal.
     - Include the electrogyration image of the single-domain crystal.

7. **Sample statistics and key measurement parameters are missing.**
   - *Samples.* The headline result comes from one single-domain crystal and one multidomain crystal. Reproducibility on a second single-domain crystal, or on a different face preparation, is not shown. The front and back surfaces of a flux-grown platelet may differ in surface quality. The paper does not say whether they were polished.
   - *Parameters and heating.* Laser power, objective numerical aperture, the spot size used for Fig. 3, and acquisition times are not reported. At 785 nm the excitation is resonant with an absorption band, so local heating is possible. The Stokes/anti-Stokes ratio in Fig. 3 could be used to estimate the local temperature. g_ROA should be shown to be independent of power.
   - *Polarization at high numerical aperture.* A high-NA objective depolarizes circularly polarized light. The NA matters for how pure the cross-circular channels are.
   - *Intensity extraction and errors.* The paper does not explain how the intensities in Eq. (1) were extracted (peak height or fitted area, and the background model), nor how the ±0.02 error in Table S4 was obtained. That error looks small compared with the pixel-to-pixel scatter of roughly ±0.2–0.3 visible in Fig. 5(a,b).
   - *What the authors should do.* Add all of the above.

8. **Terminology and the comparison of magnitudes are misleading.**
   - *The 10⁻³ comparison.* The statement that g_ROA ≈ 1 is "several orders of magnitude larger" than molecular natural ROA (about 10⁻³) compares different physics. Molecular natural ROA is a reciprocal effect, averaged over orientation, arising from interference between electric dipole and magnetic dipole or electric quadrupole terms in transparent media. Here the signal is a directional, resonance-enabled electric-dipole effect along a single crystal axis.
   - *The effect is direction-dependent.* The paper itself shows that the sign follows A·k (front versus back). The signal therefore averages to zero over orientations, for example in a powder. This is the opposite of what "optical activity" normally denotes.
   - *What the authors should do.* Remove the 10⁻³ comparison or qualify it, and clarify that the observable is a directional cross-circular intensity difference governed by A·k. "Ferroaxial" or "directional" Raman circular intensity difference would be clearer than "natural ROA" for an achiral crystal.

**4. Suggestions for improvement**

1. Recast the theoretical message around the -3 Raman tensor (c, d) and the condition Im(c d*) ≠ 0. Ferroaxial order supplies d, and resonant absorption supplies the phase. This is simpler, general and testable, and it unifies the NiTiO3 and 1T-TaS2 observations.
2. Add a resonant, non-ferroaxial null sample, such as ruby, measured with the same optics. This would cleanly separate the ferroaxial contribution from any absorption-only or instrumental effect.
3. Measure g_ROA of E_g(1) across the Ni2+ d–d band with a tunable source, on both faces. Compare with the measured absorption spectrum and with the sign changes predicted in Fig. 4.
4. Calibrate the absolute sign of g_ROA against A+ and A− using an independently determined domain, for example by electrogyration or circularly polarized second-harmonic generation on the same spot. With this calibration, the Raman map becomes a quantitative domain-imaging tool.
