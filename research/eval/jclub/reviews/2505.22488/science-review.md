A. Must address

1. The proposed mechanism leaves out absorption, which is a necessary second ingredient, and this changes both the main claim and its novelty relative to Ref. [4]. On p. 8 the paper says the static ferroaxial distortion "renders 1E_g and 2E_g inequivalent, leading to |R1| ≠ |R2|". The abstract and summary present the effect as intrinsic electron–phonon coupling "within the electric dipole approximation", and the introduction (p. 3) calls the mechanism "unknown" and contrasts it with the attribution of the 1T-TaS2 effect to "complex Raman tensors owing to optical absorption" (Ref. [4]). However, the in-plane E_g Raman tensors allowed in -3 have the Cartesian forms (c, d; d, -c) and (d, -c; -c, -d). Comparing with Eq. (2) gives R1 = (c − id)/2 and R2 = (c + id)/2, so I_LR ∝ |c − id|², I_RL ∝ |c + id|², and I_LR − I_RL ∝ Im(c d*). Two conditions are therefore needed: d ≠ 0, which requires the loss of the vertical glide/mirror (in -3m, d = 0), i.e. the ferroaxial order; and a relative phase between c and d, which requires dissipation or resonance. With real, non-resonant tensors in a time-reversal-invariant transparent crystal the asymmetry vanishes. The paper's own results show this: in Fig. 4 the model gives Ī_LR = Ī_RL = 0.5 below the JDOS onset (ω < 1.38), and the experiment gives g_ROA ≈ 0 at 633 nm, where absorption is minimal (Table S4). The mechanism is thus "ferroaxial-allowed off-diagonal component plus an absorption-induced phase", which is a ferroaxial-allowed version of the Ref. [4] ingredient, not a separate mechanism; the d component itself is allowed in electric-dipole Raman scattering by -3 symmetry alone, so "even within the electric dipole approximation" is not a surprise. The end of Sec. S3 argues that interference "cannot give rise to ROA" because 1E_g and 2E_g are eigenmodes, and that resonance "does not change the selection rules"; both need reconciling with the Cartesian picture, where the effect is precisely an interference between the (xx−yy) and xy components and resonance is what makes it non-zero. The authors should state the Im(c d*) condition in the main text (ferroaxial order supplies d ≠ 0, absorption supplies the phase); revise the abstract and conclusion so the probe is described as working under resonant or absorbing conditions; explain what the tight-binding calculation adds beyond this condition, including model curves showing that g_ROA goes to zero as t_ax → 0 and as η_J, η_X → 0 away from resonance; and state clearly how the mechanism relates to, and differs from, Refs. [4–7].

2. The correlation between the Raman map and the ferroaxial domains is not shown on the same region, and the absolute sign is not assigned. The claim that g_ROA tracks the ferroaxial domain pattern, rather than only the crystal face, rests on Fig. 5(c) against Fig. 5(d), and the abstract and conclusion base the "correlates with the ferroaxial domain structure" claim and the domain-imaging application on it. Fig. 5(d) is "adapted from Ref. [23] (image inverted from the original)". The text does not say whether it shows the same crystal, the same area or the same orientation as Fig. 5(c), and the two do not visibly match: (d) has sharp, straight stripes and large blocks, while (c) shows diffuse, lower-contrast patches at 20 µm sampling. "Inverted" is ambiguous (a colour-sign inversion and a spatial mirror flip have very different implications), and a sign inversion is an unexplained free choice that fixes the claimed sign correspondence. The paper never states which sign of g_ROA corresponds to A+ and which to A−. The single-domain state is "confirmed by electrogyration", but no such data are shown. In addition, |g_ROA| in the multidomain map is only about 0.2–0.5 (Fig. 5c), against about 0.6–0.8 in the single-domain map (Fig. 5a), about ±0.7–0.75 in the line scan of the same crystal (Fig. S10a) and about 1 in Table S4. With roughly 100 µm domains and a 3.3 µm spot, lateral averaging cannot explain this; possible causes include domains stacked through the thickness within the probed depth, partial Ni/Ti antisite disorder after the 1620 K anneal, a different focal depth, NA or spot size, or local strain. The authors should acquire electrogyration (or circularly polarized SHG, Ref. [16]) and Raman maps of the same region of the same multidomain crystal with fiducial marks; show them co-registered at the same scale; quantify the correspondence (for example a pixel-wise correlation or sign-agreement fraction between sign(g_ROA) and sign(ΔT/T)); state the absolute relation between the sign of g_ROA and A+/A−, including how the electrogyration sign maps onto the domain state; include the electrogyration image of the single-domain crystal; and explain the reduced amplitude in the multidomain crystal. If co-registration is impossible, they should at minimum state precisely what "inverted" means and whether the images show the same sample and area.

3. A bulk origin is not separated from a near-surface or field-induced one. The front/back reversal shows that the signal follows an axial, time-even quantity along c. But any surface breaks inversion, and the product n·A of the surface normal and the ferroaxial vector is a pseudoscalar whose sign is opposite on the two faces. A surface-localized gyrotropic layer would therefore reproduce exactly the front/back reversal and the domain contrast, as would electrogyration driven by a surface or depletion field, which NiTiO3 is known to show (Refs. [15, 23]). These would be ROA from an inversion-broken region, not from the centrosymmetric bulk stressed in the title and abstract. The setup is confocal (Sec. S2) and the crystal is 60 µm thick, so the authors should measure g_ROA(E_g^(1)) as a function of focal depth through the crystal and compare the depth profile with the confocal depth resolution and the 785 nm absorption length; ideally they should also measure g_ROA under an applied electric field along c to bound any electrogyration contribution. A depth-independent g_ROA inside the bulk would substantiate the "centrosymmetric bulk" claim.

B. Should address

4. The instrumental baseline and polarization purity are not established, and an offset is visible in the data. A single QWP between the non-polarizing beam splitter and the objective defines both the incident and analysed polarizations (Fig. S1, Sec. S2); the beam splitter's polarization-dependent phase, any QWP retardance error at each of the three wavelengths, and high-NA depolarization in a uniaxial crystal can all produce a fixed-sign LR/RL imbalance, and none is characterised. The front and back values in Table S4 do not reverse symmetrically: their means are about −0.04 (E_g(1)), −0.025 to −0.03 (E_g(2)), −0.03 (E_g(3)), −0.03 (E_g(4)) and 0.00 (E_g(5)), a common offset of roughly −0.03, larger than the quoted ±0.02 error, and E_g(4) does not change sign at all (−0.01 front, −0.05 back). At 532 nm the values (0.10, 0.01, 0.10, 0.05, 0.09) are front-face only and all positive, including E_g(2) and E_g(4), which are negative at 785 nm, so they cannot be separated from an instrumental offset; this undermines the "discernible ROA signals at 532 nm" (Sec. S5). The A_g(3) line at about 398 cm⁻¹ appears in the 633 nm cross-circular spectrum (Fig. S5), where it should be forbidden, indicating imperfect circular polarization at that wavelength; a mismatched QWP mixes LR and RL and reduces g, so the 633 nm null and the small 532 nm signal (the resonance evidence) could be partly instrumental. The only stated control, g_ROA ≈ 0 "outside the sample or near cracks" (p. 10), is not a control, because without a phonon peak g is a ratio of noise. The authors should specify the QWP (zero-order or achromatic, design wavelength, whether a separate plate is used per wavelength) and the beam splitter; report the circular extinction ratio at each wavelength (A_g leakage into LR/RL, E_g leakage into LL/RR); measure back-face spectra at 532 and 633 nm; measure a non-ferroaxial -3m reference with E_g modes under identical 785 nm conditions, where g_ROA must vanish, for example α-Al2O3 or calcite, and preferably an absorbing one such as ruby (Cr:Al2O3, Cr3+ d–d absorption) to separate "absorption alone" from "absorption plus ferroaxial order"; preferably measure a chiral reference of known handedness such as α-quartz to confirm the L/R convention; and subtract the measured instrumental offset.

S37–S51, Fig. 4) uses spinless p orbitals on a two-sublattice triangular lattice with dispersive bands, hand-chosen hoppings (t1–t6 = 0.1–1.0, t_ax = 0.1, μ = −1.05, Eq. The model shows only that a ferroaxial term is a necessary parameter (Eqs. S64–S65), which already follows from symmetry. Statements in the abstract and main text that the calculations "reveal"/"show that the observed NROA originates directly from the ferroaxial order" and "support the experimental results obtained with different light sources" (p. 9) should therefore be reworded as a symmetry-level, minimal-model proof of principle. Alternatively the authors should tie the model to NiTiO3 (d orbitals with a realistic crystal field, ω mapped onto measured transition energies, a mode-resolved electron–phonon vertex), justify the parameters, and show that the ROA amplitude scales linearly with t_ax at small t_ax, as Eqs. 11. The local-symmetry argument on p. 9 appears incorrect. The text says the ferroaxial distortion "further lowers the local symmetry from 3m to 3" at the Ni site. In corundum R-3c the cation site (Wyckoff 12c) already has site symmetry 3, and in R-3 the Ni site (6c) also has symmetry 3; the ordering transition changes cation occupancy and removes the c-glide, not the point symmetry of the cation site. Sec. S3 itself gives the Ni site symmetry as 3. Since this statement motivates why the d–d resonance couples selectively to the ferroaxial order, the authors should correct it and reformulate the argument in terms of the loss of the c-glide and the resulting d ≠ 0 tensor component.

12. The Stokes/anti-Stokes argument for a non-magnetic origin is asserted, not derived. The claim on p. 7 that the same sign in Stokes and anti-Stokes indicates a non-magnetic origin, "in contrast to magnetic ROA where the sign reverses", is transferred from molecular ROA (Refs. [29–31]), which concerns different geometries and observables, without showing that it applies to the degenerate 1E_g/2E_g pair. The model reproduction (Fig. 4 inset) is uninformative because δω = ±10⁻³ is far smaller than the smearing η = 0.01, so Stokes and anti-Stokes are identical by continuity. At 295 K, well above T_N = 22.5 K with no net magnetization, a magnetic origin is already unlikely, so the test adds little. The authors should either derive the time-reversal/Stokes–anti-Stokes relation for cross-circular backscattering from a T-even axial order in -3 under resonance (for example in the SM) and evaluate the model at δω comparable to the real phonon energy relative to the resonance width, or present the test as a consistency check only.

13. The comparison with molecular ROA and the term "natural ROA" are misleading. The statement (p. 7) that g_ROA ≈ 1 is "several orders of magnitude larger" than molecular natural ROA (g ~ 10⁻³) compares different physics: molecular natural ROA is a reciprocal, orientationally averaged electric-dipole/magnetic-dipole or electric-quadrupole interference effect in transparent media, whereas here the signal is a directional, resonance-enabled electric-dipole effect along one crystal axis. The paper itself shows the sign follows A·k (front versus back), so the signal averages to zero over orientations, for example in a powder, which is the opposite of what "optical activity" normally denotes. The authors should remove or qualify the 10⁻³ comparison, give instead the cross-circular asymmetries reported for 1T-TaS2 (Refs. [3–6]) and NiCo2TeO6 (Ref. [7]) as the relevant benchmark, and clarify that the observable is a directional cross-circular intensity difference governed by A·k; a term such as "ferroaxial" or "directional Raman circular intensity difference" would be clearer for an achiral crystal.
