A. Must address

1. The central claim that every non-BCS/non-Fermi-liquid feature is extrinsic is never tested quantitatively against an independent, measured disorder parameter. In the abstract, the Fig. 1 caption ("we attribute those discrepancies to the effects of disorder"), p. 2, and the sections "Reconciling results on the Fermi-liquid ground state" (p. 7), "Evidence that large phase fluctuations are extrinsic" (p. 8) and "Concerning the non-mean-field T-dependence of the gap" (p. 9), the paper assigns the boomerang in Tθ, the low-T T-linear resistivity, the residual C/T and residual Drude weight, gaps above Tc and the linear end of the dome to disorder. Each section shows that a disorder mechanism could produce the anomaly. None shows that the measured size of the anomaly follows from the measured amount of disorder. Of the evidence offered, only Ca-YBCO versus YBCO (Fig. 2) and the irradiation studies (refs 87, 88) vary disorder at all. As written, the hypothesis can absorb any discrepancy, so the abstract's statement that the inconsistencies "can in fact be attributed to the expected effects of material disorder" is a conjecture rather than a conclusion. The paper already cites the needed inputs: NQR hole-density spreads and linewidths (refs 71–74), Dingle rates from quantum oscillations (ħ/τ = 2.4 meV in Tl2201), residual resistivities, irradiation series (refs 87, 88) and STM gap-map widths. The authors should do at least one of the following: (a) for one material with a known disorder metric, estimate with the puddle or dirty-d-wave model they favour the expected suppression of Tθ, the residual C/T and the T-linear coefficient A1, and compare with experiment; (b) compile existing data at fixed p (or fixed Tc) across samples and families (LSCO, Tl2201, Bi2212, Bi2201, YBCO and Ca-YBCO, MBE LSCO films, irradiated LSCO/YBCO) in a table or "disorder budget" figure, plotting Tθ/Tc, A1 per CuO2 plane, residual γ0/γn and the uncondensed Drude fraction against ρ0 per plane, Dingle temperature or NQR linewidth, and show whether each anomaly shrinks monotonically as disorder shrinks. Two existing tensions must be addressed directly. First, Tl2201 and LSCO differ in disorder by the paper's own account (p. 12), yet their T-linear slopes per plane are comparable (refs 16, 17), and the slope has been reported to correlate with Tc or with the dome itself rather than with ρ0 (a correspondence the paper raises on p. 7 but does not resolve); the authors should state what their puddle mechanism (ref. 49) predicts for A1 as a function of ρ0 and whether existing data already constrain it. Second, the MBE LSCO films of ref. 14 are presented in the literature as highly homogeneous yet show Tθ ∝ Tc over hundreds of samples, while the "Swiss cheese" description (refs 68, 69) comes from bulk crystals; the authors should explain why it applies to those films.

2. Fig. 2, the main positive evidence that clean overdoped cuprates avoid the boomerang and have Tθ >> Tc, is open to a chain explanation, is internally inconsistent with its own clean-limit reference line, and covers only a marginal part of the overdoped range. The teal points mix λa^-2, λb^-2 and √(λaλb), and the caption says these "produce different results" without separating them. In YBa2Cu3O7 the CuO chains superconduct and contribute strongly to λb (ref. 59, "Evidence for chain superconductivity", is cited on this very point), so the roughly 50% rise of Tθ from p = 0.16 to 0.19 (p. 7) could come partly or mostly from chain filling as O7 is approached rather than from planar stiffness. Several teal points near p ≈ 0.18–0.19 (about 330–450 K) lie above the "1 + p" line (about 360 K), which is the stiffness of all planar carriers with m* = 5 me; taken at face value, the measured stiffness exceeds that of every carrier in the planes. The m* = 5 me value is taken from single-layer Tl2201, not measured for overdoped YBCO. Moreover, the data reach only p ≈ 0.19, where Tc has fallen only from 94 K to 88 K, so YBCO tests just the first few percent of the overdoped regime. The authors should show the chain-free planar (λa-only) stiffness as its own data set with its own p dependence, separately from λb and from geometric means; justify or replace m* for YBCO and explain why points exceed the 1 + p bound; state plainly the limited doping range tested; and, if data exist, add other low-disorder overdoped systems (YBCO-248, inner planes of multilayer cuprates, pp. 8 and 12) and existing overdoped Tl2201 and LSCO stiffness on the same Tθ axis. If Tθ from λa alone also rises across p = 0.16–0.19 the argument stands; if not, the contrast with Ca-YBCO and LSCO weakens considerably.

3. Tl2201 is the paper's benchmark for clean overdoped Fermi-liquid behaviour, yet in the same family it shows the features attributed to disorder, and the two are not reconciled. On p. 6 Tl2201 is "the cleanest cuprate that can access the overdoped regime" and supplies quantum oscillations for p > 0.27 with Lifshitz–Kosevich form, m* = 5 me and ħ/τ = 2.4 meV (ref. 36). On pp. 7–8 the same family shows T-linear resistivity (ref. 17) attributed to scattering off superconducting puddles, strong extrinsic phase fluctuations with Tc/Tθ behaving like the underdoped side (refs 63–65), a specific-heat jump at Tc only 10% of the residual C/T (ref. 65), and inhomogeneity on a scale of a few hundred Å (ref. 70), which is the only reconciliation offered. Samples showing quantum oscillations must have a mean free path of that order or longer, and the puddle scenario sets the disorder correlation length at L_dis ~ p^{-1/2} lattice constants; it is not shown how a landscape strong enough to leave most of the specific-heat weight uncondensed still permits coherent cyclotron orbits around the full Fermi surface. Also, ħ/τ = 2.4 meV is comparable to kBTc ≈ 2.2 meV for Tc = 26 K, so homogeneous pair breaking may already account for the Tl2201 observations without mesoscopic inhomogeneity. The authors should estimate the Dingle damping expected from the hole-density variance the puddle picture requires and compare it with the measured damping; estimate ξ0/ℓ for these samples and place it relative to the Abrikosov–Gorkov critical value; discuss whether the quantum-oscillation, specific-heat and transport samples are at comparable p and disorder; and, if a single sample shows both quantum oscillations and T-linear resistivity, make the puddle reconciliation (refs 49, 50) quantitative for that sample, including the magnitude of the T-linear term relative to the T² term. This is the one family where both sides of the argument are measured, so it is the natural quantitative test.

4. The gap-equation inversion in Fig. 3 depends on an unexamined cutoff choice that conflicts with the paper's effective-theory framework, and its procedure and uncertainties are not given. The effective theory is defined below an intermediate scale of order J (p. 4) and the analysis is restricted to energies below about 0.1 eV (p. 6), yet on p. 10 Eq. 7 is inverted "without introducing any additional high-energy cutoff (taking F(ε) = 1)", so the integral runs over the full tight-binding band of Eq. 10 (bandwidth of order 1 eV) with a fixed renormalized dispersion (t = 166 meV). The inferred Ṽ depends logarithmically on this cutoff, so the value Ṽ ≈ 0.6 eV at optimal doping, the identification J ≈ 0.15 eV via Ṽ = 4J, and the "smooth but nonetheless pronounced decrease" of Ṽ used as evidence for magnetic pairing (p. 11) all depend on it; with a 0.1 eV cutoff Ṽ would be substantially larger and the "reasonable agreement" with J would change. The paper should state whether μ is recomputed at each p from the Luttinger count, whether t, t′ and t⊥ are held fixed with doping, how the bilayer bands are averaged, whether Δ0 is the T → 0 antinodal gap or the gap at the measurement temperature, which points the red line is fitted to, and how Δ_extrap is computed (including why the purple Δ_extrap points track 2.14 kBTc rather than Δ0 below p ≈ 0.24). It should show Ṽ(p) for a range of cutoffs (for example 0.05, 0.1, 0.3 eV, the bandwidth, and none) and propagate the uncertainties in Δ0, the tight-binding parameters and the bilayer treatment into error bars on Ṽ and on the inferred J.

B. Should address

6. The inversion is applied where the paper itself says BCS does not hold. For p ≲ 0.2 the measured Δ0 (gold points) in Fig. 3 is about twice 2.14 kBTc (light-blue points), and p. 9 states that Δ0/kBTc reaches 4 or more at and below optimal doping. Using that Δ0 in a weak-coupling BCS inversion gives a Ṽ of unclear meaning, yet these points set the slope of the decrease used to argue for a magnetic origin (p. 11), and the J ≈ 0.15 eV estimate uses exactly the optimal-doping value. Since the inversion uses Δ0 rather than Tc, Ṽ inferred from the gap and Ṽ inferred from Tc must differ substantially there. The authors should restrict the fit to the range where Δ0 ≈ 2.14 kBTc or state how much the J estimate changes, and show both Ṽ inferred from Δ0 and from Tc (or plot the Tc predicted from the inferred Ṽ next to the measured Tc), so readers can see where the weak-coupling description is self-consistent.

7. The paper claims weak coupling while its own inferred coupling is of order one. It reports Ṽρ(EF) ≈ 1 at optimal doping and about 0.5 at the highest doping (p. 11), and defends the analysis by analogy with Migdal–Eliashberg theory for Pb (λ ≈ 1). In Eliashberg theory, however, retardation (ωD << EF) is what controls λ ≈ 1, whereas here F = 1 explicitly removes retardation and the gap equation has no frequency dependence. The authors should explain what small parameter justifies mean-field treatment at Ṽρ ≈ 1 without retardation, and estimate the strong-coupling corrections to Δ0/kBTc expected at this coupling, before Δ0/kBTc → 2 is used as evidence of BCS behaviour; these corrections also set how close to 2.14 the ratio should come in the cleanest samples (prediction 3, p. 12).

8. The Abrikosov–Gorkov statements on p. 5 and the scaling in Eq. 9 need derivation and appear to conflict with standard results. The text says that in AG theory Tc vanishes linearly as ξ0/ℓ approaches the critical value and that κ vanishes in proportion to Tc, and then that in 2D (L = ac) Tθ/Tc ~ kFℓ ~ kFξ0 ≫ 1 diverges as Tc → 0. If κ ∝ Tc with a fixed prefactor, the 2D ratio tends to a constant; a divergence requires the prefactor to change, for example through ξ0 growing at the pair-breaking boundary. In the standard AG treatment of a sign-changing gap, Tc vanishes as the square root of the distance to the critical scattering rate, and the T = 0 superfluid density in the gapless regime scales as Δ0², i.e. as Tc²; this dirty-d-wave ρs ∝ Tc² behaviour is what refs 28–30 use for overdoped LSCO, with near-linear relations arising only under specific scattering assumptions. On that scaling homogeneous pair breaking already drives Tθ/Tc downward as Tc → 0, which changes the case for needing mesoscopic granularity. In addition, ξ0 is only 2–3 lattice constants (p. 5), so kFξ0 is of order a few and order-one prefactors decide the conclusion. The authors should give the explicit AG result for κ(Tc) in the d-wave Born and unitary limits, derive Eq. 9 with prefactors and stated assumptions, and show where the observed Tθ/Tc values fall relative to the homogeneous prediction.

9. A gap shape close to d(k) does not by itself show that the interaction is nearest-neighbour or rule out pairing by a quantum-critical mode. On pp. 4 and 10–11 the argument runs: Δ(k) is fixed by the form of V because φ(r) is "much longer ranged" than V; the measured Δ(k) ≈ Δ0 d(k); therefore V is short-ranged, which "precludes" pairing by any familiar quantum-critical collective mode. But ξ0 is only about 2–3 lattice constants (p. 5), weakening the assumed separation of length scales; the gap shape is a Fermi-surface projection; and spin-fluctuation exchange peaked near (π, π) with correlation lengths of one to two lattice spacings, and weak-coupling calculations generally, also give gaps dominated by the lowest d-wave harmonic. The authors themselves note that bounds on higher harmonics are lacking. They should quote the bound the cited ARPES data (refs 83, 96, 102) actually impose on the cos 2kx − cos 2ky admixture, translate it into a bound on the interaction range and on the correlation length of any mediating mode, and state how large a harmonic a critical mode with a given correlation length would produce. Otherwise "precludes" and "no quantum critical mode is the prime driver" should be softened to statements of consistency.

10. Bi2212 is used selectively. It is the main source for the BCS-like gap ratio (p. 9) and for the inversion (Fig. 3), yet the same material is cited as showing large gap inhomogeneity, puddles up to 120 K, and an ARPES gap persisting 50% above Tc (p. 8), all attributed to disorder.
