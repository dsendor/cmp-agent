A. Must address

1. **At n = 1/2 and 1/4 near t = 1.8 meV, the specific-heat maximum may not mark melting of charge order.** T_c(t) is defined throughout as the position of the C_v/N maximum (Figs. 2, 3). The claim that quantum fluctuations increase the melting temperature "by as much as 30-40%" (abstract, Conclusions) depends on this definition. In Figs. 2(b), 2(c), 2(e) and 2(f), raising t does not move the sharp low-temperature peak present at t = 0 (about 27 K at n = 1/2, about 31 K at n = 1/4) to higher T. Instead that peak loses weight, and a separate broad hump at higher T (about 45-65 K) grows and becomes the global maximum. The green dots at t = 1.8 meV sit on this hump, and near the t = 1.8 meV dashed line (about 42-44 K) all curves cross close to one point. This looks like an ordering anomaly handing over to a kinetic crossover, not a melting temperature that rises; the "T_c" in Fig. 3 seems to jump from one feature to another. The paper itself says that in the Fermi-liquid phase the maximum "corresponds to a characteristic scale associated with the kinetic energy" (p. 3), which grows roughly linearly with t. It also says that at t = 1.8 meV n = 1/2 is "close to the GWC-FL boundary" and n = 1/4 "appears to be in the FL regime" (p. 4). Fig. 1 agrees. At n = 1/4 (N = 36) the order parameter sqrt(S(k)/N) falls steeply near 1.6 meV, from 0.25 to about 0.15 by t = 1.8 meV. At n = 1/2 the steep rise of the normalized shift in Fig. 3 (t ≈ 1.2-2 meV) coincides with the collapse of the many-body gap (second and fourth rows of Fig. 3) and with the order-parameter jump near t ≈ 2 meV in Fig. 1. Meanwhile, for t below about 1 meV, where the ground state is clearly a GWC, Fig. 3 shows almost no shift. Fig. 1 gives S(k)/N only at T = 0, and no finite-temperature order parameter is shown. The authors should: (i) compute the charge structure factor S(Q, T)/N at the ordering wavevectors with FTLM, and the charge susceptibility χ(Q, T) or a Binder-type ratio across clusters, versus T at each t, and show whether the loss of charge order coincides with the C_v maximum; (ii) track the low-T peak and the high-T hump separately in Fig. 3, for example by following the local maximum continuously from t = 0 or by a two-component decomposition; (iii) split C_v into kinetic and potential-energy fluctuation contributions to identify the Coulomb-ordering feature; (iv) mark the GWC-FL boundary from Fig. 1 and the spectra on Fig. 3, and report T_c(t) only where the ground state is a GWC on every cluster studied. If the increase survives only in the perturbative regime, report its size there; the NN fits 0.097 t² and 0.077 t² would imply about +30% and +25% at t = 1.8 meV. If it survives only beyond the GWC-FL boundary, rephrase the central claim.

2. **The cluster T_c at t = 0 is about twice the thermodynamic value at n = 1/2 and 1/4, so moving the relative shift δT_c/T_c(0) to the thermodynamic limit is not justified.** At t = 0, the FTLM C_v maxima sit near 27 K for n = 1/2 (Fig. 2(b), N = 28, LR) and 31 K for n = 1/4 (Fig. 2(c), N = 36, LR). The authors' own large-lattice Monte Carlo for the same LR model (ε = 4.67, a = 7.98 nm, d/a = 10) gives 13.40 K (Table I, Fig. 5(d), already converged by 12×12) and 14.31 K (Fig. 5(b)). Fig. 7(a) shows the same problem at n = 2/5: about 25 K on N = 25 against 15.6 K. At n = 1/3 the agreement is much better (about 41-42 K against 37 K). So the clusters overestimate the classical T_c by about a factor of two at exactly the fillings where the increase is claimed. The t = 0 transitions at these fillings are argued to be first order (Ref. 8, p. 6). On 20-36 sites (6-14 particles) the C_v peak is then a finite-size-rounded, Schottky-like feature set by short-range correlations and the few lowest classical excitations. Nothing guarantees that its relative t-dependence matches that of the true transition. Normalizing by T_c(0) (p. 3) removes the common scale but not this concern. The size spread of the shift is also large. At n = 1/2 and t = 1.8 meV, the normalized shift ranges from about 0.4 (N = 20) to about 0.6 (N = 28), up to about 50% apart, and the spread between N = 20, 24a and 28 is already comparable to the effect for t below about 1.5 meV. At n = 1/3 the N = 18 curve turns upward well before the N = 24b and 27 curves. The text only says α is "more sensitive to system geometry and size". The authors should: (i) list the t = 0 cluster T_c for every cluster next to the Monte Carlo value; (ii) run classical Monte Carlo on exactly the FTLM clusters, show that it reproduces the FTLM t = 0 curves, and show how T_c(0) evolves from these clusters to the large-lattice value; (iii) add size-trend plots, or at least a bound, for δT_c/T_c(0) at fixed t (for example t = 1 and 1.8 meV) for each filling, with the particle number of each cluster stated; (iv) most decisively, run large-lattice classical Monte Carlo with the temperature-dependent effective Hamiltonian V'_i of Eqs. 5-7 / Eq. 25 (Appendix E) at n = 1/2 and 1/4, and at the other fillings. This would give the thermodynamic-limit sign and t² coefficient of δT_c in the perturbative regime, free of cluster artifacts, using machinery the paper already has. Otherwise, explain why the relative shift should be insensitive to size when the absolute T_c(0) is off by a factor of two.

3. **The claim that quantum effects "capture the shift" towards experiment is not shown quantitatively, and the numbers suggest otherwise.** The abstract says "quantum effects capture the shift relative to the classical estimates, which in some cases are more than 50 percent off". The Conclusions say the quantum effects bring T_c "closer to experimental values for several fillings". Table I lists only classical T_c values, and no quantum-corrected T_c in kelvin is ever set against experiment; the conclusion rests on applying cluster ratios to thermodynamic numbers without saying so. The numbers do not support the claim:
   - n = 1/4: experiment needs +124% (14.31 K to 32 ± 2 K). A 30-40% increase gives about 19-20 K, leaving most of the gap, and at t = 1.8 meV this filling is in the FL regime by the paper's own account (concern 1).
   - n = 1/2: experiment needs +116% (13.40 K to 29 ± 2 K). 13.40 K × 1.3-1.6 gives about 17-21 K.
   - n = 2/5: the paper is internally inconsistent here. Appendix D and the text report a small reduction in the GWC regime, which would move T_c away from the 18 ± 2 K experiment (classical 15.62 K). But Fig. 7(c, d) suggests about +30-40% at t = 1.8 meV, which would give about 21 K and overshoot it.
   - n = 1/3 is the fitting density. ε = 4.67 was fixed by matching the classical T_c to 37 K, ignoring the experimental error bars (Appendix A). Hopping then lowers this T_c by about 10% (Fig. 3; −11% from Eq. 10). The fit therefore no longer reproduces experiment once t = 1.8 meV is switched on. Because T_c ∝ 1/ε, a consistent refit would lower ε by about 10% and raise every classical T_c in Table I by a similar amount, which is comparable to the effects discussed at n = 2/5 and 1/7. Appendix A acknowledges this but does not quantify it.

   The authors should add rows or a column to Table I giving the quantum-corrected T_c at t = 1.8 meV for each filling where this is possible: the classical thermodynamic-limit T_c × (1 + δT_c/T_c(0)), with ε refitted self-consistently at n = 1/3 with the quantum shift included, the experimental error bars propagated, and an uncertainty from the finite-size spread. They should then restate the abstract and Conclusions to match those numbers, including n = 2/5, or remove the claim.

B. Should address

4. **The spin-polarized restriction removes an entropy term that may decide the sign of the effect at n = 1/2 and 1/4.** All calculations are spin-polarized (p. 3), but the experiments are at zero field. The proposed mechanism for the increase is entropic (Eq. 11). At T ≈ 20-40 K the localized carriers of the insulating GWC carry spin entropy close to ln 2 per particle, because the exchange J ~ t²/V is far below T_c. In a metallic or near-metallic disordered state, spin entropy is quenched below the Fermi temperature. This difference enters S_d − S_o directly, at the same order as the claimed effects, since exchange also scales as t². It is the Pomeranchuk-type physics that can itself raise an ordering temperature. Spin also roughly halves the kinetic scale of the FL at fixed density, and finite-U virtual hopping differs from the polarized case. These effects matter most near the GWC-FL boundary, where n = 1/2 and 1/4 sit. Ref. 28's evidence that spin is unimportant (p. 3) concerns n = 1/3 and 2/3 only. The authors should run a spinful FTLM calculation (large but finite U) on at least one small cluster at n = 1/2 or 1/4, for example N = 18 or 20 at n = 1/2 or N = 24 at n = 1/4, to check whether the sign of δT_c(t) survives. Failing that, they should estimate the spin-entropy contribution to Eq. 11 quantitatively, or argue that it cancels between phases, and narrow the predictions to spin-polarized conditions (e.g. a large in-plane field), saying so in the abstract.

They should also explain why a mechanism described as "spatial in nature" should apply equally to the stripe and 2×2 states. 9. **The n = 1/3 perturbative domain-wall estimate rests on unjustified steps and has a numerical inconsistency.**
   - T_c is assumed proportional to the T = 0 domain-wall energy per length (Eq. 10). For the 2D three-state Potts transition, T_c is set by the vanishing of the wall free energy, which includes the entropy of wall meandering, kinks and roughening; this is not included. The argument also mixes a T = 0 wall energy with the temperature-dependent f(βV_1). The quantum correction does not rescale all classical excitations uniformly: wall particles gain extra t² energy from the zero-cost hop (the k = 0 term). So the proportionality is not guaranteed.
   - Eq. 26 assumes β|K_ij| and Σ_j X_ij are small. At T_c ≈ 37 K ≈ 3.2 meV and t = 1.8 meV, βt ≈ 0.56. Each zero-cost hop then contributes about (βt)²/2 ≈ 0.16, and disordered configurations near T_c have many such hops, so the expansion parameter is not small where it is used. The Δ_ij = 0 terms, which give the dominant −w contribution (Appendix F), connect degenerate classical configurations. Strictly they call for degenerate perturbation theory rather than a second-order Dyson term, and βt²/2 grows at low T.
   - The quoted −11% (p. 6, Appendix F) equals −w t²/V_1², the leading −w term only. The Fig. 3 dashed line (NN, n = 1/3), labelled −t²/(T_c(0)V_1), is also only this term. The full f(w) of Eq. 9/Eq. 34 at w = 2.8 gives f ≈ −2.8 − 5.32 + 4.93 ≈ −3.18, hence δT_c/T_c(0) = f/25 ≈ −12.7% for V_1 = 5t.
   - w is set with T_c = 37 K, the LR/experimental value, not the NN model's own classical T_c for V_1 = 9 meV.
   - Eq. 7's "under additional approximations" does not list them (NN hopping only, the small-ΣX expansion of Eq. 26, and so on).

   The authors should state which expression is compared with the numerics and plot the full Eq. 10 on Fig. 3. They should use the NN model's own T_c(0) in w, list the approximations behind Eq. 7, and state the range of βV_1, βt and t/V_1 over which Eq. 10 is controlled. They should also justify neglecting wall entropy or include its leading t-dependence.

12. **The novelty framing omits known cases where quantum fluctuations create order or raise a melting line.** The abstract's framing ("it is generally believed" that quantum and thermal fluctuations act together) leaves out textbook counterexamples. Hopping lifting a classical degeneracy and creating order is the known quantum order-by-disorder mechanism. A closely related case is the supersolid of hard-core bosons on the triangular lattice at half filling, which is cited only for bosons (Refs. 50-52), along with the "pinball" phase (Refs. 39-41) and Ref. 46. The Pomeranchuk effect is another case where fluctuations raise a melting line. The authors should discuss their n = 1/2 and 1/4 results against these mechanisms, and make clear what is specific to GWCs in moiré systems.
