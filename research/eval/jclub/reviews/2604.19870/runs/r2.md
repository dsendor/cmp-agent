Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper asks how quantum fluctuations (nearest-neighbour hopping t) shift the thermal melting temperature of generalized Wigner crystals (GWCs) in WS2/WSe2 moiré bilayers. It uses exact diagonalization and the finite-temperature Lanczos method (FTLM) on spin-polarized extended Hubbard models, with both screened long-range (LR) and nearest-neighbour (NN) interactions. The headline is that hopping lowers the specific-heat maximum T_c at n = 1/3 but raises it by "30-40%" at n = 1/2 and 1/4. This is presented as a counterexample to the idea that quantum and thermal fluctuations act together, and as a partial explanation of the gap between classical Monte Carlo and experimental T_c. The question is timely and the n = 1/3 perturbative domain-wall calculation is a useful piece of analytic understanding. But the central claim for n = 1/2 and 1/4 rests on locating specific-heat maxima on 6-14-particle clusters, and it is not yet shown that these maxima track melting of a crystal.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The question is clear and testable: does bandwidth raise or lower GWC melting temperatures? It connects to existing data (Table I) and to experiments that can tune the bandwidth with displacement field.
- The LR and NN models are treated side by side. Several cluster geometries are used, including the 27-site cluster with full point-group symmetry (Figs. 3, 6).
- The n = 1/3 result is backed by an analytic finite-temperature perturbation theory (Eqs. 5-10, Appendices E-F). It gives the sign and rough size of the T_c reduction through the domain-wall energy, and it agrees with the FTLM data at small t (Fig. 3).
- The classical Monte Carlo baseline (Fig. 5, Table I) is done carefully, with sizes up to 35×35 and replica exchange. It documents a filling-dependent discrepancy with experiment of up to about 55%.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. **At n = 1/2 and 1/4 near t = 1.8 meV, the specific-heat maximum may not mark melting.**
   - The claim that quantum fluctuations *increase* the melting temperature at n = 1/2 and 1/4 (abstract, Conclusions: "by as much as 30-40%") identifies T_c with the position of the C_v/N maximum (Figs. 2, 3).
   - Figs. 2(b), 2(c), 2(e) and 2(f) show a sharp low-T peak (around 27-33 K at t = 0) that loses weight as t grows, and a broad hump at higher T that grows. The green dots at t = 1.8 meV sit on the broad hump.
   - The authors themselves say that at large t the maximum "corresponds to a characteristic scale associated with the kinetic energy" of the Fermi liquid, and that it grows roughly linearly with t.
   - The steep rise of (T_c(t) − T_c(0))/T_c(0) at n = 1/2 (Fig. 3, t ≈ 1.2-2 meV) coincides with the collapse of the many-body gap in the spectra (second and fourth rows of Fig. 3) and with the order-parameter jump near t ≈ 2 meV in Fig. 1.
   - The text also concedes that at t = 1.8 meV n = 1/4 "appears to be in the FL regime" (p. 4). Fig. 1 agrees: at n = 1/4 the order parameter has already crossed over by t ≈ 1.6 meV.
   - So at the "experimentally relevant" t, the quantity reported as an increased melting temperature may be the maximum switching from the ordering anomaly to a kinetic/coherence crossover of a non-crystalline state.
   - What to do: (i) track the low-T peak and the broad hump separately, for example with a two-component decomposition or by following the local maximum continuously from t = 0; (ii) compute the charge structure factor S(Q, T) and its susceptibility χ(Q, T) versus T at each t, and define the melting scale from where charge order is lost rather than from C_v alone; (iii) restrict the "T_c increase" claim to the range of t where the ground state is still a GWC on every cluster studied. If the increase survives only in the perturbative regime, report its size there; the NN fits of 0.097 t² and 0.077 t² imply about +30% and +25% at t = 1.8, but the text notes a possible flat feature at small t.

2. **The t = 0 cluster T_c is roughly twice the thermodynamic-limit value, so relative shifts on these clusters are not controlled.**
   - Fig. 2 gives cluster values at t = 0: about 27 K for n = 1/2 (N = 28, LR) and about 31 K for n = 1/4 (N = 36, LR).
   - The classical Monte Carlo for the same LR model and ε gives 13.4 K and 14.3 K in the thermodynamic limit (Table I, Fig. 5). Fig. 7(a) has the same issue for n = 2/5: about 25 K on N = 25 against 15.6 K.
   - At n = 1/3 the mismatch is smaller (about 42 K against 37 K).
   - The paper normalizes by T_c(0) to put sizes "on the same scale", but a factor-of-two finite-size error in the classical anomaly means the cluster peak is mostly a short-range-correlation (Schottky-like) feature of 6-14 particles, not the transition. Nothing guarantees that its relative t-dependence matches that of the true transition.
   - At n = 1/2 the three sizes in Fig. 3 (N = 20, 24a, 28) differ by up to about 50% in the normalized shift at t = 1.8 meV. The text does not discuss this.
   - What to do: (i) state the t = 0 cluster T_c for every cluster next to the Monte Carlo value; (ii) run classical Monte Carlo on the *same* small periodic clusters, to show that the cluster C_v peak at t = 0 is reproduced and to quantify the finite-size offset; (iii) add size-trend plots of the normalized shift at fixed t (for example t = 1 and 1.8 meV), with the particle number of each cluster stated, so readers can judge convergence.

3. **The claim that quantum effects "capture the shift" towards experiment is not shown quantitatively, and the numbers point the other way.**
   - The abstract says "We show that quantum effects capture the shift relative to the classical estimates, which in some cases are more than 50 percent off". The Conclusions say quantum effects bring T_c "closer to experimental values for several fillings".
   - Table I needs increases of +124% at n = 1/4 (14.31 K to 32 K) and +116% at n = 1/2 (13.40 K to 29 K). The paper's own figure is "30-40%", which would give roughly 19-20 K.
   - At n = 1/3, the fitting density, hopping lowers T_c by about 10% (Fig. 3; −11% from Eq. 10). The ε fitted classically to 37 K therefore no longer reproduces experiment once t = 1.8 meV is switched on, and every other entry in Table I shifts when ε is refit.
   - At n = 2/5 the text reports a small reduction near t = 0, yet Fig. 7(c, d) suggests about +30-40% at t = 1.8 meV. That would move the 15.6 K classical value to about 21 K, past the 18 ± 2 K experiment.
   - What to do: add a row to Table I giving the quantum T_c in kelvin at t = 1.8 meV for each filling, using classical T_c × (1 + δT_c/T_c) with a finite-size uncertainty, and refit ε self-consistently at n = 1/3 with the quantum shift included. Then rephrase the abstract and Conclusions to match what the numbers show.

B. Should address

4. **The entropic mechanism for the T_c increase is asserted but not computed.**
   - Eq. (11) attributes the increase at n = 1/2 and 1/4 to a reduced entropy jump between ordered and disordered phases ("quantum fluctuating stripes" and "shear modes", p. 6). No term in Eq. (11) is evaluated.
   - The FTLM data already give E(T) and S(T) = ∫C_v/T dT for each t.
   - What to do: compute δE and δS on the ordered and disordered sides of the peak, or the full free-energy curves. Show which term dominates, and whether the ordered-phase entropy really grows faster than the disordered-phase entropy. Without this, the "energetic and entropic" explanation in the Conclusions is a conjecture.

5. **T_c(0) is ill-defined for the NN model at n = 1/2 and 1/4, and the order-by-disorder context is missing.**
   - The paper notes that the classical NN model at n = 1/2 and 1/4 has a highly degenerate ground manifold (p. 4) and no conventional insulating GWC. Yet Fig. 3 normalizes by a NN "T_c(0)" and fits it to α t².
   - Hopping lifting a classical degeneracy and *creating* order is the known order-by-disorder mechanism. A closely related case is the supersolid of hard-core bosons on the triangular lattice at half filling, which is cited only for bosons (Refs. 50-52), along with the "pinball" phase (Refs. 39-41).
   - What to do: state what the NN C_v peak at t = 0 represents physically, drop or caveat the NN normalized curves at these fillings, and discuss the results against quantum order-by-disorder and the Pomeranchuk effect. These are textbook cases where quantum fluctuations stabilize order or raise a melting line, so the abstract's "it is generally believed" framing should acknowledge them.

6. **Leaving out spin removes an entropy that enters the proposed mechanism directly.**
   - All calculations are spin-polarized (p. 3). In the real system at T ≈ 30-40 K, the localized carriers of the GWC carry spin entropy of up to ln 2 per particle, since the exchange J ~ t²/V is far below T_c. The Fermi-liquid side has much less spin entropy.
   - This term enters Eq. (11) at the same order as the effects claimed, and it is the very Pomeranchuk-type physics that can raise an ordering temperature.
   - Exchange also scales as t², the same order as the claimed shifts.
   - What to do: at minimum, estimate the spin-entropy contribution to Eq. (11), and run spinful calculations on the smallest clusters (for example N = 20 at n = 1/2, or N = 24 at n = 1/4) to show whether the sign of δT_c(t) survives. Otherwise, narrow the experimental predictions to spin-polarized conditions, such as a large in-plane field.

7. **The parameters are not consistent across the LR and NN models.**
   - Appendix A refits ε = 4.67, "instead of the previously reported value of 3.9", and calls the difference small. It is 20% in the overall energy scale.
   - The NN value V1 = 9 meV and the hopping t = 1.8 meV (Ref. 18) come from earlier work. It is not stated which ε, moiré period or material these assume.
   - What to do: state how V1 and t follow from the present ε, a = 7.98 nm and d/a = 10, and show how sensitive the sign and size of δT_c/T_c at t = 1.8 meV are to t over a plausible range (for example 1-2.5 meV). This matters because Fig. 3 shows the n = 1/2 shift changing steeply right around 1.8 meV.

8. **The n = 1/3 perturbative estimate has internal inconsistencies.**
   - The argument assumes T_c ∝ domain-wall energy per length at T = 0. It then evaluates the temperature-dependent f(βV1) at w = V1/T_c ≈ 2.8, mixing a T = 0 wall energy with a finite-T correction. Wall entropy (meandering), which sets the Potts transition temperature, is not included.
   - Fig. 3's caption gives the analytic curve as −t²/(T_c(0) V1), which equals −w t²/V1². That is only the leading −w term of f(w) in Eq. (9). The full Eq. (10) with f(2.8) ≈ −3.18 gives about −12.7% at t/V1 = 0.2, against the text's "about −11%".
   - What to do: state which expression is plotted, justify neglecting wall entropy (or include its leading t-dependence), and reconcile the numbers.

9. **T_c has no uncertainty, and the comparison with experiment uses a different definition.**
   - FTLM with R ≤ 200 random vectors gives statistical noise, and the maxima in Fig. 2 are broad. No error bars are given for the T_c values in Figs. 2-3 or Fig. 7. Bootstrap errors over random vectors would help, especially near t = 1.8 meV where the peaks are flattest.
   - The experimental T_c in Ref. 8 comes from optical sensing of the insulating state, not from a thermodynamic anomaly.
   - What to do: discuss whether a C_v maximum on a cluster and an optically detected loss of the insulating state should agree, particularly when the C_v maximum sits on a kinetic crossover (concern 1).

10. **The experimental prediction is not stated in usable form.**
   - The abstract and Conclusions say the predictions "should be observable in future experiments where the bandwidth can be tuned". No prediction is given in measurable units.
   - What to do: give T_c (K) against t, or against the displacement field via a stated t(D) relation, with uncertainty bands, for each filling. Say over what range of t the GWC ground state, and hence a melting transition, still exists.

C. Minor

11. The Conclusions say "30-40%", but Fig. 2(b) (about 27 K → 44 K) and Fig. 3 (N = 28 LR, about +0.6-0.7 at t = 1.8 meV) show n = 1/2 increases of about 60-70%. Make the quoted range match the figures, and state which size and model it refers to.
12. Duplicate references: [23] and [26] are identical, and so are [30] and [38].
13. Fig. 7(a, b): the x-axis is labelled T_c(K) but should be T (K).
14. Figs. 2, 5 and 7 give C_v/N "in arbitrary units". For a lattice model, C_v/N in units of k_B per site is natural and would let readers compare entropies, for example ∫C_v/T against n ln-type estimates.
15. Fig. 5 states that ε was fitted to n = 1/3 data "not shown here". Since the fit sets every number in Table I, include the n = 1/3 Monte Carlo panel.
16. Table I: define the sign convention of ΔT_c (T_exp − T_model), and quote the experimental values with their source panel in Ref. 8.
17. Eq. (7): "under additional approximations" should list the approximations: NN hopping only, the small-ΣX expansion of Eq. (26), and so on. Note also that hops with Δ_ij = 0 are degenerate, so the expansion parameter βt²/2 grows at low T. State the range of βV1 and t/V1 for which Eq. (10) is valid.
18. Fig. 1 caption: say whether the averaging over degenerate ground states for n = 1/2 includes the bent-stripe states, and that √(S(k)/N) = n holds only for the perfect classical crystal.
19. The n = 1/2, 1/4 and 2/5 analyses use clusters with 6-14 particles. State the particle number next to N in Fig. 3 and the Appendix C table.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Base the melting scale on the order parameter (S(Q, T), χ(Q, T), or a Binder-type ratio across clusters) as well as C_v. Report T_c(t) only while a GWC ground state exists. This would directly address whether the n = 1/2 and 1/4 "increase" is melting.
2. Run classical Monte Carlo on the same small clusters used for ED/FTLM, to calibrate the finite-size offset at t = 0. Then present quantum T_c in kelvin next to Table I, with ε refit self-consistently.
3. Evaluate the energy and entropy terms of Eq. (11) from the FTLM thermodynamics. Add a spinful calculation on one small cluster per filling to test whether spin entropy changes the sign of δT_c.
4. Reframe the novelty against known cases where quantum fluctuations stabilize order (order by disorder, the Pomeranchuk effect, triangular-lattice supersolids, Ref. 46), and make clear what is specific to GWCs in moiré systems.
