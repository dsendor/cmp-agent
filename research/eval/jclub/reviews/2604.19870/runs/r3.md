Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper asks how quantum fluctuations (a finite nearest-neighbour hopping t) change the thermal melting temperature of generalized Wigner crystals (GWCs) in WS2/WSe2 moiré bilayers. It uses exact diagonalization and the finite-temperature Lanczos method (FTLM) on spin-polarized 18–36 site triangular clusters, with both a screened long-range (LR) Coulomb model and a nearest-neighbour (NN) model. The headline claim is that the sign of the shift depends on filling. At n = 1/3, hopping lowers Tc roughly as t^2, and a finite-temperature perturbative domain-wall argument (Appendices E, F) explains this. At n = 1/2 and 1/4, hopping raises Tc. The authors attribute this rise to an entropic mechanism and say it narrows the gap between classical Monte Carlo and the measured Tc values. A filling-dependent sign of the quantum correction to melting would be an interesting and testable result for moiré Wigner-crystal physics. As presented, though, the evidence for the increase at n = 1/2 and 1/4 rests on specific-heat maxima of very small clusters, and it is not clear those maxima track melting of charge order.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The question is well posed and matters in practice: classical models are off by about 50% at some fillings (Table I), and the bandwidth can in principle be tuned with displacement field.
- Two microscopic models (LR and NN) are compared side by side, and several cluster geometries are used (Fig. 3, Appendix C), including the 27-site hexagonal cluster that has the full symmetry of the √3×√3 state.
- For n = 1/3 the authors give an analytic finite-temperature perturbative calculation (Eqs. 5–10, 27–35). It gives a closed-form t^2 coefficient, and its order of magnitude agrees with the FTLM data.
- The paper is open about several limitations: the spin-polarized sector, the degeneracy and gaplessness of the NN model at n = 1/2 and 1/4, and the uncertainty in t.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. **The Cv maximum may not track melting of charge order at n = 1/2 and 1/4.**
   - *What the figure shows.* In Fig. 2(b,c,e,f), raising t does not move the low-temperature peak to higher T. The t = 0 peak (about 27 K for n = 1/2, about 31 K for n = 1/4) loses weight. A separate broad hump grows at higher T (about 45–65 K) and becomes the global maximum. The "Tc" plotted in Fig. 3 therefore appears to jump from one feature to another rather than follow one transition.
   - *Why this matters.* In a metal or near-metal, a broad Cv maximum at a temperature of order the kinetic scale is expected. The authors say this themselves for the FL phase (p. 3: "it corresponds to a characteristic scale associated with the kinetic energy"). They also state that at t = 1.8 meV the n = 1/2 system is close to the GWC–FL boundary and n = 1/4 "appears to be in the FL regime" (p. 4). In that regime a rising Cv maximum says nothing about the thermal stability of a GWC. Yet the abstract and conclusions present it as quantum fluctuations increasing the melting temperature by 30–40%.
   - *What is missing.* No finite-temperature order parameter is shown. Fig. 1 gives S(k)/N only at T = 0.
   - *Requested.*
     - Compute S(Q,T)/N at the ordering wavevectors (and, if possible, the corresponding charge susceptibility) as a function of T for each t. Show that its drop coincides with the Cv maximum used as Tc.
     - Track the low-T peak and the high-T hump separately in Fig. 3, and report Tc only over the t range where the ground state is a GWC (from Fig. 1 and the spectra).
     - Decompose Cv into kinetic and potential-energy fluctuation contributions. This would show which feature is the Coulomb-ordering one.

2. **Finite-size effects are large and not controlled, and the relative shift is transferred to the thermodynamic limit without justification.**
   - *Mismatch at t = 0.* The cluster Tc values at t = 0 disagree strongly with the authors' own large-lattice Monte Carlo for the same LR model (ε = 4.67, a = 7.98 nm, d/a = 10).
     - n = 1/2: the t = 0 peak in Fig. 2(b) (N = 28) is near 27 K. Table I and Fig. 5(d) give 13.40 K, already converged by 12×12.
     - n = 1/4: the peak in Fig. 2(c) (N = 36) is near 31 K, against 14.31 K in Table I and Fig. 5(b).
     - So the clusters overestimate the classical Tc by about a factor of two at exactly the fillings where the increase is claimed.
   - *Size spread of the shift.* The normalized shift varies strongly with size in Fig. 3. For n = 1/2 at t = 1.8 meV, δTc/Tc(0) ranges from about 0.4 (N = 20) to about 0.6 (N = 28). For n = 1/3, the N = 18 curve turns upward well before the N = 24b and 27 curves. The text acknowledges that the n = 1/2 and 1/4 coefficients α are "more sensitive to system geometry and size".
   - *First-order transitions.* The t = 0 transitions at n = 1/2 and 1/4 are argued to be first order (ref. [8]). On 20–36 sites they appear as broad Schottky-like bumps. Normalizing by the cluster Tc(0) does not obviously remove finite-size effects that depend on t.
   - *Requested.*
     - Run classical Monte Carlo (t = 0) on exactly the FTLM clusters and show that it reproduces the FTLM t = 0 curves. Then show how Tc(0) evolves from these clusters to the large-lattice value.
     - Give a size analysis, or at least a bound, for δTc/Tc(0) at t = 1.8 meV for each filling.
     - Explain why the relative shift should be insensitive to size when the absolute Tc(0) is off by a factor of two.

3. **The claim that quantum effects capture the experimental shift is not demonstrated.**
   - *What is claimed.* The abstract says the quantum effects "capture the shift relative to the classical estimates". The conclusions say they bring Tc "closer to experimental values for several fillings, narrowing discrepancies that were as large as 50%".
   - *What the numbers give.* No quantum-corrected Tc in kelvin is ever tabulated against experiment.
     - n = 1/4: classical 14.31 K, measured 32 ± 2 K. A 30–40% increase gives about 19–20 K, which leaves most of the discrepancy.
     - n = 1/2: 13.40 K × 1.3–1.6 gives about 17–21 K, against 29 ± 2 K.
     - n = 2/5: Appendix D reports a *decrease* in the GWC regime, while the experimental value (18 K) is above the classical one (15.62 K). Quantum effects therefore move this filling away from experiment.
   - *Inconsistency with Fig. 4.* The authors themselves say t = 1.8 meV may put n = 1/4 in the FL phase. In that case the comparison is not with a GWC at all.
   - *Requested.*
     - Add rows to Table I giving the quantum-corrected Tc, i.e. the classical thermodynamic-limit Tc times (1 + δTc/Tc(0)) with a size-spread uncertainty, for each filling at t = 1.8 meV.
     - Restate the abstract and conclusions to match those numbers, including n = 2/5.

B. Should address

4. **The entropic mechanism for the increase at n = 1/2 and 1/4 is asserted, not computed.**
   - *Where.* Eq. (11) and the discussion after it.
   - *What is shown.* The argument only shows that a positive δTc is *possible* if δS_d − δS_o is sufficiently negative. Quantum-fluctuating stripes and shear modes are named as candidates, but none of the quantities in Eq. (11) is evaluated. The good αt^2 fits do not test the mechanism.
   - *Requested.*
     - The FTLM already gives E(T) and Cv(T). Obtain S(T) by integrating Cv/T for t = 0 and small t on the same cluster.
     - Evaluate δE and δS on the two sides of the transition and check the sign and size of the numerator of Eq. (11) against the measured δTc.
     - Alternatively, apply the perturbative effective classical Hamiltonian of Appendix E (Eq. 25) to n = 1/2 and 1/4 in classical Monte Carlo. That would test the t^2 coefficient in the thermodynamic limit and remove the finite-size issue of concern 2.

5. **The perturbative n = 1/3 calculation has unjustified steps and a numerical inconsistency.**
   - *Small parameter.* The expansion needs β|K_ij| and ΣX_ij to be small (Eq. 26). At Tc ≈ 37 K ≈ 3.2 meV and t = 1.8 meV, βt ≈ 0.56. Also, the Δ_ij = 0 terms (which give the dominant −w contribution, Appendix F) connect degenerate classical configurations. Strictly these call for degenerate perturbation theory rather than a second-order Dyson term.
   - *Domain-wall step.* The domain-wall energy is evaluated from T = 0 snapshots, and Tc ∝ μ is assumed. That proportionality ignores wall entropy and wall roughening near a Potts transition.
   - *−11% versus Eq. (10).*
     - With V1 = 5t and w = 2.8, Eq. (34) gives f(2.8) ≈ −2.8 − 5.32 + 4.93 ≈ −3.18, so δTc/Tc(0) = f/25 ≈ −12.7%.
     - The quoted −11% equals −w t^2/V1^2, i.e. only the leading term. The dashed line in Fig. 3 (NN, n = 1/3) is also labelled −t^2/(Tc(0)V1), the leading term only, not Eq. (10).
     - Which expression is being compared with the numerics?
   - *w uses the experimental Tc.* w is set with Tc = 37 K, the LR/experimental value, rather than the NN-model classical Tc for V1 = 9 meV.
   - *Requested.*
     - Plot the full Eq. (10) on Fig. 3.
     - State the range of βt over which the expansion is controlled.
     - Use the NN model's own Tc(0) in w.

6. **Restricting to the spin-polarized sector may bias the entropy balance that the mechanism depends on.**
   - *Where.* The calculations are spin-polarized throughout (p. 3).
   - *Why it matters.* The proposed mechanism at n = 1/2 and 1/4 is entropic (Eq. 11). At T ≈ 30–40 K, the spin entropy of localized charges (up to ln 2 per particle in the GWC) differs from that of the FL/disordered phase. That difference enters S_d − S_o directly. With spin, finite-U virtual hopping also differs from the polarized case.
   - *Requested.*
     - Give at least one spinful FTLM calculation on the smallest cluster (e.g. N = 18 or 20 at large but finite U), or a quantitative argument that spin entropy cancels between the two phases at these temperatures.
     - Otherwise, qualify the predictions as applying to a spin-polarized (e.g. field-polarized) system.

7. **The t^2 scaling at n = 1/2 and 1/4 is not established for the experimentally relevant LR model.**
   - *What the data show.* In Fig. 3 (top row), the LR curves for n = 1/2 and 1/4 are flat up to t ≈ 1 meV and then rise steeply. The text itself notes one "can not rule out a flat feature". The 0.097t^2 and 0.077t^2 fits are shown only for the NN model, whose ground state the authors say is not a GWC at these fillings (concern 8).
   - *Requested.*
     - Show log–log plots of δTc/Tc(0) against t for the LR data, with the fit window and the fitted exponent with its uncertainty.
     - Alternatively, drop the claim of t^2 scaling "with either sign" (Conclusions) for n = 1/2 and 1/4.

8. **The NN model at n = 1/2 and 1/4 is used as supporting evidence even though it does not host a GWC.**
   - *The conflict.* The authors state that at these fillings the NN model has a macroscopically degenerate, gapless ground state (pinball-metal physics). Its Cv maximum therefore does not describe GWC melting.
   - *How it is used.* Fig. 2(e,f) and Fig. 3 (third row), together with the t^2 fits, are nonetheless used to say "both LR and NN models show the same qualitative trends". In Fig. 2(e,f), the t = 0 curves already show a peak plus a shoulder, and the t = 1.8 meV marker sits on a shoulder rather than an isolated maximum. This makes the Tc assignment in the NN model ambiguous.
   - *Requested.* Either remove the NN results at these fillings from the evidence for increased melting temperatures, or add the NNN interaction that the authors say is needed to stabilize the GWC and repeat the calculation.

9. **ε is not fitted self-consistently.**
   - *What was done.* ε = 4.67 is fixed by matching the *classical* n = 1/3 Tc to experiment, ignoring the error bars (Appendix A). The paper then finds that hopping lowers the n = 1/3 Tc by about 10%.
   - *Why it matters.* Since Tc ∝ 1/ε, a consistent fit would lower ε by about 10% and raise every classical Tc in Table I by a similar amount. That is comparable to the effects being discussed at n = 2/5 and n = 1/7.
   - *Definitions of Tc.* The experimental Tc values come from optical sensing of the insulating states (ref. [8]) and may correspond to an onset rather than a thermodynamic transition. Comparing them with a Cv maximum needs comment.
   - *Requested.* Refit ε with the quantum-corrected n = 1/3 Tc and propagate the experimental error bars into Table I.

10. **The prediction for bandwidth-tuning experiments needs to be made concrete.**
    - *Where.* The abstract and conclusions predict that Tc can rise when the bandwidth is tuned.
    - *What the experimental knob does.* The proposed knob is a displacement field. Ref. [29] shows that it also changes the effective dielectric screening near the Wigner–Mott transition. It may also introduce further-neighbour hopping and modify the moiré potential.
    - *Source of t.* The value t = 1.8 meV is taken from ref. [18]. That paper concerns a different (kagome chiral spin liquid) context; its applicability to the WS2/WSe2 heterobilayer at a = 7.98 nm should be justified.
    - *Requested.* Give predicted Tc(t) curves in kelvin for the LR model with an estimate of t against displacement field, and discuss how changes in ε and d would compete with the t-induced shift.

C. Minor

11. **FTLM statistical errors are not reported.** No error bars appear on the FTLM Cv curves or on Tc(t), and the convergence check in R (random vectors, up to 200) and M (Krylov dimension 200) is only asserted (Appendix B). Please show the spread over independent random-vector sets for at least one cluster. Please also state the Tc resolution set by the temperature grid.

12. **The quoted magnitude of the increase is inconsistent across the paper.** The conclusions quote an increase of "as much as 30–40%". Fig. 3 shows about 0.6 for n = 1/2 (N = 28) at t = 1.8 meV, and the Fig. 2(b) dashed lines (about 27 K to about 44 K) imply about 60%. Please quote the range by filling and system size.

13. **Missing calibration curve.** Fig. 5 omits the n = 1/3 classical Monte Carlo curve ("not shown here"), yet it is the curve used to fix ε. Please include it. Table I lists n = 1/7, for which no quantum calculation is done; say so explicitly.

14. **Typographical and labelling errors.**
    - References [23] and [26] are identical, as are [30] and [38].
    - The Fig. 7(a,b) x-axes are labelled "Tc(K)" where T (K) is meant.
    - Cv/N is given in "arbitrary units" in Figs. 2, 5 and 7. Since it is per site, units of k_B would let readers judge the entropy released.
    - Fig. 1 and the text refer to "momentum sector" level crossings but do not state which k is used for each filling.

15. **Quenched disorder is set aside without an estimate.** Disorder is mentioned only in passing (ref. [45]). Twist-angle and strain inhomogeneity in real samples can shift and broaden Tc at the 10–15 K scale under discussion. A brief estimate of whether disorder could account for the residual discrepancy would help put the claims in context.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Make the finite-temperature charge structure factor S(Q,T) the primary diagnostic of melting, using Cv only as a secondary one. Report Tc(t) only where S(Q,T) shows a clear ordering crossover and the ground state is a GWC.
2. Use the effective classical Hamiltonian of Eq. (25) in large-lattice classical Monte Carlo for all fillings. This would give thermodynamic-limit t^2 coefficients that complement the small-cluster FTLM and resolve the size dependence in Fig. 3.
3. Compute the entropies of the ordered and disordered phases from integrated Cv/T to test Eq. (11) quantitatively at n = 1/2 and 1/4. This would turn the proposed mechanism into a demonstrated one.
4. Add a revised Table I with quantum-corrected Tc values and uncertainties for each filling at the experimentally estimated t, and tone the abstract to what those numbers support, including the n = 2/5 case.
