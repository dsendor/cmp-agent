Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper asks whether quantum fluctuations (a finite nearest-neighbour hopping t) always lower the thermal melting temperature of generalized Wigner crystals (GWCs) in WS2/WSe2 moiré bilayers. Using exact diagonalization and the finite-temperature Lanczos method (FTLM) on 18 to 36 site spin-polarized clusters, it reports that t lowers T_c at n = 1/3 but raises it at n = 1/2 and 1/4. It also gives a finite-temperature second-order perturbation theory that yields a domain-wall estimate of the n = 1/3 suppression. The question is timely, because classical Monte Carlo misses the measured T_c at several fillings by up to about 50%, and the bandwidth is tunable in experiment. A sign change in the quantum correction to T_c that depends on filling would be an interesting and testable result. As presented, however, the evidence that the enhancement belongs to melting of the GWC is not yet convincing.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- Clear physical question with a direct experimental handle: bandwidth tuned by displacement field.
- Two complementary interaction models (screened long-range and nearest-neighbour) and several cluster geometries, including the 27-site cluster that has the full symmetry (Fig. 3, Fig. 6).
- A transparent, checkable analytic treatment for n = 1/3 (Eqs. 5-10, Appendices E-F). The NN-model curve -t^2/(T_c(0)V_1) follows the N = 24b and 27 data up to t of about 1.5 meV (Fig. 3, third row).
- The classical Monte Carlo baseline is updated and documented: refit of epsilon, Table I, and Fig. 5 with sizes up to 35 x 35.
- The order-parameter data (Fig. 1) and the many-body spectra (Fig. 3) give useful context on where the GWC-to-Fermi-liquid boundary lies at each filling.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The specific-heat maximum is not shown to be the melting of charge order at n = 1/2 and 1/4, and at the experimentally relevant t it probably is not. T_c(t) is defined everywhere as the position of the C_v/N maximum (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). The paper itself says that in the Fermi-liquid phase this maximum "corresponds to a characteristic scale associated with the kinetic energy" (p. 3), which rises with t trivially. At n = 1/4 the paper states that t = 1.8 meV "appears to be in the FL regime" (p. 4). Fig. 1 agrees: for N = 36 the order parameter sqrt(S(k)/N) has dropped from 0.25 to about 0.15 by t = 1.8 meV, after a steep fall near 1.6 meV. At n = 1/2 the system is described as "close to the GWC-FL boundary". In Figs. 2(b), 2(c), 2(e) and 2(f), the sharp low-temperature peak present at t = 0 (about 27-33 K) loses weight as t grows. The maximum then moves to a broad high-temperature hump. Near the t = 1.8 meV dashed line (about 42-44 K), all curves cross close to one point. This is what one expects when a charge-order peak hands over to a kinetic crossover; it is not what one expects from a melting temperature that rises. Meanwhile Fig. 3 shows almost no shift for t below about 1 meV, where the ground state is clearly a GWC. So the headline statement that quantum fluctuations "increase the melting temperature by as much as 30-40%" (Conclusions) rests on the regime where, by the paper's own diagnosis, there is little or no GWC left to melt.

   What to do:
   - Determine T_c from an order-parameter-based quantity: the charge structure factor S(k*, T) at the ordering wavevector, its temperature derivative, or the charge susceptibility at k*, computed with FTLM.
   - Show that this quantity tracks the C_v maximum at each t.
   - Restrict the claim of enhancement to the t range where the ground state is a GWC (Fig. 1). Mark that boundary on Fig. 3.
   - If the enhancement survives only beyond the GWC-FL boundary, rephrase the central claim accordingly.

2. The cluster T_c differs from the thermodynamic T_c by about a factor of two at n = 1/2 and 1/4, so the transferability of δT_c/T_c(0) has not been shown. At t = 0 (classical), the FTLM C_v maxima in Fig. 2 sit near 27 K (n = 1/2, N = 28, LR) and 31 K (n = 1/4, N = 36, LR). The large-lattice Monte Carlo values in Table I and Fig. 5 are 13.4 K and 14.3 K. For n = 1/3 the agreement is much better (about 41 K against 37 K). For transitions argued to be first order (Ref. 8, p. 6), a 20-36 site C_v peak is dominated by finite-size rounding and by the few lowest classical excitations. The sign and size of a quantum shift measured relative to this inflated T_c(0) need not carry over to the thermodynamic limit. The spread between N = 20, 24a and 28 in Fig. 3 (n = 1/2) is already comparable to the effect for t below about 1.5 meV. Normalizing by T_c(0) (p. 3) removes the common scale but not this concern.

   What to do:
   - Show the classical (t = 0) C_v from Monte Carlo on the same 20-36 site geometries, so the finite-size T_c(0) of each cluster is tied to the thermodynamic value.
   - More decisively, run classical Monte Carlo on large lattices with the temperature-dependent effective Hamiltonian V'_i of Eqs. 5-7 (Appendix E) at n = 1/2 and 1/4. Report the thermodynamic-limit δT_c/T_c(0) in the perturbative regime. This is a direct test of the sign of the effect, free of cluster artifacts, and it uses machinery the paper already has.

3. The abstract claims that "quantum effects capture the shift relative to the classical estimates", but no quantitative comparison with experiment is given. Table I lists only classical T_c values. No quantum-corrected T_c in Kelvin is tabulated next to the experimental values at n = 1/7, 1/4, 2/5 or 1/2. The conclusion that the inclusion of quantum effects "brings the theoretical T_c closer to experimental values" therefore rests on applying cluster ratios to thermodynamic numbers without stating so. Three further issues compound this:
   - epsilon = 4.67 was fixed by matching the classical n = 1/3 T_c to 37 K. Since t lowers T_c at n = 1/3, refitting epsilon with t = 1.8 meV would shift every other prediction (Appendix A acknowledges this but does not quantify it).
   - n = 1/7 and 2/5 are treated quantum mechanically only at a single size (2/5) or not at all (1/7).
   - At n = 1/4 the relevant t lies in the FL regime (concern 1).

   What to do: add a column to Table I with quantum-corrected T_c, with epsilon refitted self-consistently at the chosen t, for each filling where this is possible. Give an uncertainty that reflects the finite-size spread. Otherwise, remove the claim from the abstract and conclusions.

B. Should address

4. The spin-polarized restriction may control the sign of the effect at n = 1/2 and 1/4. The experiments are at zero field, so electron spins are present. The proposed mechanism for enhancement is entropic (Eq. 11): quantum fluctuations reduce the entropy jump S_d - S_o. In the insulating GWC the spins carry close to ln 2 per electron at 20-40 K, because exchange is small. In a metallic or near-metallic disordered state, spin entropy is quenched below the Fermi temperature. Spin also roughly halves the kinetic scale of the FL at fixed density. Both effects enter δS_d - δS_o directly, and both matter most near the GWC-FL boundary where n = 1/2 and 1/4 sit. Ref. 28's evidence that spin is unimportant (p. 3) concerns n = 1/3 and 2/3 only.

   What to do: at minimum, repeat the n = 1/2 and 1/4 calculation for a small spinful cluster (large U) to check the sign of δT_c. If that is not feasible, add an explicit estimate of how spin entropy changes Eq. 11 and state the limitation in the abstract.

5. The mechanism for the enhancement at n = 1/2 and 1/4 is only qualitative. Eq. 11 is never evaluated. The candidate mechanisms (fluctuating stripes, shear modes of the 2 x 2 crystal, p. 6) are not tied to any computed quantity. The finite-temperature perturbation theory of Appendix E is general, and the paper applies it only to n = 1/3.

   What to do:
   - Compute E_o, E_d, S_o and S_d (or their t-derivatives) from the FTLM data, at least on one cluster. This would show whether the numerator of Eq. 11 is indeed positive and which term dominates.
   - Alternatively, use the effective classical model proposed in concern 2.
   - Clarify why a mechanism that is "spatial in nature" should apply equally to the stripe and 2 x 2 states.

6. The t^2 scaling claim at n = 1/2 and 1/4 is not supported by Fig. 3. The text says T_c "increases with t with a t^2 dependence at low t" and that "very good fits" to alpha t^2 were obtained (pp. 3, 7). It also concedes "one cannot rule out a flat feature". In Fig. 3 the data are flat (within the cluster-to-cluster spread) up to about 1 meV and then rise more steeply, which looks sigmoidal rather than quadratic. The dashed 0.097 t^2 and 0.077 t^2 curves are fitted over a range that includes the region beyond the GWC-FL boundary. The fitted alpha differs between N = 24a and 36 by about 30% at n = 1/4.

   What to do: give the fit window and the residuals. Fit only the GWC regime, and compare quadratic with higher-order or activated forms. Report alpha with an uncertainty for each cluster.

7. The assumptions behind the n = 1/3 domain-wall estimate need justification.
   - (i) T_c is taken proportional to the T = 0 domain-wall energy per length (Eq. 10). For the 2D three-state Potts transition, T_c is set by the vanishing of the wall free energy, which includes the entropy of wall meandering and kinks. The quantum correction is not a uniform rescaling of all classical excitations: wall particles gain extra t^2 energy from the zero-cost hop (the k = 0 term). So the proportionality is not guaranteed.
   - (ii) Eq. 26 assumes sum_j X_ij is small. At t = 1.8 meV and T near 37 K, beta*t is about 0.56. Each zero-cost hop then contributes about (beta t)^2/2 ≈ 0.16, and disordered configurations near T_c have many such hops, so the expansion parameter is not small precisely where it is used.
   - (iii) The -11% quoted on p. 6 and in Appendix F appears to come from the leading -w term only, which is also what the Fig. 3 caption plots (-t^2/(T_c(0)V_1)). The full f(w) of Eq. 9 at w = 2.8 gives f ≈ -3.18 and hence δT_c/T_c ≈ -12.7% for V_1 = 5t.

   What to do: state which expression is compared in Fig. 3. Show the full Eq. 10 curve alongside it, and comment on the validity of the expansion at T_c.

8. The robustness to model parameters is not shown. The results use a single t = 1.8 meV, taken from Ref. 18, which concerns a kagome spin liquid in twisted TMD bilayers. Hopping is nearest-neighbour only, with no further-neighbour or complex (valley-dependent) hopping. The screening is fixed at d/a = 10, and V_1 = 9 meV in the NN model is taken from the n = 1/3 mapping. The paper itself raises the possibility (p. 4) that the effective V_1 depends on density and is larger at n = 1/2 and 1/4. That would move these fillings back into the GWC regime and could change the sign of the result.

   What to do: show how the sign of δT_c at n = 1/2 and 1/4 depends on d/a and on a density-dependent V_1 (or a next-nearest-neighbour V_2 for the NN model, which p. 6 says is needed to realize a GWC at all). Justify t = 1.8 meV for the WS2/WSe2 heterobilayer specifically.

9. FTLM statistical errors and degeneracy handling are not shown. Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 have no error bars. Appendix B states only that R = 200 random vectors and M = 200 Lanczos steps were "verified" to converge. The C_v peak positions at low T, where FTLM is noisiest and where the near-degenerate NN manifolds at n = 1/2 and 1/4 dominate, set the whole δT_c analysis.

   What to do: show error bars from the spread across random vectors on representative curves. For the largest clusters, show a comparison with full ED where the Hilbert space allows.

10. The experimental "T_c" may not be the same quantity as the C_v maximum. The experimental temperatures in Table I (Ref. 8) come from optical sensing of the insulating states and are likely onset or crossover temperatures, possibly affected by disorder (Ref. 45). Comparing them quantitatively with a thermodynamic C_v peak should be discussed, especially when the discrepancy being explained is 13% at some fillings.

C. Minor

11. "As much as 30-40%" (Conclusions) does not match Fig. 2 and Fig. 3. For n = 1/2 (LR, N = 28), the dashed lines in Fig. 2(b) move from about 27 K to about 44 K, roughly +60%. Fig. 3 gives about 0.4-0.6 at t = 1.8 meV for n = 1/2 and about 0.3-0.4 for n = 1/4. Please quote cluster-resolved values.

12. Fig. 5 needs three corrections:
   - The caption says epsilon was obtained by fitting n = 1/3, "not shown here", but n = 1/3 is the calibration point and should be shown.
   - Panel (b) (n = 1/4, 16 x 16) and panel (c) (n = 2/5, 20 x 20) both list T_c = 15.98 K, which looks like a copy error.
   - The caption sentence "One sweep is defined a sequence..." is garbled.

13. Appendix A reports epsilon of about 4.67 against the earlier 3.9 but does not say why the refit changed. Please state whether the cause is the specific-heat recalculation, the system sizes, or d/a.

14. Fig. 7(a,b): the x-axis is labelled "T_c (K)" but should be T (K). Only one cluster (N = 25) is shown for n = 2/5, so the "observable reduction" there cannot be separated from finite-size effects. Please say so.

15. Duplicate references: Refs. 23 and 26 are identical, and Refs. 30 and 38 are identical.

16. Fig. 1: please state which momentum k is used for each panel. For n = 1/2 with degenerate stripe orientations, also state how S(k) is averaged over degenerate ground states. The ordered values (1/3, 1/2, 1/4) imply a specific normalization that should be given.

17. Fig. 4 and Appendix F: count explicitly why a wall particle has five empty neighbours (four costing +V_1 and one costing 0). Confirm that hops of non-wall particles adjacent to the wall, which are lumped into "rest", indeed cancel between E'_D and E'_A.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Replace or supplement the C_v-maximum definition of T_c with an order-parameter-based one (S(k*, T), or the charge susceptibility at k*). Plot the GWC-FL boundary from Fig. 1 on Fig. 3, so readers can see where an "enhanced melting temperature" refers to an ordered state.
2. Use the effective classical Hamiltonian V'_i (Eqs. 5-7) in large-scale classical Monte Carlo at n = 1/2 and 1/4. This would give a thermodynamic-limit sign and size of δT_c in the perturbative regime and cross-check the FTLM clusters.
3. Add a table of quantum-corrected T_c in Kelvin next to the experimental values, with epsilon refitted at the chosen t and uncertainties from the cluster spread.
4. Test the sign of the effect with spin included, on at least one small cluster at n = 1/2 or 1/4, and check how robust it is to d/a and to a density-dependent V_1.
