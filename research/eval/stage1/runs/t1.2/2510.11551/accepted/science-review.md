A. Must address

1. The growth of the slow-component weight w2 may be an artifact of spin-echo T2 weighting, not a growing "RS-like volume fraction". The headline RS-like evidence is that w2 = −c ln(T/T0) rises from ~0 at T0 ≈ 0.4 K to ~65% at 0.03 K (Fig. 3d), described as "growing towards 100%". The paper also states that 1/T2G of the fast (spinon) component diverges on cooling and "becomes too short to be reliably measured" below 0.4 K (Fig. 3b inset, p. 2), which is exactly where w2 starts to grow. In a spin-echo measurement, a component's echo is reduced by roughly exp(−2τ/T2), or exp[−(2τ/T2G)²] for a Gaussian decay. If nuclei with fast T1 also have short T2, their contribution to M(t) is suppressed more and more on cooling. The apparent weight of the slow component, whose T2 is presumably longer, would then grow even if the true population were fixed. The text says only that w2 is "calculated from the P(1/T1) distribution"; no T2 correction is mentioned. The spectral w2 (Supplementary Fig. 5c) is affected in the same way, so it is not an independent check. The authors should give the echo delay τ used for M(t) and for the low-T spectra (Methods). They should measure or bound T2 separately for the fast and slow components, for example by T1–T2 correlation spectroscopy (ref. 45, which they cite), and measure M(t) at several echo delays with the component amplitudes extrapolated to τ → 0. They should also show that total echo intensity × T follows the Curie expectation through 0.4 K, so that no fast-relaxing intensity goes missing. Without this, the −ln T law, the T0 and c parameters and the "effective volume fraction" picture are not established.

2. The low-field "RS-like" signatures are not separated from the staggered-field (DM / g-tensor) soliton physics that the authors themselves invoke at higher field. Extrapolating the measured gap (Fig. 4e: Δ ≈ 0.3 meV ≈ 3.5 K at 10 T, Δ ∝ H^0.8 or H^2/3) gives Δ ≈ 0.1–0.25 K at 0.2 T (and ~0.25 K at 0.5 T), comparable to the Zeeman scale gμBH ≈ 0.27 K. This is the same scale as the 0.4 K crossover, the growth of w2, and the downturn of 1/T1f below 0.2–0.3 K that the text itself attributes to a "field-induced origin ... soliton behaviours" (p. 2). At 2 and 5 T, DM-induced canting produces a low-temperature Kn upturn (Fig. 4b,c) qualitatively like the 0.2 T upturn in Fig. 3e that is fitted to the RS form. The Discussion's statement that there is "no Zeeman gap" at 0.2 T is asserted, not demonstrated. The authors should run the same LTRG calculation (staggered field plus DM canting) at 0.2 T, 0.5 T and 1 T and overlay it on Fig. 3e and on 1/T1f in Fig. 3b, showing quantitatively that the clean chain's field-induced staggered magnetization cannot produce the observed Kn upturn or the onset of the slow channel. They should present w2(T), T0 and the 1/T1s slope at 0.5 and 1.0 T quantitatively in the main text (currently only the ILTA maps of Supplementary Fig. 10), and show that the slow component behaves differently with field from what a soliton gap ∝ H^2/3 would produce. Ideally they should add data at lower field (e.g. 0.1 T) or with the field along a direction that minimises the staggered-field coefficient, which in copper benzoate depends strongly on orientation; an RS-like signal that survives where the staggered field is suppressed would be a strong null control.

3. The slow relaxation channel may come from a distinct proton species or an ordinary relaxation mechanism rather than from disorder-driven RS physics. The two-component recovery is measured on P5, which is "attributed either to the H(3) site in water molecules or to protons in the benzoate groups" (p. 2), so the line may contain inequivalent protons with very different hyperfine couplings and form factors. A proton whose transferred hyperfine field cancels at q = π (for example, one placed symmetrically between two Cu ions) is relaxed only by q ≈ 0 fluctuations, which in the HAFC give 1/T1 ∝ T: precisely the linear-in-T, two-orders-slower behaviour attributed to the RS component. The authors themselves note a q = 0 term ∝ T above 4 K. Benzoate protons with weak, mostly dipolar coupling could act the same way, and a temperature-dependent shift (Kn changes strongly below 0.8 K) can move spectral weight into or out of the excitation bandwidth, changing the apparent weight with temperature. Other alternatives are relaxation near dilute paramagnetic defects (chain ends, broken bonds, free Cu spins), spread by nuclear spin diffusion or limited by a spin-diffusion bottleneck, whose affected fraction can grow as the defect moments polarize; and freezing of water or benzoate motion below ~0.4 K, which the authors themselves raise as a source of disorder and which would give a temperature-dependent weight unrelated to the Cu spins. Supplementary Note 5 reportedly excludes spectral overlap from the frequency dependence of M(t), but that test is not visible in the main text and does not address the form-factor scenario. The authors should compute the q = 0 and q = π form factors for every proton plausibly contributing to P5 from the hyperfine tensors of Supplementary Note 2; show recoveries from different parts of the P5 line; repeat the two-component and ILTA analysis on the well-resolved H(1) line (P1/P2); and report the ratio 1/T1s / 1/T1f across sites. RS physics in the Cu chain should affect all sites scaled by their hyperfine couplings, whereas a second proton species would not. They should also show that the slow component is absent above 0.4 K for a physical reason rather than because it is buried under the fast one, and state what distinguishes their data from impurity relaxation (field dependence of 1/T1s, its scaling with hyperfine coupling across P1–P5, its absolute value).

B. Should address

4. The link between the NMR observables and a random-singlet state is qualitative, and the measured distribution does not obviously match RS expectations. The claim that "1/T1s ∝ T signals a type of gapless excitation… a direct consequence of quenched disorder" is supported only by analogy to a thermal-conductivity prediction (ref. 49, quadratic in T); no local 1/T1 is predicted for an RS-like state at T ≈ 0.02J and 0.2 T. Earlier NMR on random Heisenberg chains (refs. 50, 58) found a broad, continuous, stretched-exponential distribution of rates, whereas Fig. 3c shows a bimodal P(1/T1) with two modes two orders of magnitude apart and a gap between them. The QMC (Fig. 3d inset) computes only a correlation length, which is tied to w2 by a heuristic ("increasing effective correlation length", called "phenomenological" on p. 4). It is also unclear what the two spatial regions are: with ΔJ/J ≈ 0.03 on 10% of bonds, every ~10-site segment contains a modified bond, so why would a nucleus in an "RS-like region" relax 100× slower and linearly in T while one in a "clean region" keeps the spinon rate? The authors should compute, with the same disordered model, a quantity NMR measures (the distribution of local susceptibilities, bearing on Kn and the line broadening, or of local 1/T1, via QMC with analytic continuation, strong-disorder RG, or the real-time tensor-network methods of ref. 52), and show whether it gives a bimodal distribution, a linear-in-T slow mode with the observed magnitude and lobe width, and a −ln T weight. Failing that, "random-singlet-like" should be presented as a hypothesis and the title and abstract softened.

5. The robustness of the ILTA results and of the w2(T) law (Fig. 3c,d) is not shown. The Tikhonov parameter α = 0.001 was chosen by inspection, "to provide stability without inducing excessive smoothing" (p. 7), and the text notes that α "directly influences the distribution's width"; weights and peak positions also depend on the time window and on how M(∞) and the inversion factor are fixed. At 0.03 K the slow rate is ~0.1–1 s^-1, so the recovery should extend to tens of seconds, yet Fig. 3a ends near 10–30 s. The authors should show w2(T) for α spanning one to two decades; state the maximum recovery delay, the number of recovery points and the S/N at each temperature; explain how the slow weight is constrained when its recovery is incomplete; compare the ILTA w2 with the amplitude a_s from the double-exponential fit, stating whether a_f and a_s and M(∞) were constrained or shared; compare with a stretched-exponential (or stretched-plus-single) fit, the standard form for random chains; and state how the Fig. 3d error bars were propagated. The −ln(T/T0) form is fitted to about six points over one decade; they should show that a power law w2 ∝ T^−x or a smooth crossover fits measurably worse (χ² or an information criterion) before giving the logarithm physical meaning.

6. The Knight-shift upturn at 0.2 T (Fig. 3e) is fitted over a narrow window, and the lowest-temperature data are discarded. Kn = aT^-1 ln^-2(Ω/T) is fitted over less than a decade (roughly 0.2–0.8 K) with no constant offset; the authors note that "the above fit is only applied within a narrow temperature window". The downturn below 0.2 K, where w2 > 50% and the RS regime should dominate, is attributed to signal loss on the low-frequency tail beyond the spectrometer window (p. 5). Over this window a Curie term C/T + K0 from dilute free spins at chain breaks, a Curie–Weiss form, or a boundary-induced (Eggert–Affleck) chain-end contribution would likely fit as well. The authors should fit these alternatives with an offset, report fit statistics, the stability of Ω and the implied impurity concentration, and show the excluded points. They should test the claimed artifact by spectra over a wider frequency range (frequency-stepped sweeps covering the tail), by integrating the full line, by measuring at a second frequency or field, or by checking whether intensity × T drops at the same temperature, and either recover the missing weight or exclude those points from all conclusions consistently. They should also consider whether the downturn reflects the soliton gap at 0.2 T (concern 2) or incomplete thermalisation (concern 10).

7. The absolute Knight shift at 0.2 T is inconsistent with the higher-field values and needs a field calibration. In the linear-response regime Kn should not depend on field, yet the 0.2 T "spinon" Kn at 1–4 K is ≈ −0.7 to −0.8% (Fig. 3e), while the same H(1) site gives ≈ −0.3% at 2–4 K at 2 and 5 T (Figs. 2c, 4b). At 0.2 T (γH ≈ 8.5 MHz), a remanent or trapped field of a few mT in a superconducting magnet, or an error in the reference frequency, shifts Kn by about 1%; the Methods definition Kn = (f/γH − 1) × 100% requires H to better than 10^-3. The authors should state how the field was calibrated at 0.2 T (for example with a reference sample or by measuring at both field polarities) and reconcile the 0.2 T and 2 T values.

8. The QMC support for a disorder-driven crossover at T0 ≈ 0.02J is incomplete, and the disorder model does not match the one invoked in the Discussion. For weak bond disorder in the S = 1/2 Heisenberg chain, disorder is marginally relevant and the crossover scale to the infinite-randomness regime is expected to be exponentially small in the inverse disorder strength (Doty–Fisher; Fisher 1994, ref. 16); for ΔJ/J ≈ 0.03 on 10% of bonds it would naively lie far below 30 mK. The QMC inset of Fig. 3d (ξ/a ∝ −ln(T/T0) for T/J ≈ 0.003–0.1) has no clean-chain (ΔJ = 0) reference on the same axes, although the clean ξ ∝ J/T with log corrections can look logarithmic over a limited window in this plot. It uses only L = 256 with open boundaries and 32 realisations, and at T/J ≈ 0.003 the clean ξ reaches hundreds of sites, so finite-size saturation could mimic slower-than-power-law growth. The 10% fraction and ΔJ = 0.03 appear chosen rather than derived. In addition, QMC uses a dilute random-bond model, while the Discussion argues for "domain-type" disorder (dirtier regions isolated by vast cleaner ones), and reading w2 as a volume fraction of RS-like regions next to "pure HAFC" regions (p. 4) only makes sense for the latter. The authors should add the ΔJ = 0 curve, results for at least two L and several ΔJ and bond fractions, state how the parameters were fixed, use a disorder model consistent with the Discussion, and explain why a crossover at 0.02J is expected for such weak disorder, or else reconsider the identification.

9. Only one crystal was measured by NMR, and the disorder is not characterised independently. The RS-like interpretation rests on "weak quenched disorder" of speculative origin ("domain-type… stress or unbalanced benzoate group rotation freezing", p. 5), yet NMR was done on a single sample (Methods, p. 7). The disorder strength ΔJ/J ≈ 0.02–0.05 is inferred from the same NMR energy scales it is meant to explain. If the slow channel is disorder-driven, w2(T), T0 and c should vary between crystals and growth batches; if intrinsic, they should not. The authors should show the 0.2 T two-component recovery and w2(T) on a second crystal, ideally from a different growth or with deliberately increased disorder (light substitution, partial deuteration, faster growth, ageing), plus a check after thermal cycling; a systematic change of T0 and c with disorder level would be the most convincing evidence. They should also give an independent bound on the disorder: the low-T Curie tail from ac susceptibility or magnetisation in the dilution refrigerator (bulk susceptibility here stops at 1.8 K, and this would also test the claimed T^-1 ln^-2 enhancement directly; if unavailable, say so), a Schottky or low-T anomaly in specific heat (Supplementary Note 3), ESR linewidths, or structural characterisation (X-ray rocking curves, mosaicity or diffuse scattering, water content and stoichiometry).

10. Thermalisation of the insulating crystal at the lowest temperatures is not demonstrated. The heating checks (randomised recovery delays, ±6 dB pulse power, a factor of 4 in repetition time) show that RF pulses do not heat the sample on the timescale of M(t), but not that the crystal reaches the bath temperature. The thermometer is on the holder, and the sample is an insulator whose thermal conductivity the authors say is strongly suppressed below 0.3 K (ref. 29). The authors should state where the thermometer sits relative to the sample, the thermal anchoring geometry and the waiting times after temperature changes, and show that a thermometric NMR quantity keeps evolving down to 30 mK (for example the nuclear-magnetisation intensity × T, the line shift, or w2 at various waiting times). If the crystal saturates near 60–80 mK, the "w2 = 65% at 0.03 K" point and the −ln T fits change.

11. The spinon evidence at 0.2 T is narrower than the abstract states. The abstract and p. 2 describe 1/T1f as "nearly constant between 1.4 and 0.03 K", while p. 2 also reports "a weak downturn below 0.3 K" and p. 3 a "field-induced suppression below 0.2 K"; Fig. 3b shows the downturn. 12. The 1/T2G ∝ T^-1/2 evidence is weak. The fit spans only ~0.4–2 K, a factor of 5 (Fig. 3b inset), yet the introduction describes "a diverging 1/T2G ∝ T^−1/2" below 1.4 K and the abstract a "divergence-like" 1/T2G "upon cooling", although T2G is measurable only down to 0.4 K. 13. The field misalignment is not quantified, although the staggered field depends strongly on it. The resolved peak count is taken to show that the field is "slightly away from the crystallographic b-axis" (p. 2), but no angle is given. In copper benzoate the staggered field h = c(θ)H, and hence the soliton gap and the DM-induced canting, vary strongly with field direction. The 0.2 T analysis (concern 2), the exponent α = 0.8(1), and the comparison with Cp and INS gaps measured in specified orientations (Fig. 4e) all depend on this. The authors should estimate the misalignment angle from the site splittings and the hyperfine tensors of Supplementary Note 2 and propagate it into the staggered-field coefficient used in LTRG and in Fig. 4e.

14. The extraction of the high-field gap needs more detail and theory-motivated fit forms. Kn = a e^{−Δ/kBT} + b has three free parameters, ignores the power-law prefactors expected for the activated soliton susceptibility, and treats b, attributed to zero-temperature DM canting, as a free constant; the authors should state the fit range, constrain b with the LTRG canting value, and check the procedure by fitting the LTRG Kn(T) of Fig. 4c with the same form to see whether it returns the input gap. The 1/T1 fits in Fig. 4d use a single activated form at P5, whose assignment is uncertain; in the sine-Gordon model intra-branch soliton scattering, soliton–antisoliton creation and breather contributions give different activation energies (Δ or 2Δ) and prefactors, so the authors should state which process dominates and why the activation energy equals Δ. The exponent α = 0.8(1) is only marginally consistent with 2/3; they should say whether the log correction Δ ∝ H^2/3 ln^1/6(J/H) or the field range accounts for this, and report how Δ changes with the fit window. Because the gap is already known from specific heat and INS, they should also state what the NMR determination adds.
