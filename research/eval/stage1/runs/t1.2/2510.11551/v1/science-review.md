A. Must address

1. The slow relaxation component has not been shown to come from disorder in the chain, and simpler site-related explanations have not been ruled out (Fig. 3a, 3c, 3d; "Slow T1 component" section; p. 2, p. 4). The random-singlet (RS) claim rests entirely on the slow component 1/T1s and its weight w2, measured on peak P5. The paper assigns P5 to "either the H(3) site in water molecules or to protons in the benzoate groups". The field is admittedly off the b axis, and Fig. 2a shows the lines are not fully resolved at 0.2 T. P5 is therefore probably a superposition of inequivalent protons with different hyperfine couplings and form factors. Because 1/T1 scales with the square of the hyperfine coupling, such a superposition gives a two-component recovery with rates far apart. These sites would also relax differently once the electronic fluctuations become anisotropic or gapped at low temperature. In a Heisenberg chain, a site whose form factor filters out the q = pi fluctuations sees only the q ≈ 0 contribution, which itself gives 1/T1 proportional to T and a much smaller rate. That is exactly the phenomenology attributed here to the RS state. Other candidates fit the data equally well: paramagnetic impurities or chain ends acting as local relaxation centres, and nuclei relaxing through a channel that becomes visible only once the fast channel saturates. The authors should: (i) measure the recovery at low temperature on well-resolved, single-site lines (P1 to P4, assigned to H(1) and H(2)) and show whether the same slow component with the same w2(T) appears there, or explain why this is impossible; (ii) if P5 contains several sites, quote the slow fraction implied by the site multiplicities and compare it with w2; (iii) ideally repeat the measurement in a second orientation. Without these checks, the "two spatially distinct contributions" picture (p. 4) is an assumption, and the slow channel cannot be attributed to an RS state.

2. At 0.2 T the system is not in the zero-field gapless regime, and the onset of the slow channel coincides with field-generated energy scales; the field dependence of the slow channel is not shown (Figs. 3b–d, 4d; Discussion, p. 5). Scaling the authors' own H^(2/3) gap from Fig. 4d (about 0.2 meV at 5 T, about 0.18 meV at 4 T) gives a staggered-field gap of about 0.023 meV ≈ 0.27 to 0.3 K at 0.2 T. The Zeeman energy g muB H/kB at 0.2 T is about 0.27 K. Both are essentially equal to T0 ≈ 0.4 K, where the slow component appears, and both lie well above the 0.03 K base temperature and above most of the range in Fig. 3d. They also match the weak downturn of 1/T1f below 0.3 K that the authors attribute to solitons. This opens simpler explanations for the second channel: Zeeman polarization of weakly coupled or orphan spins (chain ends, defects), which suppresses their fluctuations and leaves nearby protons relaxing slowly, or partial opening of the soliton gap. In this regime singlets with couplings below the Zeeman energy are polarized and the field opens the staggered-field gap, so the low-energy scaling of a random chain in a field differs from the zero-field RS fixed point. The argument on p. 5 that field-polarized singlets with Delta ≈ g muB H give "effective gapless excitations" is qualitative and does not address the staggered-field gap at all. The decisive test is the field dependence. The authors should analyse the 0.5 T data in Supplementary Fig. S3 exactly as in Fig. 3d, add 1 T (and if possible 0.1 and 2 T), and plot T0, a, the onset temperature and 1/T1s against H together with the soliton gap Delta(H). A T0 that tracks the H^(2/3) gap or the Zeeman energy, or a slow channel that disappears as the gap grows, would point to a field-induced origin; a field-independent T0 would support disorder. The authors should also show that the RS signatures extrapolate to zero field, or alternatively give a calculation (QMC or tanTRG with random bonds, a uniform field and the staggered field) showing that w2 proportional to -ln T survives at 0.2 T.

3. The identification of w2 with a correlation length, and the claimed -ln T law and its link to an infinite-randomness fixed point, are not derived and conflict with RS theory (Fig. 3d, 3e; p. 3–4, "Within the 1D limit, the increase of w2 directly reflects the growth of the thermal correlation length"). w2 is the fraction of nuclear magnetization that relaxes slowly, and no argument or model is given for why it should be proportional to a correlation length; the headline claim "xi proportional to -ln T" rests entirely on this identification. At the Dasgupta–Ma/Fisher infinite-randomness fixed point, length and energy scales are related by ln(Omega0/Omega) proportional to L^(1/2) (psi = 1/2), so xi grows as ln^2(Omega0/T), not ln(1/T), and the fraction of undecimated spins falls as 1/ln^2(Omega0/T). Neither gives w2 linear in ln T. The statement that xi ~ T^(-1/z) with z → infinity "signifies" a ln T law does not fix the power of the logarithm, so the conclusion does not follow as written. The experimental fit spans about one decade (0.03 to 0.3 K) with six to eight points and no error bars; it cannot distinguish ln T from ln^2 T, a weak power law or a saturating crossover. The w2 = 0 points above 0.4 K are excluded by construction, and the extrapolation that the RS state "percolates throughout the entire system" near 8 mK (w2 → 1) is an artefact of the chosen form. The authors should give a model, analytical or numerical, in which w2 is a known function of a physical length or of the density of unpaired spins (for example, the fraction of protons within some distance of a decimated strong bond, or local 1/T1 computed from a disordered-chain QMC or tanTRG ensemble with the actual hyperfine form factors, compared directly with P(1/T1), w2(T) and 1/T1s(T) in Fig. 3c,d). Otherwise they should drop the correlation-length language and present w2(T) as an empirical result. They should also compare ln, ln^2 and weak power-law fits on the same data with fit quality and uncertainties, and remove the 8 mK extrapolation or label it as speculative.

B. Should address

4. The robustness of the ILTA decomposition and the uncertainties on w2 and 1/T1s are not shown (Fig. 3a, 3c, 3d; Methods; p. 2). P(1/T1) and w2 come from a Tikhonov-regularized inversion, whose peak positions, widths and weights depend on noise, the regularization parameter, the time window and imperfect inversion (M(0) not equal to -M(infinity)). At 30 mK the slow channel has T1 of order 3 to 10 s, close to the end of the recovery window in Fig. 3a. Fig. 3d has no error bars. The text says single-exponential fits are used above 0.4 K and biexponential fits "below 0.1 K" (Fig. 3a caption: "0.1 K and below"), yet w2 is nonzero from about 0.3 K, so the procedure between 0.1 and 0.4 K is unclear. The breadth of the slow-component distribution is cited as evidence for RS physics (p. 4), but that breadth is partly set by the regularization. The authors should give error bars on w2 and 1/T1s; show that w2(T) is stable over a range of regularization parameters, time windows and signal-to-noise; cross-check against direct biexponential (a_s/(a_f + a_s)) and stretched-exponential fits of M(t); state the fitting procedure between 0.1 and 0.4 K; show whether the intrinsic width of the slow peak is resolved; and make sure the longest recovery delay exceeds about 5 T1s at every temperature.

5. The quenched disorder is not identified or characterized, and its strength is assumed (Materials and methods; p. 4, "weak quenched disorder unavoidable in real materials"). The paper attributes the state to bond disorder of about Delta J/J = 0.2 but does not say what it is, while also stressing that the crystals are "high-quality". Possibilities include water loss from the trihydrate (which dehydrates readily), water disorder, Cu vacancies, chemical impurities, stacking or bond defects, and surface degradation during measurement. No independent measure of the defect concentration is given: no low-temperature Curie tail in the susceptibility, ESR estimate of free spins, stoichiometry or water content, or analysis of the Kn and linewidth tails. Describing the disorder as "weak" while it controls about 60% of the relaxation weight at 0.04 K also needs discussion. The authors should give an independent estimate of the defect concentration, relate it to Delta J/J = 0.2, and check that it is consistent with the amplitude a ≈ 25.

6. The QMC comparison is not independent or quantitative and lacks a clean-chain reference (Fig. 3e; Supplementary Sec. S4; p. 4). The bond-disorder strength Delta J/J ≈ 0.2 is not justified by any measurement and appears chosen by eye. Fig. 3e shows xi logarithmic over T/J ≈ 0.005 to 0.1–0.15 with about five points, but the paper does not give how xi is defined (from which correlation function: second moment, exponential fit) or averaged over disorder realizations, the system size, the disorder distribution, the number of realizations, error bars, or the fitted T0. The same calculation for Delta J = 0, where xi is proportional to J/T, is not shown, so it is unclear whether the logarithm comes from disorder or reflects a crossover window. For weak bond disorder in the Heisenberg chain, the crossover to the RS fixed point is expected at energies exponentially small in (J/Delta J)^2, so an onset at T0 ≈ 0.02J needs justification; the QMC curve probably sits in the weak-disorder crossover regime rather than the asymptotic RS regime. The QMC is also not compared quantitatively with the measured T0 or amplitude a, nor converted into a predicted w2 or 1/T1. The authors should state all these details in the main text or caption, plot clean and disordered xi(T) together over a wider range for two or three values of Delta J/J, show how T0 and a depend on Delta J/J, and fix the disorder strength from an independent estimate (concern 5).

8. The linear-in-T slow rate, 1/T1s proportional to T, is not connected to any RS prediction (Fig. 3c; Discussion). Korringa-like 1/T1 proportional to T can arise from many mechanisms, including relaxation through paramagnetic centres combined with nuclear spin diffusion, protons far from Cu2+, field-polarized free spins, and the q ≈ 0 contribution at sites that filter q = pi (concern 1). The authors should state, with a calculation or reference, what random-singlet theory predicts for the distribution of local 1/T1 and its temperature dependence for nuclei coupled to a distribution of singlet gaps, and compare it quantitatively with Fig. 3c. Otherwise the "gapless excitation not captured by the pure HAFC" is a separate observation, not specific evidence for an RS state.

13. RS signatures other than the relaxation-rate distribution are not tested (Fig. 2; Discussion). 14. The functional forms used to extract the soliton gap, and the identification of the activation energy with the soliton mass, are not justified (Fig. 4b–d; p. 5). The low-temperature upturn of |Kn| is attributed (Fig. 4e) to growth of the field-induced staggered magnetization, an order-parameter-like quantity, yet Delta is extracted with Kn = a exp(-Delta/kBT) + b, the form of a thermally activated uniform susceptibility, which would make the shift decrease toward zero rather than grow and saturate. In the sine-Gordon description of copper benzoate (Essler 1999), the spectrum contains solitons, antisolitons and breathers, and the lightest breather B1 can lie at or below the soliton mass; Kn probes thermal populations, so its activation energy should be set by the lightest excitation. 1/T1 at the NMR frequency requires two-particle (Raman-type) processes, whose activation energy need not equal the single-particle mass, and the form a exp(-Delta/kBT) for 1/T1 is also unjustified. The "excellent agreement" with INS is overstated: in Fig. 4d the INS points at about 7–8 T lie well below the NMR points. The authors should state which excitation each probe measures; justify the fit forms or use the sine-Gordon expressions for the susceptibility and 1/T1; fit the tanTRG Hhf(T) curve, whose gap is known, with the same form to show that it recovers Delta; and give uncertainties on Delta at each field.

16. The spinon assignment is only qualitative and does not account for logarithmic corrections or the hyperfine form factor of P5 (Fig. 3b; p. 3). For the isotropic S = 1/2 Heisenberg chain, 1/T1 from the staggered channel carries a multiplicative ln^(1/2)(J/T) correction (Sachdev; Sandvik; Starykh, Singh and Sandvik), which over T/J from 0.002 to 0.08 changes 1/T1 by tens of percent, plus a q ≈ 0 diffusive term proportional to T. A constant 1/T1 is a necessary but not unique signature. If the staggered contribution is filtered out at P5, 1/T1 is not a direct measure of the eta = 1 spinon correlations. The authors should state the hyperfine form factor of P5 at q = pi versus q = 0, compare the magnitude and temperature dependence of 1/T1f quantitatively with the log-corrected field-theory prediction (Ref. 33) using J = 18 K and the P5 hyperfine coupling from a Kn–chi plot, and say whether the data can resolve the expected logarithmic variation.
