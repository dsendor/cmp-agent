Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports circularly dichroic RIXS (CD-RIXS) at the O K edge on a freestanding BaTiO3 membrane capacitor. It claims that reversing the ferroelectric polarisation with an out-of-plane field reverses the sign of the phonon-region dichroism, which the authors read as electric-field switching of phonon angular momentum (PAM) at finite momentum. They also claim that the contrast follows the DFT-computed J·q as the sample azimuth is rotated. If established, this would be the first deterministic, field-controlled reversal of phonon chirality at finite q, a clear step beyond the static CD-RIXS observations in quartz and LiNbO3. The experiment is novel and technically demanding. However, the central signal is small, sits largely on the elastic-line tail at a resolution comparable to the phonon energies, comes from one device, and lacks the analyses and controls needed to exclude artefacts that helicity or field reversal could themselves produce.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The question is timely and of broad interest, and the symmetry logic (reversing P is equivalent to a mirror normal to c, which flips the in-plane PAM at fixed q) is sound.
- In situ switching of a freestanding BTO membrane under a thin Au/Cr electrode at a soft-X-ray RIXS beamline, with a "+ − − +" helicity sequence and repeated field cycles over about 15–16 h, is a demanding and carefully designed experiment.
- The design builds in two sign reversals (helicity and field) and an azimuthal q dependence, including q points (q2, q4) that are mirror images with opposite predicted J·q.
- DFT phonon eigenvectors give a mode-resolved helicity map, and the raw data are deposited (ESRF DOI, Zenodo).

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. Elastic-line and normalisation artefacts have not been excluded from the difference spectra. The largest CD and field-switching features (Fig. 3b(i,ii), Fig. 4a, region I) lie within 0–50 meV of the elastic line, at ≈30 meV FWHM resolution, so region I (≈0–0.03 eV) is essentially inside the elastic peak. The elastic peak (≈1.0–1.5 a.u. in Fig. 3a) is about ten times stronger than the phonon features (≈0.1–0.4). The difference spectra are bipolar and derivative-like: positive near 0.01–0.02 eV, negative near 0.04–0.05 eV, then positive again. This is exactly the shape a small relative energy shift, width change or intensity-normalisation mismatch of the elastic or quasi-elastic peak produces; a shift of a few meV gives a difference of ≈0.1 in Fig. 3 units, matching the reported contrast. Such shifts are plausible between helicities (different undulator settings, beam position on the sample) and between field states (piezoelectric or electrostrictive height change of the membrane under the ±0.2 V hold moving the scattering point on the spectrometer, or field-induced changes in specular or diffuse elastic scattering). Because phonon energies are P-even (time reversal plus the C2 rotation of C4v maps ω(q, −P) onto ω(q, +P)), an intrinsic field-induced energy shift is not expected, which makes an extrinsic shift the natural alternative explanation. The paper does not show the fitted elastic-line positions, widths and amplitudes, nor how spectra were normalised (incident flux, acquisition time, total counts, XAS, or elastic or dd intensity), nor whether the energy zero is set per scan or per summed spectrum. The authors should: tabulate or plot the elastic-peak position, width and integrated intensity for every individual scan, by helicity and field state, through the full sequence; overlay the four raw spectra I(C±, ±E) with their fits and state the residual shifts in meV; show the difference spectrum that a measured shift of that size would produce (shift a C+ spectrum by the observed scatter and subtract), and the CD and field-switching spectra after elastic-line subtraction and after deliberate ±few-meV shifts, to quantify how much of regions I and II survives; report the CD of the elastic (zero-loss) intensity itself and in a window with no phonon weight; and state the normalisation explicitly. Without this, the field-induced sign reversal could reflect a reproducible field-dependent shift rather than reversal of the PAM.

2. Figs. 3b(i) and 3b(ii) are not independent, and the helicity × field decomposition that would isolate the chiral signal is missing. With four spectra I(helicity, field), CD(+E) − CD(−E) is identically FS(C+) − FS(C−), so the two panels are one double difference displayed twice, not "two observations" as stated (p. 8). A pure PAM effect requires I(C+, +E) = I(C−, −E) and a helicity-averaged spectrum independent of field; yet Fig. 3b(ii) shows each helicity's spectrum changing on field reversal. The authors should decompose the four spectra into helicity-even/field-even, helicity-odd/field-even, helicity-even/field-odd and helicity-odd/field-odd parts. They should show that the field-odd, helicity-even part, FS(C+) + FS(C−) (non-chiral field-induced changes from strain, electroabsorption or domain-dependent diffuse scattering), is consistent with zero; that the helicity-odd, field-even part, CD(+E) + CD(−E) (a direct measure of birefringent or instrumental CD), is small; and that the reported effect is the helicity-odd, field-odd part. The genuinely independent tests should also be quantified: that CD(+E) and CD(−E) are each individually non-zero, of opposite sign and of equal magnitude within error, and likewise for FS(C+) and FS(C−).

3. The statistical significance of the contrast is not quantified, and the error bars are inconsistently defined. The CD amplitudes in Fig. 3b (≈±0.1 a.u.) are comparable to the error bars at many points, and the curves are Savitzky–Golay smoothed. The Fig. 3 caption says errors come from "Poisson statistics on re-binned data (by a factor 2)", while Methods says "standard deviation of individual scans". The number of scans per spectrum, the counting time per helicity and field state, and how errors propagate into the integrated contrasts of Fig. 4f–h are not given. The authors should state which error estimate is used and give these numbers, present unsmoothed data in the main figures, and report for each energy window the integrated contrast with its uncertainty and significance against zero (in σ, or χ² against zero).

B. Should address

4. Switching of the X-ray-illuminated volume and its domain state are not verified, and the write voltage appears marginal. The PFM loop in Fig. 2b is strongly imprinted: one branch switches near −1 to −2 V, the other near +3.5 to +4.5 V, while the caption quotes "approx 3 V" and the write pulses are ±3.5 V (Fig. 2c, Methods), so the positive write may be incomplete. PFM and the I–V check were done with a tip before the beamtime, presumably on a different area, not through the 60 µm top electrode. The membrane thickness is never given ("various thicknesses"), so the fields in kV/cm and their relation to the coercive field are unknown. The 50 µm beam long axis is barely smaller than the 60 µm electrode, so part of the footprint may lie on unswitched or unelectroded membrane, especially with drift over 15 h. The Methods claim of "a single ferroelectric domain state" is unsupported, possible 90° (a/c) domains in a freestanding membrane are not discussed (they would dilute the P-odd signal and add birefringence), and X-ray photocurrents or charging could depolarise or pin domains. The authors should give the thickness and fields, show in situ switching currents or P–E loops on the actual device at the beamline before, during and after the RIXS sequence (ideally per write pulse), explain why 3.5 V suffices given Fig. 2b, estimate the switched and electroded fraction of the beam footprint, and provide post-beamtime PFM or XRD evidence of the domain population. A field-sensitive X-ray observable recorded at the same time (polarisation-sensitive XAS or linear dichroism, or a Bragg-sensitive elastic intensity) would strongly support switching.

5. The scattering geometry and the link between the CD-RIXS signal and J·q are asserted rather than derived, and the statements are mutually inconsistent. The field-switching measurements are said to be at normal incidence with the beam along c (p. 5; Methods), while the relevant PAM is "perpendicular to this axis" and "projected in-plane", and "the outgoing angle is chosen to minimise the component along c". With the incident beam along c, the incident helicity has no projection on an in-plane J. The text then says the CD "will only follow" the in-plane component of q and is "sensitive only to out-of-plane ion displacements" (p. 9), and elsewhere that it "directly probes the rotation of the 2p orbitals" and "measures the circular motion of the oxygen atoms" (pp. 5–6). Eq. (1) gives A_m with phase factors e^{±2iφ} but does not define φ or say how A_m depends on k_in, k_out, q and J; it is also unclear how normal incidence is compatible with q_z = 0.42 Å⁻¹, the large in-plane q and φ rotation. J is estimated as "the maximal area of the ellipse the oxygen atom traverses" rather than from the standard l = ħ ε†Mε summed over sublattices, and it is not stated which oxygen sublattice (apical O1 or equatorial O2) is used, although these differ in symmetry and in hybridisation with the Ti 3d states probed at 531.25 eV. Since Ti motion also modulates the hybridised states, the CD may weight Ti and O displacements differently from the mass-weighted J of Eq. (2). The authors should give the full geometry (incident and outgoing k-vectors relative to the crystal axes, 2θ, any analysed outgoing polarisation) for each q; derive, at least in a minimal model (for example Franck–Condon with circularly displaced O sites using the DFT eigenvectors), how the dichroism depends on J and why J·q rather than J·k_in or J·(k_in − k_out) is the relevant projection; compute J with the standard expression per sublattice and in total, and show the J·q maps of Fig. 4c–e are robust to this choice; and reconcile the "out-of-plane displacements" statement with an in-plane J.

6. The comparison of the q dependence with DFT J·q (Fig. 4f–h) is a weak quantitative test and contains an unexplained magnitude mismatch. Four φ points per mode over about 1.2 rad are compared with J·q "when scaled accordingly", with a free scale factor per mode whose sign appears to differ between modes (positive in panel f, where negative-J·q points have negative contrast; apparently negative in panel g, where points near φ ≈ 1.1 rad have contrast ≈ +1). Because the sign of the contrast relative to P is the key prediction, a free per-mode sign leaves only the shape of a once-sign-changing curve to be tested. The q1 point in region I is called an outlier and attributed to mode smearing, and the integration windows (regions I–III) appear chosen after the fact. In panel h the computed J·q (±0.002) is about 50 times smaller than for the 11 and 38 meV modes (±0.1, ±0.06), yet the measured contrast is comparable (≈0.75 versus ≈2 a.u.), which is unexplained if contrast ∝ J·q. Fig. 4a shows only q1, q2 and q4 in C− light. The authors should state all fitted parameters (scale, sign, offset) with uncertainties and justify any sign difference between modes from the scattering matrix element; give a goodness of fit against null models (φ-independent or zero contrast, random sign, or sin/cos forms not tied to DFT); explain the panel h magnitude; state whether field-switching data were taken in both field states and both helicities at all four q; and temper "excellent agreement" accordingly.

7. The assignment of the switching CD to specific chiral modes is not supported at the stated resolution. Features at ≈20, 40 and 100 meV are assigned to computed modes at 11, 38 and 100 meV (p. 7). An 11 meV mode cannot be separated from the elastic line at 30 meV FWHM, and the 20 versus 11 meV mismatch is not explained; several modes contribute to each window, as the paper itself invokes for q1. The 100 meV region lies at the top of the one-phonon spectrum, where p. 5 says features are presumed multi-phonon, and a clear feature near 0.2 eV is not discussed. The high-resolution (15 meV) scan is described as confirmation (p. 6) but not shown. The authors should show the high-resolution spectra at the same q with their CD and field switching; show the computed dispersion and effective-charge intensity estimate (Fig. S.11) in the main text; and fit all spectra (both helicities, both field states, all q, plus the high-resolution data) with resolution-convolved, effective-charge-weighted DFT mode intensities, energies fixed and a minimal stated set of amplitudes, comparing predicted and measured CD over the full spectrum rather than three windows, and stating which modes contribute to each window and how the windows were chosen.

8. There is no null control where the effect should be absent. All data are from one device at q points where PAM is predicted to be non-zero. The paper's own symmetry argument provides nulls: q3 = (0, 0.24, 0.42) lies on a σ_v mirror plane of C4v, where J·q = 0 (white regions of Fig. 4c–e), yet its spectrum is omitted from Fig. 4a (shown only dotted or as a single integrated point in Fig. 4f–h) and not analysed. The authors should show the full q3 spectra and field-switching contrast with error bars and state whether they vanish as predicted. Further controls would separate polarisation reversal from other field-odd effects: a sub-coercive control (±1 V write pulses, where no reversal should occur) to exclude direct voltage effects; measurement above Tc (≈120 °C); an unpoled, depolarised or multidomain state; a region outside the electrode; a high-symmetry q at both polarities; and a non-polar reference membrane (e.g. SrTiO3) in the same electrode stack to rule out electrode, charging or birefringence effects.

9. Fitting the elastic line from the energy-gain side is biased at room temperature. The text (p. 6) treats the energy-gain side as containing only thermally excited states and fits it with a half-Lorentzian. At kT ≈ 25 meV, the 11 and 38 meV modes have substantial anti-Stokes weight there (Bose factors ≈0.6 and 0.2 relative to Stokes), biasing both the energy zero and the elastic subtraction, possibly differently for the two helicities if the anti-Stokes phonons carry CD. The authors should fit Stokes and anti-Stokes phonons together with the elastic line under detailed balance, or calibrate the zero independently (e.g. a carbon-tape reference before and after each block), and show that the CD conclusions do not depend on the choice.

10. The non-volatility and 15-hour stability claims are not demonstrated. All RIXS data were taken under a ±0.2 V hold bias (Fig. 2c, p. 6), applied "to ensure the ferroelectric domains remain stable", so the data show reproducible switching under bias, not retention at zero field. "Stable for at least 15 hours" (abstract, Fig. 3 caption, p. 10) is inferred from spectra summed over about five to eight state changes. The authors should show the CD or field-switching contrast per cycle and per block versus time across the sequence, demonstrating that the sign follows the field every time, and measure at least one state at zero bias after writing, or remove the non-volatility claim.

11. The exclusion of birefringent CD rests on unstated assumptions. Birefringence is said to be removed by normal incidence along c (p. 5, Methods Eq. 5), although Fig. S.4 shows it can be "strong". This assumes a pure c-domain membrane with no a/c domains, no in-plane anisotropy from transfer, clamping or wrinkling, and exact alignment, while the outgoing beam is far from c and the electrode layers are in the beam path. Birefringent CD should be P-even, so CD(+E) + CD(−E) measures it directly (concern 2), but 180° switching could alter domain-wall configurations, and a linear electro-optic contribution under the hold bias would be P-odd. The authors should give the incidence and exit angles for each q and the misalignment tolerance, estimate the residual birefringent CD at those angles from the Fig. S.4 model, characterise the domain state of the measured membrane (PFM on the device, or XRD for a-domain fractions), and estimate any electro-optic contribution.

14. The DFT structure departs substantially from experiment in the quantity that controls the effect. The relaxed lattice (p. 13) has c/a = 4.11/3.99 ≈ 1.030 against the experimental 4.03/4.00 ≈ 1.0075; PBE overestimates tetragonality and hence the polar distortion that, by the paper's argument, sets the PAM magnitude. At 0 K, PBE tetragonal BaTiO3 is not the ground state and typically has unstable (imaginary) modes, which affects precisely the low-energy, soft-mode-derived 11 and 38 meV branches. The relaxation "to an internal pressure of 70 kPa" with a D3 correction is not explained, and it is not stated whether the non-analytic LO–TO (Born effective charge) correction was included at |q| ≈ 0.48 Å⁻¹. Bulk calculations are compared with a membrane of unspecified thickness and possibly different strain. The authors should show the full dispersion and any imaginary modes and their treatment; repeat the key mode energies, eigenvectors and J·q (sign pattern and magnitude) at the experimental lattice parameters or with PBEsol; state the non-analytic treatment; and compare with the membrane's actual lattice parameters from the XRD in Figs. S.1–2, confirming it is tetragonal at the measurement temperature.

15. The "gyroelectric effect" framing (abstract, p. 8, Eqs. 3–4) is not justified for C4v. The analogy draws on Pb5Ge3O11 (point group 3, gyrotropic), but tetragonal BTO (4mm) is not optically active, so the q→0 gyration tensor in Eq. (4) vanishes for a uniform polar state; the effect here relies on finite-q spatial dispersion. Eq. (3) uses an undefined λ_klj, and Eq. (4) posits g ∝ Ps, whereas only two states (±P) are measured, so linearity in P is untested. The authors should derive the symmetry-allowed tensor component for 4mm at finite q mapping onto the CD-RIXS observable, or soften the wording to a sign reversal consistent with a P-odd response (or measure partially switched states).
