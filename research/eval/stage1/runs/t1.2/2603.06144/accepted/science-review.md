A. Must address

1. No null control separates a polarization-driven phonon CD from other effects that are odd in the field or in P. The central claim is that the CD that switches with the field "must arise from the phononic subsystem" (p. 3), resting on the sentence "Because we have shown that other sources of circular contrast in the RIXS signal are negligible". The main text supports this only with symmetry arguments ("CD is absent for isotropic charge scattering and BTO is non-magnetic"). In each field configuration the sample is written at ±3.5 V and then held at ±0.2 V (about 25 kV/cm over 80 nm) during measurement (Methods; Fig. 3 caption), so the sign of the DC bias flips together with P. Reversing the bias or P also changes several things that are not phonon chirality: piezoelectric strain, and with it membrane thickness, height and local tilt under the 50 µm spot, which shift the elastic line and the scattering geometry; electro-optic response (BTO has very large coefficients); electrostatic or photo-induced charging under the electrode; oxygen-vacancy and interfacial-charge redistribution under the Cr contact, which would alter the O K-edge absorption; and electroabsorption. The incident energy, 531.25 eV, sits on the leading edge of the O K-edge XAS, where a small edge shift produces a large intensity change. In addition, with P, k_in and k_out non-coplanar the scattering geometry is itself chiral, so a polar crystal can show a P-odd CD in elastic, quasi-elastic or electronic scattering with no phonon angular momentum. The existing internal checks (C+ and C− field-switching traces that are near mirror images; a sign change between the mirror pair q2 and q4) exclude purely helicity-independent artifacts, but not a field-odd intensity or geometry change multiplied by a static, helicity-dependent background, nor a tilt-induced in-plane projection that rotates with φ. The authors should show, under matched conditions, at least some of: (a) the field-switching difference measured with linear (π or σ) polarization, which should be zero; (b) the field-switched CD of the elastic line itself (integrated over about −20 to +10 meV), as a quantitative null; (c) the field-independent part of the CD, [CD(F+) + CD(F−)]/2, which should vanish if all the CD comes from P; (d) the CD at zero hold bias after writing, and with the same sign of hold bias in both polarization states; (e) a toggle of a sub-coercive voltage (for example ±0.2 V without the write pulses, or a ±1.5 V write, where Fig. 1c shows no switching), under which a P-driven signal must not switch, and ideally a hysteresis loop of the integrated CD (region I or II) against write voltage that matches the PFM loop of Fig. 1c; (f) the switched CD at a q where J·e_q vanishes by symmetry (q3 = (0, 0.24, 0.42) Å⁻¹ on the mirror plane, the (110) mirror direction, or q∥c), stated and quantified explicitly; (g) the switched CD above the single-phonon cutoff (0.12–0.25 eV), where Fig. 3 still shows some structure; (h) the O K-edge XAS (TFY) for F+ and F− at fixed helicity, to rule out a field-induced edge shift; (i) a consistency check on the anti-Stokes side, where at 295 K the 11 and 38 meV modes are appreciably populated and detailed balance fixes the expected CD; and ideally (j) the same protocol above T_C or on a non-polar reference membrane such as SrTiO3 with the same electrode stack, where the CD should vanish and not switch. Without such controls the attribution to chiral phonons is not secure.

B. Should address

2. The statistical significance of the switching signal is not established, and the key traces are smoothed. The field-switching and CD differences in Fig. 3 (middle and bottom) are about ±0.05–0.1 on spectra peaking near 1.2, and many error bars are comparable to the shaded lobes, especially above 50 meV. A Savitzky–Golay filter is applied "to guide the eye" in Fig. 3 and to the curves compared across q in Fig. 4a; smoothing a noisy difference can create oscillating, derivative-like lobes on the scale of the smoothing window, and the region assignment I/II/III relies on those lobes. The error-bar definition is inconsistent: the Fig. 3 caption says s.d. "reflecting Poisson counting statistics", the Methods say "the standard deviation of individual scans", and the Fig. 4 caption uses Gaussian propagation; for n = 16 the standard error of the mean differs from the s.d. by a factor of 4. How the integrated values and errors in Fig. 4f–h are obtained within each window is not stated. The authors should show unsmoothed differences with the smoothed curves overlaid; state one error definition per figure (s.d. or s.e.m. over the 16 repetitions); report for each region and q the integrated switching and CD contrasts with uncertainties, significance against zero and a χ² against zero, giving most weight to the interaction term I(C+,F+) − I(C+,F−) − I(C−,F+) + I(C−,F−), which alone isolates a helicity-odd, field-odd response; and add a split-half test, in which the same difference computed between two halves of the repeats at the same field direction should be consistent with zero.

3. The polarization state of the illuminated volume during RIXS is never verified. Ferroelectricity was confirmed by PFM (Fig. 1c, Methods), apparently before device completion, with an Au tip on an Au bottom electrode rather than in the final Cr/Au capacitor under the X-ray beam. The PFM amplitude loop is asymmetric and imprinted (coercive voltages of roughly −2 V and +2.6 V), the 3.5 V write voltage sits at the stated onset of degradation and fatigue (Supplementary Fig. 5), and the 0.2 V hold may not stabilize the state against back-switching, X-ray-induced photocarriers or charging. Partial switching is therefore possible. The 60 µm pad is close in size to the 50 µm long axis of the beam footprint (Fig. 2a inset), so part of the signal may come from unswitched regions or from the Cr/Au electrode edge. The authors should provide in-situ evidence of switching on the measured device, such as switching-current or P–E/P–V loops recorded at the beamline before and after each RIXS block, a structural or spectroscopic probe of the sign of P (Friedel-pair intensity asymmetry of a (00L) reflection, SHG on the device, or XAS linear dichroism before and after switching), together with an estimate of the switched fraction; and they should state the alignment tolerance of the beam on the pad.

4. Energy-shift and elastic-line artifacts in the difference spectra are not excluded. The switching and CD signals (Fig. 3, Fig. 4a) are differences of spectra taken at about 30 meV FWHM resolution, and they show derivative-like, alternating-sign lobes around the elastic line and the 40–100 meV features. This is the lineshape that a sub-meV relative energy shift, a small change in elastic intensity or a small change of scattering geometry between configurations would produce, for example from piezoelectric changes of membrane thickness and tilt. The Methods set the energy zero by fitting the gain side of the elastic line, but do not show the fitted elastic-line positions, widths and intensities for the four configurations (F±, C±), nor that the differences survive independent alignment (to the carbon-tape reference or by cross-correlating the phonon region). The helicity antisymmetry of the field-switching traces argues against a pure shift, but not against a shift acting on a helicity-dependent background. The authors should report the residual energy offsets and elastic intensities per configuration and per repetition, show that the elastic line does not change between F+ and F−, and simulate the difference spectrum those offsets would produce, showing it is much smaller than, or differently shaped from, the measured signal.

5. The mode assignment is finer than the energy resolution. With about 30 meV FWHM resolution, the CD and switching traces in Fig. 3 show lobes 15–20 meV wide that are assigned to individual modes at 11 and 38 meV; the text locates the strongest contrast "around 20, 40 and 100 meV" while the dashed lines are drawn at 11, 38 and 100 meV. Region I (0 to about 40 meV, Fig. 4a) lies on the tail of the elastic line, where a field-induced elastic shift or a resolution-function asymmetry produces a derivative-shaped difference. The text says BTO phonons end at about 100 meV and higher features are "presumed to be multiphonon"; region III (centred at 100 meV) sits on that boundary, beside a strong FY peak near 0.1 eV, and the highest LO branch of tetragonal BTO is lower, so it may be multiphonon or partly elastic-tail and background. Each window also contains several modes, presumably with different signs of J·e_q. The 15 meV high-resolution data are described but not shown in the main text, and are used only to locate phonons, not to test switching. The authors should show the high-resolution spectrum with a multi-peak decomposition, ideally the field switching at 15 meV resolution for at least q2 and q4; model the region I signal after elastic subtraction; fix the integration windows before analysis from the DFT dispersion convolved with the resolution and state how robust the integrated contrasts are to the window edges; and justify assigning region III to a single one-phonon mode, discussing multiphonon and continuum contributions.

6. The comparison with DFT is not quantitative, contrary to the abstract. In Fig. 4f–h the measured "CD contrast (a.u.)" and the calculated J·e_q are on unrelated scales, with a free scale factor apparently set separately for each region; each region rests on four points with large error bars, several consistent with zero in region III; and the RIXS cross-sections (electron–phonon coupling per mode) were not calculated. Given the mirror symmetry, any contrast odd under x → −x crosses zero at φ = π/2 and has opposite signs at q2 and q4, so agreement in sign and zero crossing tests symmetry rather than the DFT eigenvectors. Where the data do test the calculation the agreement is weak: in Fig. 4f the q1 point (φ ≈ 2.1) is about 0.4 ± 0.4 against a calculated 1.8–1.9, contradicting the statement on p. 5 that the "CD contrast is largest near q1". The calculated J·e_q for the 100 meV mode (colour scale ±0.002, Fig. 4e, h) is about 50 times smaller than for the 11 meV mode (±0.1), yet the measured contrasts in regions I and III differ by only about a factor of 4; if CD ∝ J·e_q this is a discrepancy, otherwise a large unknown mode-dependent prefactor is being absorbed. It is also unclear whether J in Eq. 2 and Fig. 4 is the oxygen-projected (as Fig. 4b states) or total-cell angular momentum, why that choice suits the O K-edge contrast, and how modes within a window are weighted. The authors should state which quantity is plotted; fit all regions with one global scale factor, or with factors fixed by the mode effective charges they already compute (Supplementary Section 4), and report the fitted factor and χ²; compare with simple alternatives (zero, constant, sin φ with free amplitude, a geometry-only model); and compute a resolution-convolved CD-RIXS spectrum from the effective-charge-weighted sum of DFT helicities over all modes in each window (extending Supplementary Fig. 17) and overlay it on Fig. 3 across 0–120 meV. Otherwise "quantitative agreement" should become "consistent in sign and symmetry".

7. The g-wave symmetry in the title is not established by the measured q range. All four q points lie on one arc with q_y ≈ 0.2–0.24 Å⁻¹ and fixed q_z = 0.42 Å⁻¹, spanning roughly 40° in φ (Table 1, Fig. 4f–h) and containing a single nodal plane at φ = π/2. Many angular forms are odd under the mirror through b* and vanish at π/2, including p- and d-wave forms and any geometric or birefringence effect that depends on azimuth. A g-wave (C4v, eight-sector) pattern requires further sign changes at the diagonal mirrors (φ = π/4 and 3π/4 ≈ 2.36 rad, just beyond q1) and equal signs at C4-related points. The conclusion that the contrast "inverts upon measuring along an oppositely oriented q" (p. 5) is also not what was measured: only mirror-related in-plane components (q2 and q4) were compared, at the same q_z. The authors should add points across a diagonal mirror (for example φ ≈ 2.4–2.8 rad), C4-related points or another quadrant, and show the expected sign pattern; otherwise they should restate the claim as "sign reversal across a mirror plane, consistent with the predicted g-wave pattern" and adjust the title, abstract and conclusions.

10. The residual birefringence contribution in the actual geometry is not quantified. Normal incidence along c suppresses birefringence for the incident beam, but the outgoing beam is not along c for any q in Table 1, and Supplementary Fig. 8 shows "strong dichroism" from birefringence away from this geometry. A free-standing membrane on a patterned electrode is unlikely to be perfectly flat, so local tilts give a finite in-plane projection that rotates with φ. A 180° switch leaves the optic axis unchanged, but the film is electro-optically active, and piezoelectric strain under the hold bias or any a-domain population changes Δn between the two states. The authors should estimate the residual birefringent CD at the measurement geometry from Eq. (5) and the measured optical constants, make explicit the argument for the polarization-insensitive detection, and show that the residual is much smaller than the signal in Fig. 3 or is even under polarization reversal.

11. The domain structure, morphology and thermal state of the membrane are not shown. Free-standing 80 nm BTO membranes often contain 90° (a-) domains and wrinkles; either breaks the assumed geometry (c along the beam), mixes in in-plane polarization components outside the 4mm picture of Fig. 1a, and alters the birefringent background. The text says orientation "was confirmed by non-specular X-ray diffraction", but tetragonality, a-domain fraction, rocking-curve width (the tilt distribution under the spot) and lateral PFM are not reported. Measurements are at 295 K, near the 278 K orthorhombic transition that the authors note can cause shear strain, without showing that the membrane is single-phase tetragonal there or stating temperature stability and beam-induced heating; field-induced phase coexistence near 278 K would matter. These data should be given, and the CD magnitude discussed in light of any a-domain fraction.

12. Oxygen sources other than the BTO lattice are not addressed. The signal passes through a 4 nm Cr / 8 nm Au top electrode; Cr in contact with BTO or exposed at pad edges is likely partly oxidized, and photoresist residues, carbon tape and adsorbates may also contribute O K-edge signal. A field-dependent redox reaction at the Cr/BTO interface could produce a field- and polarization-dependent spectral change. The authors should estimate the fraction of RIXS intensity from non-BTO oxygen, confirm it carries no field-odd CD, compare the calibration XAS (Supplementary Fig. 6) with reference BTO, and show that the XAS does not change between F+ and F−.

13. The DFT structural model needs justification. In harmonic PBE, tetragonal BaTiO3 is dynamically unstable toward the orthorhombic and rhombohedral phases, so imaginary or strongly anharmonic soft modes are expected, particularly in the low-energy branch relevant to region I (11 meV); the paper does not say whether the dispersion (Supplementary Fig. 11) has unstable branches or how they were treated. The relaxed structure has c/a ≈ 1.030 (a = 3.99 Å, c = 4.11 Å) against an experimental c/a ≈ 1.0075 (4.00/4.03 Å), a known PBE overestimate that inflates the Ti off-centring, shifts soft-mode energies and probably enlarges the PAM; membrane strain is not modelled. The treatment of the LO–TO non-analytic correction away from Γ along the measured q is not explained. Because Fig. 4 relies on the signs and φ dependence of J·e_q for specific modes, the authors should show these are robust, for example with PBEsol or at the experimental lattice parameters.
