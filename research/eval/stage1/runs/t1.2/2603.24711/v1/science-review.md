**A. Must address**

1. **The NbN threshold is not shown to be intrinsic depairing, and the proposed reason the picosecond and DC limits differ (vortices "inertially frozen") is not tested by any experiment.** The central claim (abstract; Discussion p. 9) is that DC transport is limited by vortex entry, motion and heating, while a ~2 ps pulse is too short for vortices to move, so the intrinsic Jc* ≈ 2.2 Jc is reached. The evidence is a kinematic estimate (vortex velocities of tens of km/s × 2 ps ≈ tens of nm), zero-field cooling, and the NbN/YBCO contrast, comparing only two limits (ms pulses, Fig. S6, and ~2 ps pulses). None of these tests the mechanism, and two other routes to a sharp collapse within the pulse are not excluded. (a) Electrothermal runaway: a ~45–47 mA pulse (the threshold current in Fig. S12) through R_n ≈ 140 Ω dissipates I²Rτ ≈ 0.5–0.6 pJ in 2 ps into ~6 µm³ of film, against a condensation energy B_c²V/2μ0 ≈ 0.04 pJ for the 10 × 30 × 0.02 µm³ strip (B_c ≈ Φ0/(2√2πλξ) ≈ 0.13 T from the authors' λ and ξ). A small seed of dissipation (a weak spot, an edge phase slip, or a kinematic vortex, which moves much faster than the Abrikosov vortex speeds cited in refs. 7, 18–22) could self-amplify into a hotspot that mimics a sharp threshold, so the "threshold" would reflect the weakest point plus feedback rather than uniform bulk depairing. The gradual approach to the normal state above threshold (Fig. 4a, 2.5–6 Jc), which the adiabatic BCS model does not reproduce (Fig. 5b, S20), is suggestive of heating. (b) Edge-nucleated vortex entry, which is sensitive to current crowding (see concern 11). The authors should provide at least one, and preferably several, discriminating controls: (i) threshold versus pulse duration or shape (2 ps to tens or hundreds of ps, via a different switch material or gap, filters, a dispersive line or a pulse train), showing that Jc* is duration-independent, or a crossover from Jc* towards Jc on the timescale predicted by vortex entry and transit; (ii) field-cooled measurements with a known perpendicular vortex density, and threshold versus a small perpendicular field, showing that the DC Jc falls strongly while Jc* stays near the depairing value (depairing depends weakly on field, edge vortex entry strongly); (iii) a strong-pump/weak-probe two-pulse experiment measuring how fast superconductivity recovers after a supra-threshold pulse, since recombination-limited and thermal recovery look different; (iv) devices with different width (including w ≪ Λ_Pearl), pinning or edge quality (e.g. an ion-irradiated strip, cf. ref. 21), in which Jc should change and Jc* should not. The vortex argument should also be made quantitative, by showing that the voltage across the strip just above threshold (of order volts) would require an impossibly large vortex flux rate for the displacements allowed. Without such controls, "Jc* ≫ Jc because vortices are inertially immobile" remains a hypothesis, and the observation could equally be a pulse-length-dependent instability threshold.

2. **The calculated Jc*(T) in Fig. 5a, and hence the "excellent agreement" with "microscopic dynamics based on BCS theory" (abstract), rests on a formula that mixes clean and dirty limits, so the agreement may be partly coincidental.** The main text (p. 10) and SI Eq. 9 apply the clean-limit Landau criterion ħk_F v_s = Δ with v_s = ħ/(πmξ), giving Jc* = 2ħe n_s/(πmξ) = Φ0/(π²μ0λ²ξ). The identity Δ/(ħk_F) = ħ/(πmξ) uses the clean BCS (Pippard) length ξ0 = ħv_F/(πΔ), but the ξ(0) = 5.24 nm inserted is the dirty-limit GL length from Bc2 (WHH, Eqs. 2–3), which for disordered NbN is much shorter than ξ0. The authors themselves stress that NbN is strongly disordered (p. 11, SI S10), and in that limit the Landau criterion does not set depairing: pair breaking is gradual and the film becomes gapless at finite superfluid momentum before the current maximum. The prefactor matters at the level of the claimed agreement. With λ = 350 nm and ξ = 5.24 nm the paper's formula gives ≈260 GA/m² at T → 0, matching the dashed line; the GL expression Φ0/(3√3πμ0λ²ξ) is smaller by 3√3/π ≈ 1.65 (≈160 GA/m²), below the measured 220 GA/m² at 7 K; and a rough Kupriyanov–Lukichev / Clem–Kogan estimate (ref. 11), j_d(0) ≈ 1.491 Δ0^(3/2)/(eρ_n(ħD)^(1/2)) with ρ_n ≈ 90 µΩ cm (R_n ≈ 140 Ω over ~3 squares), D ≈ 0.9 cm²/s (Bc2 slope ≈ 1.2 T/K) and Δ0 ≈ 1.76 k_BTc, gives several hundred GA/m². Agreement to ~10% with one particular prefactor therefore does not test BCS. The temperature dependences are also ad hoc: λ(T) in Eq. 10 is the two-fluid (1 − t⁴)^(−1/2) form rather than dirty BCS, and ξ(T) in Eq. 11 is a non-standard tanh/nested-root form that differs from Eq. 27, ξ(T) = ξ(0)[Δ(0)/Δ(T)]^(1/2). The authors have a full Usadel calculation (SI Eq. 29) but do not use it for Fig. 5a, and the main text says the measured Jc* lies slightly below the Fig. 5a estimate while the Usadel simulation is said to reproduce the threshold. The authors should derive Jc*(T) consistently in the dirty limit (Kupriyanov–Lukichev or their own Usadel route) from independently measured ξ(0) or D, λ(0) or ρ_n, and Tc, plot it in Fig. 5a with propagated uncertainties (concern 5) alongside GL and Kupriyanov–Lukichev benchmarks, state which parameters are free, and reconcile the two theoretical routes. If agreement survives only with tuned parameters, "well described by" should become "consistent with".

3. **The YBCO data do not support the claims that a d-wave superconductor has no well-defined depairing current, that the gradual suppression "reflects its d-wave symmetry" (abstract), or that the method offers a "new approach to probing gap symmetry".** The statement (p. 7, p. 10) that with an anisotropic gap "there is no well-defined intrinsic current density threshold" and Jc* "is not a well-defined quantity" is incorrect as theory: in a d-wave superconductor nodal quasiparticles make the superfluid density fall with superfluid velocity (the nonlinear Meissner effect; Doppler-shift depairing), but the Eilenberger/Usadel supercurrent j(v_s) still has a maximum, softened but present. Nodes mainly change the low-current nonlinearity and the temperature dependence, and gap anisotropy alone does not obviously turn a sharp collapse into a nearly linear decrease that starts near Jc(DC) and has not saturated at 8 × Jc. Moreover, the YBCO depairing current is usually estimated at several thousand GA/m² at low temperature, while the largest transmitted current in Fig. 4c,d is ≈300 GA/m², so the absence of a maximum most plausibly means the depairing current was never reached; and a ~50% drop in E_T at J/J_d ~ 0.1 looks far larger than the intrinsic nonlinear Meissner effect. Extrinsic explanations are not excluded: the broad resistive transition (~82–90 K, Fig. S10b) and reduced Tc (85 K, suggesting oxygen deficiency); twin domains, which YBCO on r-sapphire/CeO2 normally has despite the "monocrystalline" description (p. 10, based only on XRD, Fig. S2), and low-angle grain boundaries or weak links acting as Josephson-like nonlinear inductance (the authors themselves invoke grain-boundary phase slips for polycrystalline films, ref. 26); an implied λ(50 K) ≈ 0.7 µm (from the quoted Pearl length of 21 µm with d = 50 nm), several times typical values and pointing to disorder or inhomogeneous superfluid density; vortex entry at T/Tc ≈ 0.6; and heating in a film whose normal resistance depends on temperature (concern 4). All of this rests on one device at one temperature (50 K), with no model. To support any symmetry-related claim the authors need: a d-wave calculation (e.g. quasiclassical nonlinear Meissner plus depairing, in the same lumped-circuit framework as the NbN Usadel model) of transmittance versus J_T,peak compared quantitatively with Fig. 4c,d; YBCO data at several temperatures, including well below 50 K and close to Tc, and at T/Tc matched to NbN (7 K is 0.48 Tc, 50 K is 0.59 Tc); the bridge orientation relative to the Cu–O bonds, ideally comparing current along nodal [110] and antinodal [100] directions; evidence on film homogeneity and twinning; and ideally a second YBCO device, an untwinned film, or a disordered s-wave control with a comparably broad transition. Alternatively, reaching much higher current densities (narrower or thinner bridges) would test whether a maximum exists. Otherwise the conclusion should be narrowed to "YBCO shows no sharp threshold" and presented as a qualitative contrast.

4. **The YBCO 110 K "normal-state" reference is not the normal-state response at 50 K, so the "incomplete suppression" conclusion may follow from the choice of baseline.** Fig. 4c,d and SI S6 compare the 50 K data with the 110 K response and state that the transmittance at 6 × Jc (≈43%) exceeds the 110 K value (≈23%), "indicating incomplete suppression of superconductivity". Unlike NbN, whose R(T) is flat above Tc (Fig. S5a), YBCO's resistance is roughly linear in T (Fig. S10a). In the lumped series-element picture, T ≈ 2Z0/(2Z0 + R): R ≈ 230 Ω at 110 K gives T ≈ 0.30, while a linear extrapolation to 50 K gives R ≈ 100–140 Ω and T ≈ 0.42–0.5, close to the 43% at 6 × Jc and the ~0.37 at 8 × Jc in Fig. 4c. The data may thus be consistent with a fully resistive (or heated) strip at 50 K rather than partial depairing. The authors should estimate the normal-state transmittance expected at 50 K, for example by passing the extrapolated ρ(T) through the SI circuit model (Eqs. 5–8 / 21–24), redraw Fig. 4c,d against that reference, and discuss how electron heating during the resistive part of the pulse shifts it.

8. **The effective sample temperature, optical exposure and heating during the picosecond measurements are not characterized.** The measurement is a stroboscopic average over many laser shots, but the repetition rate, average power and pump fluence on the switches, spot positions and switch-to-strip distance, how the pulse amplitude is varied (switch bias voltage or optical power), and whether scattered 515 nm light reaches the strip are not given. Repetition-rate-averaged substrate heating or optically injected quasiparticles would lower the effective gap and shift the T axis of Fig. 5a. Within a pulse, once the strip is resistive (ρ ≈ 9×10⁻⁷ Ω m from R_n = 140 Ω), a 2 ps pulse at ~2×10¹¹ A/m² deposits ~10⁵ J/m³, enough to raise the NbN electron temperature by tens of kelvin or above Tc, which will shape the curve above Jc*. The authors should report these parameters; show that the low-drive transmittance (or kinetic inductance) and Jc* do not depend on repetition rate, on laser power at fixed switch bias (reaching the same current by bias versus power), or on scattered light (e.g. by moving or blocking excitation of the reference switch); use the weak-pulse Lkin(T) as an in-situ thermometer; and estimate the electron-temperature rise and discuss electron heating above threshold.

9. **Time-resolved waveforms near threshold are not shown, and the extraction of Jc* from transmitted peak amplitudes may be biased.** Fig. 3c and Fig. S13c show waveforms only far below and far above threshold (between 0.2 and 6.5 Jc), and Jc* is read as the local maximum of J_T,peak versus J_IN,peak (Fig. 4b inset, S14d). 11. **The uniformity of the current distribution is overstated, so the quoted cross-section-average Jc* may underestimate the local current at depairing.** SI S8.3 and S10.1 argue that the current is uniform because Λ_Pearl ≈ 12–13 µm is "larger than" the 10 µm width; these are comparable, so the sheet current is still enhanced at the edges. 12. **The adiabatic, equilibrium assumption in the Usadel model is justified circularly.** SI S10.1 assumes the order parameter follows the current instantaneously with an equilibrium (tanh) distribution, and the final paragraph of S10.3 argues that agreement with the data justifies this ("Since the two values are consistent, we can justify the use of instantaneous equilibrium theory"). In NbN, τ_GL at 7 K is sub-ps, but energy relaxation and quasiparticle recombination (≳10 ps) exceed the pulse duration, so the quasiparticle distribution is likely non-thermal under 2 ps drive, affecting both n_s and the threshold, especially once the gap collapses within the pulse. The authors should give τ_GL, τ_E and the gap relaxation time at each temperature and discuss why a dynamic threshold should coincide with the static depairing current rather than exceed it, as is often found for short pulses.
