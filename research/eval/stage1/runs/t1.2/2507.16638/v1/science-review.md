A. Must address

1. The pump fluence varies across the X-ray probe footprint, and this alone could produce the heterogeneity attributed to nanoscale nucleation. The pump is 136 × 142 µm FWHM at ≈25° incidence. The X-ray beam is collimated/focused to 50 µm and hits the sample at 20.9°, so its footprint along the beam is ≈50 µm / sin(20.9°) ≈ 140 µm, essentially equal to the pump FWHM (Methods, p. 9). The probed area therefore samples local fluences from the peak down to roughly half of it, and at 7.7 mJ cm⁻² parts of the footprint are well above threshold while others are near or below it. The Methods state the opposite assumption: "the large footprint of the pump pulse compared to the footprint of the probe pulse ensures a laterally homogeneous excitation of the probed volume" (p. 10), and p. 7 says "higher fluences … transform the entire near-surface region". Because the transition has a sharp fluence threshold, a Gaussian fluence profile by itself yields a final in-plane coverage A*_FM < 1 and a transformed depth D_FM that varies across the footprint — a macroscopic version of scenarios III/IV that has nothing to do with stochastic nucleation. This bears directly on the scenario ranking (Fig. 4, Ext. Data Fig. E3), on V*_FM and the fluence assignment in E3, on the low-fluence interpretation (A*_FM = 0.66 attributed to regions "exhibiting the lowest transition temperature"), on the identification of D_FM with the depth where excitation exceeds threshold, and on the extra FM peak broadening used to size the domains. The authors should report the actual pump and X-ray footprints on the sample in both directions (including projection) and any relative pointing jitter, state whether the quoted fluences are peak or footprint-averaged, and include the sampled fluence distribution in the sonogram model as a weighted incoherent sum over local fluences (each with its own D_FM and V_FM). They should then show whether scenario III still beats a laterally graded scenario I, and whether the low-fluence A*_FM = 0.66 result survives — or show quantitatively that the gradient is negligible. Ideally a key delay scan would be repeated with a smaller X-ray footprint or larger pump spot.

2. The preference for scenario III rests on small, unweighted χ² differences with no statistical significance. χ² is defined as an unweighted sum of squared intensity differences (p. 6); no noise estimate, reduced χ², number of degrees of freedom or uncertainty on χ² is given (Fig. 4c,d; Ext. Data Fig. E3). The separations are modest: at 7.7 mJ cm⁻² the global minima are ≈0.060 (III), ≈0.067 (IV) and ≈0.082 (I) in E3c (≈0.09 vs 0.10–0.12 in Fig. 4d); at 5.2 mJ cm⁻² (E3b) III ≈0.030 vs IV ≈0.032; at 3.9 mJ cm⁻² (E3a) III, IV and I lie within ≈0.005 of one another near the measured V*_FM (IV ≈0.009 vs III ≈0.012); and in Fig. 4a curves I, III and IV are hard to tell apart. The residual also does not account for the known systematic misfit of the FM peak width (E1c), which adds a common term to every scenario. Since the headline conclusion — surface nucleation as columns that do not extend through the film — is a model-selection result, the reader cannot tell whether III vs IV, or III vs I at low fluence, is significant. The authors should weight χ² by the measured shot-to-shot and delay-to-delay intensity noise, give the number of degrees of freedom, and report uncertainties on the minima (e.g. bootstrap over pulse trains or delays, comparison of independent delay scans) together with a likelihood-ratio or information-criterion comparison for each scenario.

3. The 30 ± 10 nm lateral domain size, stated in the abstract, rests on a toy model, an apparently inconsistent broadening value and an unexamined alternative explanation. The size comes from matching an "additional broadening of the FM Bragg peak by 0.06 Å⁻¹" along q_z (p. 8; Methods p. 10–11), which the 1D model does not reproduce, to a five-site model of Poisson-coupled in-plane expansion (Ext. Data Fig. E4). First, 0.06 Å⁻¹ is hard to reconcile with Ext. Data Fig. E1c, where the full FM widths are only ≈0.012–0.03 Å⁻¹ (offset for clarity) and the data–model difference appears to be well below 0.01 Å⁻¹; the authors should state how and at which delays this number was obtained. Second, other sources of extra q_z broadening are not excluded: a strain or c-axis gradient within the FM volume, a distribution of column heights (hence of finite-size widths) or of D_FM across the footprint (concern 1), inhomogeneous strain at FM/AFM interfaces from clamping by the AFM matrix and substrate, grain-boundary strain, the excluded bottom 5.5 nm, the neglected FM thermal expansion and latent heat, inhomogeneous heating or transition temperatures (Ref. 42), and the mosaic grain-size distribution. Third, the inferred size nearly equals the known mosaic crystallite size (≈25 nm, Ref. 32): the FM "domains" may simply be grains that transform as units, in which case the coherent elastic coupling between neighbouring sites assumed in E4 (in-plane expansion propagating and being "erased" at the speed of sound) would not apply across grain boundaries, and nucleation would not be stochastic. Fourth, the Poisson ratio and elastic constants used are not given, and the ±10 nm is "estimated" rather than derived. Finally, "columnar" needs a defined column height: with D_FM ≈ 0.46 × 44 nm ≈ 20 nm, the domains are about as wide as they are tall. The authors should plot the excess broadening against delay with error bars alongside the predicted broadening for several l_FM (e.g. 10, 20, 30, 50/60, 100 nm), bound the competing mechanisms, discuss the grain-boundary alternative, and, if possible, use the in-plane (q_x, q_y) peak shape or its time dependence as an independent check. Otherwise the 30 nm figure should be presented as a model-dependent, order-of-magnitude estimate.

B. Should address

4. The scenario space is a small, discrete, hand-built set, so "scenario III" is a choice among five options rather than a measurement of D_FM(t) and A_FM(t) (Figs. 3, 4; Methods "Modelling of sonograms"). Each scenario fixes the D_FM–A_FM relation by hand — D_FM = 1 (II), D_FM = V*_FM from t = 0 (III, V), or D_FM = 1.3 V*_FM chosen as "an example" (IV) — and assumes a time-independent D_FM, a sharp phase boundary and columns of uniform height; no scenario lets columns grow into the depth over time. The most natural physical alternative, a graded depth profile set by the optical absorption profile (red shading in Fig. 2a), with V_FM(z) decreasing continuously with depth and column heights varying from site to site, is not tested. Near-surface nucleation is largely what exponential absorption in a 44 nm metal film predicts anyway; the non-trivial claim is the columnar, laterally heterogeneous early stage with a sharp depth cutoff, and it holds only if no reasonable unlisted heterogeneity fits equally well. The authors should fit a continuous parametrisation — e.g. D_FM(t) = D* f(t) with D* free and A_FM = V_FM/D_FM, free D_FM(t), A_FM(t) on a coarse time grid, or a depth-graded V_FM(z, t) tied to the local deposited energy — and report the best fit and its confidence region; at minimum, add a graded absorption-driven scenario and a scenario III variant with a distribution of column heights.

5. The robustness of the ranking to neglected physics and to fixed or tuned model inputs is asserted, not shown. The average FM strain amplitude and timing are what discriminate D_FM (p. 5), yet the model neglects latent heat, the FM-specific electronic heat capacity and electron–phonon coupling, the different (reduced) thermal expansion of the FM phase and the band-structure change (p. 10). Each acts on the stress in exactly the near-surface FM region, and at V_FM ≈ 0.46 latent heat is a sizeable fraction of the deposited energy. Further fixed or tuned inputs act on the same observables: the freely "optimised" optical penetration depth (p. 9), the exclusion of the bottom 5.5 nm "for an overall better agreement", the 0.6 % volumetric FM expansion and the Grüneisen parameters. The claim that the identification "does not depend decisively on the details of the shape of the strain pulse" needs demonstration. The authors should state which parameters were free in each scenario, give the fitted penetration depth with uncertainty and compare it with ellipsometry for FeRh at 800 nm and 25° incidence, and show that III still beats I and IV when each input is varied within plausible bounds (latent heat from calorimetry, FM thermal expansion from Ref. 32, the dead layer removed or varied, etc.).

6. The volume-fraction input and the nucleation kinetics are fixed rather than fitted, although the data deviate from them in the decisive window (Eq. 3; Ext. Data Fig. E1a). V_FM(t) is taken from integrated AFM/FM intensity ratios with τ = 8 ps from the literature, applied in every scenario at all fluences (p. 5). In E1a the FM intensity overshoots to ≈0.4 near 5 ps and dips before rising again, and the AFM intensity falls faster than the single exponential — precisely within the < 10 ps window that drives the scenario choice. The intensity-ratio method also assumes equal structure factors and Debye–Waller factors for both phases at all delays, ignores dynamical/extinction differences between a thin surface layer and a full film, and assumes transient strain does not move intensity out of the integration window. Since V*_FM sets D_FM in scenario III, these assumptions should be justified and their uncertainty propagated. The authors should let τ (and possibly a delayed onset) vary per scenario and fluence, report whether the ranking changes, and explain the overshoot if it is an extinction or dynamical-diffraction effect.

7. It is unclear which delays actually constrain D_FM, and whether the method resolves nucleation itself. The strain pulse crosses 44 nm in ≈8–9 ps, comparable to τ = 8 ps, and the transit through a ≈20 nm FM layer takes ≈4 ps. The key discriminating snapshot (Fig. 4a–c) is at t = 9 ps, when V_FM has already reached ≈67 % of its final value, and D_FM is scanned only at that fixed delay. The method thus characterises the heterogeneity after most of the conversion, while the text promises D_FM(t) and A_FM(t) "through the non-equilibrium pathway" (p. 3). The authors should show the residual against D_FM (χ²_t as in Fig. 4c) at all or several delays, to check whether the preference for III comes from a narrow window around the strain-pulse exit, state the resulting limits on resolving "transient" heterogeneity before ≈5 ps, and either extract a time-resolved D_FM(t) or narrow the wording ("nucleates at the surface", "directly visualising").

8. Sample modification and cumulative heating under burst-mode excitation are not ruled out (Methods, pp. 9–10). The sample is pumped at 56.25 kHz within 10 Hz trains at up to 11.7 mJ cm⁻², and the authors themselves attribute the non-contributing bottom 5.5 nm to "interface diffusion of FeRh and W … due to too strong laser excitations at a high-repetition rate", i.e. irreversible change during data collection. Further, FeRh is hysteretic: the base temperature may rise along the train (a baseline nearer 370 K would change V*_FM, the threshold and the depth profile), and some FM phase may persist between pulses 17.8 µs apart. The thermoelastic calibration from Ref. 26 predates the damage but is reused, and the pumped data are scaled to an unpumped reference that may itself drift. The authors should show the unpumped (002) profile and pre-time-zero sonogram at the start and end of the series and along the pulse train (no FM peak, unchanged AFM position), report any dependence on pulse index, state when the damage occurred relative to the data shown, whether the fluence series (E2, E3) came from fresh or exposed spots and in what order, and give independent evidence for the dead layer (static XRD/XRR, M(T) or TEM before and after the beamtime), with fits shown with and without the exclusion.

10. The method's depth sensitivity is not validated on a case with a known answer. All conclusions come from a single 44 nm film, and no sample or geometry was measured where the outcome is known in advance — e.g. a film thinner than the penetration depth (uniform-depth transformation, scenarios collapsing) or a different thickness where D_FM and Δt_phase should change predictably (Eq. 1). Because the technique itself is presented as a main contribution, such a benchmark matters; a second thickness would also test whether D_FM tracks the optical excitation depth rather than the film thickness. If new data cannot be obtained, the authors should at least run the full analysis on synthetic sonograms with realistic noise generated from known scenarios (I, III, IV, and graded and mixed cases) and show that the method recovers the input without confusing scenarios. A dedicated transducer or the proposed two-colour double-pulse scheme would further separate the driver of the transition from the strain marker.

11. There is no reference measurement under matched conditions to check the strain model's calibration in this experiment. 12. The in-plane expansion used to extract the domain size is left out of the model used to choose the scenario (pp. 8, 10). The authors argue that laterally heterogeneous nucleation unlocks in-plane expansion, which via the Poisson effect changes the out-of-plane lattice constant of FM domains, yet scenarios II–V are simulated with a purely 1D model, and discrimination relies mainly on the FM peak position and AFM peak width at ≈9 ps. Ext. Data Fig. E4b shows in-plane strains up to 0.4 %, comparable to the 0.6 % transition strain, so the Poisson-induced shifts are not negligible for laterally heterogeneous scenarios and could change the ranking. The authors should include this effect at least approximately in the scenario simulations, or show it does not affect the ranking, so that scenario selection and domain sizing use the same physics.

13. The interpretation of the AFM peak narrowing lies outside what the model contains (p. 7).
