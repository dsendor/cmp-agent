A. Must address

1. **The inverse Thiele model leaves out pinning, yet the data show that pinning acts during the relaxation, so the attribution of the recoil to skyrmion–skyrmion scattering is not established.** Eq. (1), p. 10 and the Methods (p. 18) treat the post-pulse motion of the L and R skyrmions as "governed solely by skyrmion–skyrmion interactions and dissipation". Yet p. 9 attributes the restoring forces partly to "the surrounding pinning landscape", and the paper's own data point to pinning wells. (i) The "immobile" class I skyrmions move about 20–25 nm during the negative pulse and then recoil with large amplitude (Fig. 2c; Table 1: 17.5 and 20.1 nm, against 12.8 and 9.1 nm for class M; the Fig. 2 caption says I shows "the strongest recoil"). This is the signature of skyrmions bound elastically to local pinning sites, and if the I skyrmions move they cannot serve as fixed scattering centres or fixed boundary conditions. (ii) In Fig. 3a, R1 relaxes to different final x positions after +Jx and −Jx (about 1.085 and 1.11 µm), so its rest position depends on pulse history. (iii) The predicted Thiele trajectories settle about 10–20 nm away from the measured ones in both x and y (Fig. 3a: −Jx branch predicted near 1.125 µm against data near 1.11 µm; y near 0.21 µm against data near 0.19 µm), a persistent force imbalance the model does not capture. If local wells exert restoring forces on L and R skyrmions, the fit folds those forces into a* and b*, which undermines the headline claim that the interaction potential was measured. Edge transients (Oersted field or field-like torque at the pulse edges) are a further mechanism not discussed. The authors should add a pinning term to the Thiele model (for example a harmonic or Gaussian well centred on each skyrmion's pre-pulse position, with fitted stiffness), estimate the stiffness independently from the class I recoil, report how a*, b* and the loss change and show final-position residuals with and without the term, and test whether the I-class relaxation is consistent with the same V(r) given those skyrmions' own neighbours. A control in which the scattering partner is absent (an isolated skyrmion or a sparse region under identical pulses) would separate interaction-driven recoil from pinning-well relaxation, since a scattering origin predicts much weaker recoil there. Varying pulse amplitude and duration at a fixed lattice configuration and reporting how amplitude and τ_relax scale would also help.

2. **Transient Joule heating is not characterised, and both the 3–20 ns recoil and the high-drive depinning could partly be thermal.** The device is a 25-repeat Pt/Ir-based stack, roughly 30 nm thick, on a 100 nm SiN membrane, driven with 5 ns pulses at 2.8 × 10^11 A/m² and 2.5 ns pulses at up to 1.2 × 10^12 A/m². Heating during the pulse changes Ms, anisotropy, DMI and interlayer coupling, and with them skyrmion size and equilibrium spacing; the membrane cools on nanosecond to tens-of-nanosecond timescales, overlapping the reported relaxation times. A recoil driven by equilibrium positions and sizes shifting back during cooling would also be deterministic, opposite to the drive and roughly exponential, and would not probe the room-temperature interaction potential. Heating also lowers pinning and could contribute to the depinning attributed to SOT (p. 13), bears on the linearity of v(J), and conflicts with the statement that the results are "free from stochastic thermal activation" (p. 10); the same group reports enhanced thermal diffusion in similar SyAFMs (ref. 20). The only mitigation described (1 µs delay, 10 mbar He; Methods) addresses cumulative, not transient, heating. The authors should estimate the peak temperature rise during and after each pulse at both current densities, from the device resistance measured during the pulse (transmitted-pulse amplitude) or from a thermal model of the stack on the membrane, together with the expected cooling time; report skyrmion diameter and contrast through the relaxation window and show that skyrmion size before and after the pulse is the same; and show that the relaxation times do not scale with pulse energy as a thermal process would, for example by varying pulse length at fixed J.

3. **The extracted interaction parameters and the exponential form are not shown to be constrained by the data.** Eq. (1) imposes F/D = (1 m/s)·exp(−(r − b)/a), in which b only sets the prefactor through exp(b/a), so a and b are strongly correlated. The separations actually sampled (Fig. 3b, lower panel) run from about 130 to 400 nm, with most weight near 200 and 350 nm, so the decay length a ≈ 30–40 nm is extracted over 4–10 decay lengths, and the steep repulsive part below about 150 nm in Fig. 3b is an extrapolation. The paper does not say which and how many skyrmions, trajectories and time points enter the loss, the number of data points against free parameters, how long the "early-time relaxation window" is and whether a* and b* are stable when it changes, the goodness of fit, or the noise model behind the "68% confidence intervals". Only R1 is shown (Fig. 3a). Without a model comparison, "reveals an exponential decay" is an assumption rather than a result. The authors should show the full two-dimensional loss landscape L(a, b) with the a–b correlation and the full covariance; report fit-window dependence; show fits for skyrmions other than R1 and a leave-one-out or bootstrap estimate of the parameter spread; compare losses for alternative forms (the modified-Bessel K1(r/λ) form expected from exchange-mediated texture overlap, a power law, or exponential plus a constant); restrict the plotted potential and its band to the sampled r interval, marking the rest as extrapolation; and validate the whole pipeline on simulated trajectories with a known potential, including pinning and deformation.

4. **The agreement with micromagnetics is overstated, the abstract quotes the simulated rather than the measured range, and the independence of the simulation is not shown.** The Methods (p. 18) give the experimental a* = (38 ± 5) nm and b* = (236 ± 9) nm; the simulation gives a* = (28 ± 4) nm and b* = (238 ± 9) nm (p. 10). The two values of a* differ by about 1.6 combined standard deviations, and Fig. 3b shows the bands overlapping only near r ≈ 200–250 nm and diverging below about 180 nm. Yet the abstract states "a range of 30 nm, in full agreement with micromagnetic simulations", and p. 15 says "excellent agreement"; the 30 nm is the simulated value. Moreover, the paper says only that the simulated system "closely match[es]" the experiment and does not state which parameters (A, D_DMI, K_u, interlayer exchange, Ms per sublattice, α) were measured and which were tuned, or whether the simulated potential was extracted with the same inverse procedure. If D_DMI or the interlayer exchange were tuned to the observed lattice spacing or skyrmion size, the agreement in b* (≈ the lattice spacing) is largely built in. The authors should quote the experimental a* in the abstract, state whether the 68% intervals overlap and describe the agreement accordingly, and list every micromagnetic parameter with its source (SQUID, spin-flop field, BLS or domain-spacing DMI), ideally fixing all of them from measurements before simulating.

B. Should address

5. **The deterministic interpretation of the averaged trajectories is assumed, not checked.** The analysis assumes each pump–probe frame shows one trajectory repeated identically over billions of cycles (p. 4: "any non-reproducible skyrmion motion will average out"; p. 6: "highly reproducible across all cycles, confirming their non-stochastic origin"; p. 10). But stochastic events do not vanish in a stroboscopic average: if a skyrmion depins, or relaxes from a metastable position, at a random time after the pulse in each cycle, the averaged contrast shifts its centroid smoothly and roughly exponentially while the texture blurs, which would mimic the recoil of Fig. 2c,d and Table 1 and be fitted by a deterministic Thiele model as an average over distinct pathways. Repeatability of the average does not prove single-event determinism, especially in a room-temperature SyAFM with enhanced thermal diffusion (ref. 20). The authors should quantify skyrmion contrast amplitude and apparent diameter or edge width frame by frame through the pulse and relaxation window for the M, L, R and I classes, compared with static images and with skyrmions far from any motion; compare static images taken before and after the pump–probe run; report any skyrmions whose contrast fades during the cycle; compare runs with different accumulation times or beamtimes if available; and show that any blurring stays within what the 20 nm resolution and tracking noise allow. This is needed to support "deterministic control ... even in the incoherent flow regime".

6. **The rigid-particle Thiele model is applied to a skyrmion the authors describe as deforming.** On p. 9 the authors say R1 shows "a breakdown of the rigid-particle approximation, as asymmetric constraints induce spin structure deformation", yet R1 is the example used to demonstrate the rigid-particle fit (Fig. 3a). Relaxation of shape (breathing, elongation or compression recovering) moves the contrast centroid without any centre-of-mass interaction, and internal-mode relaxation in a SyAFM could fall within the observed 3–20 ns window. The authors should quantify aspect ratio and area versus time during relaxation for the L and R skyrmions used in the inversion, and show that centroid motion is not dominated by shape recovery, or restrict the fit to skyrmions with negligible deformation.

7. **The number of devices, regions and lattice configurations is not stated, and no result is shown to be reproduced.** The paper appears to rest on one 5 µm × 2 µm strip, one roughly 1 µm field of view (Fig. 2a) and about 25 skyrmions (Fig. 4), with the potential extracted from L and R skyrmions in that one field of view. It does not say whether the incoherent regime, the recoil time constants, the crossover or a* and b* recur in another device, another region of the same strip, or after re-nucleating the lattice with the bipolar pulse protocol, so the extracted potential may be specific to one local pinning configuration. The authors should state the sample statistics and show at least one independent repeat of the inversion, with the spread of a* and b* across skyrmions or regions.

8. **The Co L3 edge does not isolate sublattice B, which weakens the sublattice-resolved claims.** Sublattice A is FCB(0.4)/CFB(0.2) (Fig. 1a) and contains Co, so the Co-edge XMCD signal is the Co moment of B minus that of A; only the Fe edge isolates one sublattice. A relative displacement of the two sublattices would partly cancel in the Co channel rather than appear as a shift. This bears on the "sublattice-resolved" framing (p. 4), the synchronized motion and "robust sublattice locking" (pp. 6–7, Fig. S3), and "no delay or phase shift between sublattices" (p. 13). The authors should give the Co content and Co moment fraction of each sublattice (thickness × concentration, XAS) or use a difference analysis, estimate the cross-contamination, and state what sublattice separation would be detectable given it and the 20 nm resolution.

9. **The "no inertia" and "no sublattice delay" conclusions are not compared with what the measurement can resolve or with what theory predicts.** The Fe and Co movies are recorded sequentially in separate runs (p. 6), and the paper does not say how a common time zero and spatial registration are established between them, or with what accuracy. The 2.5 ns pulse is sampled by only a few frames at about 1 ns spacing (Fig. 4c,d), so a lag below roughly 0.5–1 ns or a separation below about 20 nm would not be seen; the velocities in Fig. 4d are finite differences. "Tracking the voltage" compares against the applied voltage, not the current in the strip, and reflections or RC filtering can shift the two. The statement "defying theoretical predictions" (p. 13) needs the delay and separation that ref. 43 predicts for this stack's interlayer coupling (using the 650 mT spin-flop and 1.8 T saturation fields given on p. 16); if the prediction is sub-nanosecond or a few nanometres, there is no contradiction. The authors should fit the displacement or v(t) against the drive with a damped-mass or first-order-response model, quote an upper bound on effective mass or inertial time, state the predicted values, measure the transmitted current waveform, and rephrase the claims as upper bounds.

11. **Magnetization compensation is stated but inconsistent with the given parameters, and angular-momentum compensation is not measured.** The Methods (p. 16) give Ms = 0.85 × 10^6 A/m "for each ferromagnetic sublattice", while sublattice A is 0.6 nm (FCB 0.4 + CFB 0.2) and sublattice B 1.1 nm (CB) thick (Fig. 1a). Equal Ms would then give Ms·t differing by nearly a factor of two, which is not compensated. The suppressed skyrmion Hall angle, the angular-momentum argument (p. 15), and the neglect of the gyrotropic term in the inversion (p. 10) all rely on compensation. The authors should give Ms·t of each sublattice and the net remanent moment from the SQUID data (Fig. S1), correct the statement, and estimate the residual gyrotropic term G_eff/D, either including it in the inversion or showing it is negligible for the y-motion seen in Fig. 3a.
