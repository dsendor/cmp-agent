## A. Must address

1. **The mechanism by which a time-reversal-symmetric tip gives bias-reversing spin selectivity is asserted, not shown (Figs. 1c–e, 5d, 5l; p. 3; Discussion p. 7).** The paper argues that electrons "injected into or launched from" the tip travel along the helical edge in opposite directions and so carry opposite spins. At any energy, however, a helical edge contains both counter-propagating, oppositely spin-polarized branches (+k/↑ and −k/↓), and a time-reversal-invariant tip has a spin-degenerate DOS. In a Bardeen/Tersoff–Hamann picture, and in linear-response elastic tunneling through a single channel, such a tip gives no spin contrast. Two-terminal linear-response conductance also obeys Onsager reciprocity, G(M) = G(−M), so a signal that depends on the sign of the sample spin and reverses with bias must be a nonlinear, non-equilibrium effect, with a definite scaling with bias and current. Spin selectivity can only come from a geometry-dependent current path (which end of the edge the apex sits on, asymmetric coupling to the two branches or drains), as proposed for SmB6 nanowires (ref. 62). The authors concede this implicitly: two branches or hinges "would cancel each other", and P "varies among different tips, possibly due to the specific geometry of the tip apex". The filter is therefore not "topology-guaranteed"; its size and sign are set by an uncontrolled apex geometry. This sharpens a further puzzle: if the sign depended on which hinge or branch dominates, it should vary from tip to tip, and if the spin axis is set by each tip's crystal orientation (not controlled relative to the sample axes) it should also vary, yet all three tips in Fig. 5l show the same sign pattern (positive at negative energy, negative at positive energy). Such consistency is more naturally expected from a sample-borne, spin-independent bias-asymmetric anisotropy. Without a model the reader cannot judge whether ±12% polarization at 10–40 mV and ~1 nA through a GΩ junction is plausible, and every spin assignment in Figs. 2g–h and 5 depends on this mechanism. The authors should: (a) give a quantitative tunneling model (e.g. a Landauer/Keldysh or tight-binding junction calculation of a point contact coupled to a helical channel with asymmetric drains) that respects reciprocity, specifies the apex geometry, the number of edge channels contributing and how current divides along hinges, and predicts the sign, size and bias odd-ness of P(V); (b) test its predictions with P versus setpoint current and tip height at fixed bias, and with P(V) measured continuously through V = 0 rather than only at |V| ≥ 5–10 meV; (c) state the tip's spin quantization axis (S_z of Bi4Br4, set by the strip orientation on the PtIr post) relative to the KV2Se2O axes and moments for each tip; (d) explain the tip-to-tip sign consistency; and ideally (e) measure with tips of known, different crystallographic orientation, where the model predicts a change of sign or amplitude.

2. **Spin-independent explanations of the bias-reversing qx/qy QPI anisotropy are not excluded, and the PtIr control is not matched (Fig. 5b–c, 5f–l; pp. 6–7; Ext. Data Fig. 10).** The rotation of the dominant QPI wavevector from qx to qy on reversing bias is presented as the "smoking gun" for d-wave spin splitting, and the paper states that it "decisively rules out" artifacts. Several non-spin sources of a bias-odd x/y contrast remain. (i) By the authors' own account (Fig. 3g, p. 5) the √2×√2 density wave "potentially breaks the original local rotational symmetry" and keeps only M_1-10, and there are 2×1 K domains, so the sample is C2. A C2, particle–hole-asymmetric electronic structure can give qx ≠ qy contrast that reverses between filled and empty states, particularly across the ~20 meV gap in which the ±10/±15 meV maps sit, because the Vx- and Vy-derived bands and the density-wave gap edges (Fig. 4d) carry different weights above and below E_F; the arrow-shaped defects can add particle–hole-asymmetric scattering. (ii) The tip is cut from a quasi-1D crystal whose [Bi-Br]n chains have a definite in-plane direction; its orbital character is C2 and, given the strongly asymmetric Bi4Br4 DOS (Fig. 2b, f), differs above and below E_F, so orbital-selective matrix elements (or a multi-apex tip) can weight qx and qy differently at the two polarities. (iii) Vx defects scatter along x and Vy defects along y (Fig. 4a), so unequal numbers of the two types in a field of view bias I(qx)/I(qy) directly. (iv) Setpoint effects: all maps use V_s = 0.4 V, and spatially varying normalisation combined with x/y-anisotropic LDOS can imprint anisotropy differently at ±V. In addition, the quantity M(q,eV) = [I(q) − R_{π/2}I(q)]/I(Q_Bragg) is a B1g nematic order parameter; calling it a "magnetic contrast map" assumes the conclusion. The rotated domain of Ext. Data Fig. 10 shows that the anisotropy follows the sample rather than the tip, but a C2 charge/orbital or density-wave domain would rotate in the same way, so it does not separate spin from charge; this test is also not developed in the main text. The PtIr reference (Fig. 5b, c) is at ±15 meV while the BiBr data are at +10/−15 meV, does not appear to come from the same field of view, sample, cleave or domain, does not report Vx/Vy defect counts, and cannot address (ii) at all. The decisive control is already available: a BiBr tip can be switched in situ between its bulk-state and helical-edge-mode states (p. 4; Methods; Ext. Data Fig. 3e–f), and the bulk-state tip shares the apex geometry and orbital character but has no helical channel. The authors should show, on the same field of view and domain, at identical energies, setpoints, colour scales and analysis windows, M(q,eV) and the full energy dependence of P(E) with error bars for the helical-edge-mode tip, the bulk-state BiBr tip and a PtIr tip, together with the Vx and Vy defect counts in each field of view. Further discriminating tests: BiBr-tip QPI on a nonmagnetic but anisotropic reference surface (a C2 quasi-1D system, or the 2×1 K surface of KV2Se2O) showing no bias-odd anisotropy; BiBr-tip QPI on KV2Se2O above the ~100 K transition, where the spin contrast should vanish; and the Ext. Data Fig. 10 domain-rotation data in the main text with the same, unchanged tip shown on both domains and its spin axis fixed. Only if the non-spin tips give P ≈ 0 at all energies on the same area while the helical tip gives the reversal does Fig. 5 support the d-wave spin-splitting claim.

3. **The direction of the ordered moments is stated inconsistently, and this bears on both the Zeeman interpretation and whether the tip can sense the order at all (p. 5–6; Fig. 4d, g, h; Ext. Data Fig. 7; Methods p. 18).** The main text concludes that the opposite Zeeman shifts "reveal an in-plane AFM order", and Fig. 4d / Ext. Data Fig. 7 compare with "in-plane Néel AFM order". The Methods, explaining why only five of ten tips showed spin resolution, say that the edge-mode spin is "horizontal" (Fig. 1c) "whereas that for the AFM order in KV2Se2O is out-of-plane". These statements are incompatible. Moreover, with a perpendicular field (±7 T), in-plane collinear moments on Vx and Vy would cant identically toward the field and, to first order, would not give opposite energy shifts; opposite shifts point instead to an opposite out-of-plane component on the two sublattices, or to a sublattice-dependent g-tensor. Since the tip polarization measures the projection of the sample spin on the tip's quantization axis, the paper's own explanation of the failing tips implies that the succeeding tips should not see the order either; if the moments are instead in plane, the Zeeman result needs a different explanation. The authors should state a single moment direction with its evidence (neutron data, DFT magnetic anisotropy, field data, ideally adding in-plane field measurements), model the Zeeman shift for that spin structure, state each tip's spin axis, and show that the measured, finite P is consistent with the geometry of each tip's Bi4Br4 crystal axes relative to the sample Néel vector.

## B. Should address

4. **The Zeeman analysis of the subgap states does not establish a 1.13 μB V moment or opposite sublattice spins at the level claimed (Fig. 4g, h; p. 5).** A 0.9 meV energy difference between +7 T and −7 T gives an effective moment of about 1.1 μB, which is simply what a g = 2, S = 1/2 quasiparticle level would give. Converting it into a "local moment" assumes a free-spin Zeeman form with a known g factor; it reflects the spin of a defect-bound state (on sites neighbouring an assigned V vacancy), not the ordered V moment, and in a strongly exchange-coupled antiferromagnet a bound state's field shift does not measure a site moment, so the agreement with the neutron moment (ref. 67) is not evidence. Only one defect of each type is shown, at ±7 T only. The splitting is also comparable to thermal broadening at 4.8 K (3.5 k_BT ≈ 1.4–1.5 meV). The authors should: state whether these data were taken in the sub-kelvin Unisoku system, with which tip and at what effective electron temperature; give peak positions with fit uncertainties and how peaks were fitted; show a full field sweep (0, ±1, …, ±7 T) establishing linearity, the shift of each peak separately, absence of hysteresis and of field-induced tip changes; summarize in the main text the statistics over many Vx and Vy defects (sign and magnitude of the shift; Supplementary Note 5); and temper the comparison with the neutron moment.

6. **Whether the proposed structure is a d-wave altermagnet has not been established by a spin-group analysis, and the surface-versus-bulk scope is unclear (Fig. 3g, p. 5; Fig. 5e; Discussion p. 7).** The √2×√2 density wave splits each of Vx and Vy into inequivalent sites (Vxa/Vxb, Vya/Vyb), and the surface also carries a √2×√2 (or 2×1) K reconstruction that by itself lowers the symmetry. The paper does not determine the spins on the a/b sites. Depending on them, the supercell could contain a translation (or translation plus spin flip) or inversion that relates opposite spins, removing the altermagnetic splitting, or could make the two spin sublattices inequivalent, giving a ferrimagnet with a net moment. The paper only notes that one M_1-10 mirror survives, which "reveal[s] a modulation for a 2D d-wave altermagnetic order". Further, the Discussion concedes that neutron data favour G-type stacking (refs. 63, 67), for which the bulk is Kramers-degenerate, and that unit-cell steps could not be found; the STM signal would then reflect the top V2Se2O layer under its K reconstruction and charge transfer. The authors should give the spin space group of the combined density-wave, K-reconstruction and magnetic structure; state which a/b spin configurations are consistent with the data; include the supercell (and ideally a surface slab with the K overlayer) in the DFT/JDOS used for Fig. 5e, showing that d-wave X/Y splitting survives in the folded Brillouin zone and which bands the qx¹ and qy¹ vectors connect after folding; and state the title/abstract claims ("a layered d-wave altermagnet", "2D d-wave altermagnet") as a surface-layer result unless interlayer order can be addressed, discussing how representative the probed layer is.

8. **The Fourier-analysis pipeline may imprint or exaggerate the x/y anisotropy (Methods p. 19; Fig. 5g, i–l).** The FTs were "twofold symmetrized ... along qx or qy", drift-corrected with Lawler–Fujita, rotated and cropped, then compared with their π/2-rotated copies. Each step can create or enhance an x/y difference, for example through imperfect correction of a slightly non-square lattice or the choice of symmetrization axis. The integration windows were "adjusted accordingly at different energies", and the normalization uses Bragg peaks from the K reconstruction, whose intensities may be bias dependent and anisotropic. The authors should confirm that no operation mixes qx and qy before M and P are computed; show raw, unsymmetrized FTs at ±V; fix the windows by an independent criterion (e.g. the DFT dispersion), give their positions and sizes at each energy relative to the q-resolution, and show that P is robust to window size and placement; report the x/y ratio of the Bragg intensities and try alternative normalizations (total power, or a setpoint-effect-corrected L(r) map); and show M(q,eV) from PtIr and Bi4Br4 data processed identically on the same field of view.

9. **The Fe1.02Te benchmark does not separate spin contrast from charge or orbital contrast (Fig. 2g, h; p. 4).** This is the only test of spin sensitivity on a system with a known spin structure. A stripe contrast that reverses between +50 and −50 mV can arise from a spin-averaged LDOS with particle–hole asymmetry; contrast inversion between filled- and empty-state images is common for charge modulations and density waves, so bias reversal alone is not "the hallmark for the tunneling of helical states". Bicollinear Fe1+yTe also has a monoclinic distortion and accompanying charge/orbital modulation at the same wavevector. Moreover, Enayat et al. (ref. 51) made spin-polarized tips precisely by picking up excess Fe from Fe1+yTe, so a BiBr tip scanning Fe1.02Te could become a conventional magnetic-cluster tip. The PtIr comparison is relegated to Supplementary Note 3 and appears to address topography only, not bias-dependent dI/dV maps. The authors should show, on the same Fe1.02Te area, PtIr and bulk-state BiBr dI/dV maps at ±50 mV and other energies demonstrating absence of the stripe contrast and its reversal; the full energy dependence P(V) of the contrast; that the contrast is unchanged under reversal of an applied field (a remanent cluster tip would respond); and ideally a test only spin can explain (twin- or field-switched domain dependence, magnetic adatoms of known moment, or a Cr/Fe-coated tip on the same sample). They should also state whether any tip used on KV2Se2O had previously been used on FeTe.

11. **The evidence that tunneling occurs through helical edge modes at the apex is indirect (Fig. 2e, f; Ext. Data Fig. 3; Methods p. 17).** The "helical-edge-mode" tip state is identified by a zero-bias suppression fitted to a Tomonaga–Luttinger form. Power-law zero-bias anomalies also arise from dynamical Coulomb blockade in a resistive environment, plausible for a semiconducting strip bonded with FIB-deposited Pt at the PtIr/Bi4Br4/Pt-pad junction; FIB milling also amorphizes and Ga-implants surface layers to tens of nm, comparable to the 44 nm apex. Switching between "bulk" and "edge" states by voltage pulses could reflect apex rearrangement rather than access to a topological hinge, and the paper describes some hinge modes as "slightly gapped". The quoted expression ρ ∝ T^α cosh(ε/2k_BT) is not the standard finite-temperature TLL form (which includes a |Γ(...)|² factor) and should be corrected. The authors should give fitted α values with uncertainties, the temperature dependence of the anomaly (which distinguishes TLL from environmental blockade), a comparison with α from edge modes in bulk Bi4Br4, TEM/EDS of an apex if available, and a discussion of what fixes the hinge location relative to the milled apex and why the apex hosts a gapless hinge.
