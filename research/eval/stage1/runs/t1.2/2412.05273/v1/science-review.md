A. Must address

1. **The QQHE identification is not tested quantitatively.** The claims "our observations serve to confirm the existence of QQHE in a 3D gapless Dirac semimetal" (p. 3) and "we have observed evidence of the 3D QQHE" (Summary) rest on one feature: a change of slope in ρxy at H_S (H_S ≈ 3.5 T in Sample C, Fig. 2e; Fig. 3e–h), together with a coincident shoulder or lump in ρxx (near 5.7 T in Sample D, Fig. 3d). Nothing in the paper ties these features quantitatively to Landau-level physics in these particular samples. A rough estimate for Sample C (n = 5.8×10^16 cm^-3, fourfold degeneracy, isotropic Fermi surface) gives k_F ≈ 1×10^8 m^-1 and an SdH frequency of about 3 T. The kink therefore sits about where the last Landau-level crossing, i.e. the onset of the quantum limit, would be expected. That is consistent with the claim, but it does not separate a QQHE from ordinary quantum-limit physics or from a generic nonlinearity in the Hall response. In ZrTe5 and HfTe5 (Refs. 7, 10, 15) the QQHE was established by comparing plateau values with (h/e²)(λ_F,z/2), equivalently σxy ≈ (e²/h)(k_F,z/π), with k_F taken from quantum oscillations, and by checking that the plateau fields track the last Landau-level crossing. The authors should: (i) compare the ρxy (or σxy) value at the step with the quasi-quantized value expected from each sample's density and Fermi-surface anisotropy; (ii) plot H_S, or the lump field, against n for Samples A–D, with uncertainties, and compare with the last-crossing or quantum-limit field from the k·p model in Methods at each sample's own E_F (for an isotropic Dirac pocket H_S should scale roughly as n^(2/3); the C-to-D field ratio of about 1.6, against (n_D/n_C)^(2/3) ≈ 1.4, suggests the check is easy and informative); (iii) overlay the k·p ρxy and ρxx computed at each sample's E_F on its data, or at least tabulate predicted against observed feature fields. Fig. 1b is computed only for E_F = 20 meV and is never compared with any measured trace, not even Sample A (E_F = 20.4 meV), the obvious test. (iv) Analyse the quantum oscillations in Sample E (Fig. 2a–c), and any in Sample D, to quote the SdH frequency and fix k_F independently of the Hall slope. Without these tests the supportable claim is "a quantum-limit feature consistent with QQHE".

2. **The single-carrier analysis that sets n, E_F and eV0/E_F conflicts with the high-field Hall data, and parallel conduction is not excluded.** Methods take n from the ρxy slope below 1 T, and Fig. 1e converts this n to E_F using a single Dirac pocket. The high-field Hall slopes are much smaller than that n implies. At 14 T, H/(ne) predicts about −300 mΩ cm for Sample A (n = 2.9×10^16 cm^-3), but Fig. 3e shows about −100. The corresponding figures are about −240 predicted against −95 measured for B, about −150 against −95 for C (Figs. 2e, 3g), and about −94 against −75 for D. The mismatch grows as n falls, to roughly a factor of three for Sample A. In addition, μB ≈ 1 at 1 T, so the "low-field" slope is not in the weak-field limit. This points to field-dependent or multiple conduction channels: surface or Weyl-orbit states, conduction through the ZnxTe1−x buffer, the GaAs interface, a minority hole or second carrier, density inhomogeneity, or a field-induced change in density. The paper dismisses multiband transport only qualitatively (p. 3: "nonmonotonicity in the Hall effect of multiband models is typically smooth"). Yet E_F (Fig. 1e), eV0/E_F (Figs. 1d, 3j), the quantum-limit field scales and the "strong disorder" classification all assume one well-defined carrier density, and a parallel channel would also undermine the Hall kink as evidence for concern 1. The authors should fit ρxx(H) and ρxy(H) jointly with two-carrier (or multicarrier) models for Samples A–D and report the result; compare low- and high-field Hall densities for every sample; compare n with the quantum-oscillation frequency where oscillations are visible (Sample E, Fig. 2a); explain why the high-field Hall response departs from H/(ne) if a single pocket is in the quantum limit; and rule out buffer conduction directly, for example with a Hall measurement on a buffer-only sample.

3. **The Coulomb-disorder attribution rests on a calculated disorder strength that is not measured, and the paper's own regime assignment is inconsistent with its figures.** eV0 in Figs. 1d and 3j is computed from Skinner's theory (Ref. 9) with inputs (charged-impurity density N_imp, dielectric constant, Fermi velocity) that appear only in the Supplementary Information, if at all. The films are tuned by changing defect concentrations ("tune the defect concentrations", abstract), yet no defect characterization is presented. N_imp is therefore unlikely to be the same in every sample, and the very low n implies strong compensation: Fig. 1c mentions ~10^18 cm^-3 "in some films", and eV0 depends strongly on N_imp. The phrase "as we have hypothesized and shown" (p. 6) is therefore not backed by a measurement. The regime assignment is also internally inconsistent. The text places Samples A–C in the strong-disorder limit (eV0 > E_F) and fits only them with the strong-disorder magnetoresistance forms, but in Fig. 3j and Fig. 1d only Sample A is clearly above eV0/E_F = 1. Sample B lies on the line, and Sample C (E_F = 27.4 meV, eV0 ≈ 21 meV, ratio about 0.7–0.8) is on the weak side, alongside D and E (about 0.6 and 0.35), which are called weak. The mobilities do not follow the proposed disorder ordering either (Fig. 1e): B 0.67, A 1.10, D 1.22, C 1.52, E 2.59 ×10^4 cm²/Vs. If scattering from charged defects that screen more weakly as n drops dominated, mobility should fall systematically with n. Since the crossover the paper claims to tune through is model-dependent, the authors should: state N_imp, the dielectric constant and v_F for each sample, explain how N_imp was estimated, and show the spread from plausible ranges as uncertainty bands on eV0 in Figs. 1d and 3j; either treat Sample C consistently (as weak, like D) or justify fitting it with the strong-limit form; discuss the non-monotonic mobility; and give an independent measure of disorder. Possible measures include the Dingle temperature or the quantum-to-transport lifetime ratio from the oscillations in Samples E and D (a recognized fingerprint of long-range Coulomb disorder), temperature-dependent mobility analysis, defect or compensation estimates from growth stoichiometry or structural characterization, or local probes such as STM. A gated device on a single film, as the Discussion itself proposes, would vary E_F at fixed defect density and remove the central confound of comparing samples with one another.

B. Should address

4. **The three-dimensional, bulk origin of the features is not established for films 200–560 nm thick.** The thickness of each sample is not stated (Methods give only the range). With v_F ~ 0.5–1×10^6 m/s, the confinement subband spacing ħv_Fπ/d for d = 200 nm is about 5–10 meV, comparable to the quoted E_F of 20–34 meV. The Fermi wavelength 2π/k_F is a few tens of nm, so a 200 nm film is only a few wavelengths thick and only a few subbands may be occupied in the lowest-density films. Quasi-2D subband Landau levels would produce Hall steps by a different mechanism, and surface-state and Weyl-orbit transport is known to give quantized Hall features in Cd3As2 films of this thickness (Refs. 30, 31). Both the "3D QQHE" interpretation and the use of a 3D density of states for E_F (Fig. 1e) assume bulk states. The text says that "magnetotransport at different fixed magnetic field angles are consistent with a 3D Fermi surface" (p. 4), but the data are not shown. The authors should give each sample's thickness and an estimate of the subband spacing; show the angle-dependent ρxy and ρxx, at minimum the angle dependence of H_S, demonstrating that the features scale with total field rather than its perpendicular component; and test whether H_S and the kink amplitude depend on thickness, ideally by comparing two films of similar n and different thickness.

5. **The Eq. 1 decomposition has many adjustable elements, and the QQHE and disorder components are not uniquely separated.** For each of Samples A–C, α, β, γ and δ are free (with ρ0 subtracted), and they are fitted to smooth monotonic curves (Fig. 3a–c, gray), so "good agreement" is weak evidence. The crossover field H_c = 9.6 T and width w = 1.5 T are fixed globally by hand with no stated justification, even though in Rodionov et al. (Ref. 29) the H² → H log(δ√H) crossover field depends on disorder strength and E_F. A sample-independent H_c is thus inconsistent with a density series in which disorder strength is said to vary. In Fig. 3a–d α "is chosen to match the size of the lump-like feature in ρxx" (p. 5), so amplitude agreement holds by construction, and α and β, which both contribute below H_c, are likely correlated. Only one field polarity is fit. The decrease of β and γ with n in Fig. 3i rests on three samples with no error bars. It is not separated from the trivial dependence of classical magnetoresistance on mobility, which is itself non-monotonic. δ ≈ 0.32 T^-1/2 is given without uncertainty. The authors should report parameter uncertainties and correlations (in the main text or an SI table) and show residuals; show fits with H_c and w free for each sample or tied to theory; show that the peak positions of αH∂Hρxy match the ρxx features independently of α; test whether a simpler form, such as a power law plus αH∂Hρxy, fits equally well; and compare the fitted β, γ and δ in magnitude with Rodionov's predictions for each sample's n, v_F, dielectric constant and computed eV0, rather than only their trend with n.

6. **Alternative origins of the super-quadratic ρxx background are not tested.** The low-field H² term is generic: classical orbital, two-carrier and Parish–Littlewood mobility-fluctuation magnetoresistance all give it, and inhomogeneity or two-band magnetoresistance can also produce a quadratic-to-linear or super-quadratic crossover. Classical inhomogeneity magnetoresistance from density puddles shares the physical ingredient but predicts different field and temperature scaling from Rodionov's theory. Magnetic freeze-out, or field-induced metal–insulator behaviour in the ultra-quantum limit, known in low-density ZrTe5 (Refs. 7, 24), would also grow sharply as n falls and predicts an activated temperature dependence of the high-field ρxx. The guiding-center diffusion mechanism of Ref. 17 is another candidate. Fig. 2d also needs discussion: the field magnetoresistance of Sample C appears to grow with temperature (the 80 K trace is highest), which is unusual for disorder-limited quantum-limit magnetoresistance and may point to a thermally activated parallel channel. The authors should fit at least one competing background model with the same number of parameters and compare goodness of fit, and use the temperature dependence of the background (data to 80 K exist for Sample C) to discriminate between mechanisms.

7. **Field symmetrization and contact mixing are not described.** Methods do not say whether ρxx was symmetrized and ρxy antisymmetrized in field, and Fig. 3a–d shows measured data only for H < 0, with fits for H > 0. In a Hall bar, contact misalignment mixes ρxy into ρxx, which can produce asymmetric lumps of the kind attributed to the QQHE. Because ρxx has a sharp shoulder at H_S, admixture of ρxx into ρxy would equally produce a correlated kink in ρxy, which is exactly the coincidence used as evidence (Fig. 2d,e). The authors should state the symmetrization procedure and show raw data for both field polarities.

8. **The relation ρxx ∝ H∂Hρxy is not specific to the QQHE.** The QQHE term αH∂Hρxy in Eq. 1 (Fig. 3a–d light traces) uses the measured ρxy, which itself contains the non-QQHE background. The empirical relation also holds for ordinary SdH oscillations in 2D and 3D, and more generally in inhomogeneous conductors, where it follows from density fluctuations (Simon–Halperin-type arguments). The paper itself describes the QQHE as an extension of quantum oscillations (Discussion). That the relation works well for Sample D (Fig. 3d) is therefore not independent evidence for the QQHE. The authors should say what in this decomposition is unique to Landau-level physics. They should also explain the values α takes (0.17–0.35), for example by relating them to density inhomogeneity, and check whether α correlates with the computed disorder.
Raised by: r2, r3

9. **Temperature dependence is not analysed, and the measurement temperatures do not match.** The densities, E_F and Fig. 2 are at 2 K, while the decomposition in Fig. 3 uses 5 K data, and the fits are shown at only one temperature. The text says remnants of the H_S feature survive to 80 K (p. 3; Fig. 2d–f), but it never checks whether that is consistent with the Landau-level spacing at H_S compared with thermal and disorder broadening. Persistence to 80 K could point to a classical origin. A Landau-quantization feature should be thermally damped on the scale of the Landau-level spacing, whereas the disorder background should change through screening and mobility. The authors should either use matched temperatures or show that n, ρ0 and the features do not change between 2 and 5 K. They should also fit Eq. 1 at several temperatures and show that α(T) follows the expected thermal damping while β and γ behave as the disorder assignment requires.

10. **The Fermi energies in Fig. 1e are not justified.** For a fourfold-degenerate linear Dirac band E_F ∝ n^(1/3), but the tabulated values rise faster. Scaling from Sample A predicts about 30 meV for D (33.7 meV listed) and about 41 meV for E (50.5 meV listed). E_F(E)/E_F(A) = 2.48, against (n_E/n_A)^(1/3) = 2.0. The absolute scale is also unclear: with v_F ~ 1×10^6 m/s, n = 2.9×10^16 cm^-3 gives E_F ≈ 50 meV rather than 20 meV. In Cd3As2, energies of 20–50 meV are near the Lifshitz transition between the two Dirac cones and the velocity is strongly anisotropic, so the isotropic single-cone screening model of Ref. 9 may not apply quantitatively. Supplementary Note 1, which describes the DOS used, was not available. Because eV0/E_F is the paper's organizing variable and the choice of E_F can move samples across the strong/weak boundary, the authors should state the dispersion, velocity, anisotropy and degeneracy used, give an uncertainty on E_F, and discuss how anisotropy and the two-node structure change eV0.

11. **The magnitude of the simulated QQHE in Fig. 1b is not reconciled with the data.** The disorder-free ρxx trace in Fig. 1b is magnified ×250 relative to the "disorder" trace, so the model's QQHE ρxx is orders of magnitude smaller than the measured lumps. It is then unclear how the experimental lump can be "large compared to the non-QQHE background" in Sample D. How disorder enters the light-blue trace is deferred to the supplement, and the Fermi velocity, g-factor and disorder treatment are not given in the main text. The authors should state explicitly how the predicted and measured magnitudes compare, explain the ×250 scaling, and summarize the disorder parameters and method in the main text so readers can judge how far the clean model is from the data.

12. **There is one film per density, per-sample metadata are missing, and reproducibility is not shown.** Each density is a single film and, apparently, a single Hall bar. Thicknesses (200–560 nm) are not assigned to samples, Hall-bar dimensions, contact geometry and growth conditions are not given, and mobility does not vary monotonically with n. 13. **Several claims go beyond the evidence, and novelty relative to prior work is not stated.** The abstract says "a zero resistivity state is obscured by a carrier density dependent background". This implies that vanishing ρxx would otherwise be expected, which is not established for a 3D QQHE: ρxx need not vanish even without disorder, because the dispersion along the field remains (see Ref. 8). Nothing in the analysis shows that removing the background would leave vanishing ρxx. ρ0 is 8–30 mΩ cm, the QQHE term is modelled as an additive contribution to a finite ρxx, and even the low-disorder pentatelluride data show finite ρxx minima. Other statements are also stronger than the data allow: "confirm the existence of QQHE" (p. 3), "it is Coulomb disorder that rules the roost" (p. 6), and "our results represent the first study of its kind" (Discussion), given Refs. 30–32 and earlier quantum-limit work on bulk Cd3As2. The authors should rephrase these claims or show the background-subtracted ρxx and how close its minima come to zero, and should state what is new relative to Refs. 30–32.
