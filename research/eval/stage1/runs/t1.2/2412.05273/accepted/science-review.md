A. Must address

1. **The final ρyx plateau is compared with theory in a regime where ρyx is not set by σxy (Fig. 4k, Results p. 5, Supplementary Note 7).** For Sample D at 20–31.5 T and 0.4 K, ρxx rises to roughly 600–850 mΩ cm while |ρyx| sits near 150–250 mΩ cm, around the claimed ρyx^FP ≈ 155 mΩ cm. So ρxx/|ρyx| ≈ 3–5 and tan θH ≪ 1. Low-field tan θH is also only about 3–4 (Fig. 3c,f). The QQHE is a statement about σxy = (e²/πh) kF,z,0. Linking the measured ρyx to πh/(e² kF,z,0) needs ρyx ≈ 1/σxy, i.e. σxy ≫ σxx. The Methods state this condition ("In the weak-disorder case, we always find σxy ≫ σxx"), and the Introduction says Coulomb scattering "spoils" the approximation ρxy ≈ σxy^-1. By the authors' own reasoning, a ρyx plateau under these conditions is not evidence of quasi-quantized σxy, and the agreement could be coincidental. A rapidly rising ρxx with a flattening ρyx in the quantum limit is also the textbook behaviour of magnetic freeze-out or field-induced localization (which the authors cite for ZrTe5, ref. 38), or of two-carrier conduction (electron–hole puddles, the buffer) setting in at high field. The "excellent agreement" also depends on kF,z,0, which is taken from the k·p model at the Hall density with no uncertainty, and the measured and predicted values are not both stated in the main text. The authors should invert the tensor and plot σxy(H) = ρyx/(ρxx² + ρyx²) and σxx(H) for Sample D up to 31.5 T (and for Samples A–E up to 14 T); test whether σxy, not ρyx, plateaus at e²kF,z,0/(πh); give the uncertainty on kF,z,0 (from the uncertainty in n and the Fermi-surface anisotropy, or from the SdH frequency) and hence on the 155 mΩ cm value, including the thickness and Hall-bar geometry uncertainty in converting resistance to resistivity; show the temperature dependence of the high-field ρxx to separate a freeze-out or insulating upturn from a plateau; and report whether the plateau appears in more than one device or cooldown. If σxy is not plateau-like, the "excellent agreement" final-plateau claim must be withdrawn or restated.

2. **The sub-14 T ρyx kinks and ρxx lumps are not shown quantitatively to be QQHE features (Figs. 3d–f, 4a–j; Results pp. 3–5).** For Samples A–E the central signature is "the step-like slope change in ρyx(H) visible in all Samples A–E below 14 T", read as "a broadened plateau at higher filling factor", together with a coincident lump in ρxx (e.g. ~5.7 T in Sample E, ~5 T in Samples A–C). No filling index is assigned, no expected plateau value or field position is given, and the positions are not shown to scale with n as the 3D model predicts. The authors say the QQHE "evolve[s] from standard Shubnikov–de Haas oscillations" (Supplementary Note 4), but without a quantitative criterion the feature cannot be told apart from a generic final SdH extremum, a Zeeman-split Landau-band crossing, or any field-dependent change in Hall slope. For each sample the authors should (i) estimate kF,z from n, or better from SdH frequencies, using the known Fermi-velocity anisotropy and g-factor of Cd3As2; (ii) predict the quantum-limit field and the N = 1→0 and N = 2→1 depopulation fields, and the corresponding ρyx (better σxy) values (h/e²)(π/kF,z)/ν; (iii) overlay these on Figs. 3e and 4f–j and give a table of predicted against observed fields; (iv) show that the features scale as n^(2/3) and that the shoulder value follows kF,z; and (v) where oscillations exist, plot a Landau-index fan with the kink field, the ρxx lump and the oscillation minima. Without this, the headline claim "we have observed evidence of the 3D QQHE" rests on a qualitative reading.

B. Should address

3. **Multicarrier and parallel-conduction explanations are dismissed only qualitatively (Results p. 3).** The argument against a two-band model ("nonmonotonicity is typically not observed", abrupt Hall-slope changes "are not typical") is not a quantitative test. At n ≈ 3×10^16 cm^-3 in a 210–560 nm film the bulk sheet density is only ~10^12 cm^-2, comparable to typical accumulation-layer, surface-state or buffer sheet densities. Possible channels include the ZnxCd1−xTe buffer or the GaAs interface, topological surface (Fermi-arc) states, and a depleted or accumulated surface layer, which matters because the films are uncapped (p. 6 notes that dielectric deposition has been challenging). A low-mobility second channel that freezes out or reaches its own quantum limit at a few tesla can produce a shoulder in ρyx and a kink in the FMR without quantization. The paper's own picture also implies multi-carrier transport: when eV0 ≳ EF (Sample A has eV0/EF ≈ 1, Fig. 2b), charge puddles mean coexisting electron and hole regions. The authors should show an explicit two-carrier (or mobility-spectrum) fit of σxx(H) and σxy(H) for at least Samples A–C and show where it fails; give the expected conductance of the buffer, ideally from a bare-buffer reference sample; and use the existing thickness series (Sample B 210 nm, others 350–560 nm) to test whether the features scale as bulk (3D) or sheet (2D) quantities.

4. **The eV0/EF estimates, and the strong-disorder regime assignments built on them, rest on an assumed, sample-independent defect density (Figs. 2b, 4l, 4m; Supplementary Note 5).** Fig. 2 assumes "an average concentration of charged point defects" of ~10^18 cm^-3 for every film, two orders of magnitude above n, which implies heavy compensation for which no evidence is shown. In Cd3As2 n is set by native donors (Cd interstitials, As vacancies) and acceptors (Cd vacancies) (refs. 21, 62), so films spanning 2.9×10^16 to 2.3×10^17 cm^-3 must differ in defect population or compensation; highly compensated low-n films would sit much further into the strong-disorder regime. The non-monotonic mobilities in Fig. 2c (A 1.10, B 0.67, C 1.52, D 1.01, E 1.22 × 10^4 cm²/Vs) and the varying thicknesses also point to sample-to-sample defect variation. The Methods do not say how n was tuned (growth conditions, post-growth treatment, extrinsic doping?), which bears on whether EF is the only variable. In addition, the regime map is inconsistent with the model applied: Figs. 2b and 4m place only Sample A near eV0 ≈ EF, with B–D below the line (eV0/EF ≈ 0.6–0.9), yet Eq. 1, described as valid "in the limit of strong Coulomb disorder", is applied to A–D, while E (eV0/EF ≈ 0.5) is excluded as "weak Coulomb disorder". No measured quantity independently tracks eV0 or the Landau-band broadening. The authors should state the tuning parameter for each sample; give an independent disorder estimate per sample (Dingle temperature or quantum lifetime from SdH, the transport-to-quantum lifetime ratio, which is large for long-range Coulomb scattering, the mobility through Coulomb-scattering theory, or the temperature scale over which the features fade) and show it tracks the calculated eV0/EF; propagate a plausible range of defect density into Figs. 2b and 4m as error bars; and reconcile the regime assignment with the use of the strong-disorder expression, either by revising the defect concentration with evidence or by restating Eq. 1 as purely empirical.

5. **Equation 1 does not uniquely separate the "QQHE" and "Coulomb disorder" contributions (p. 4–5, Fig. 4a–d, 4l, Supplementary Notes 2–3).** The model has ρ0, α, β, γ and δ free per sample, plus a Fermi-function crossover with Hc = 9.6 T and w = 1.5 T fixed globally without physical justification. α is "determined from a fit, often matching the size of the lump-like feature", so the QQHE term is partly tuned to the feature it is meant to explain. Hc lies inside every sample's measured range, so it shapes the curve rather than being tested, and it is not linked to any physical scale (the quantum-limit field, or the field where the magnetic length equals the disorder correlation length), although in the Rodionov et al. theory (ref. 25) the crossover should depend on n and eV0. Fits are shown only as gray dashed curves on the positive-field half, with no residuals, uncertainties, correlations or error bars in Fig. 4l, and only four samples are fitted. The decreasing β and γ with n (Fig. 4l) could simply reflect the growing classical MR at lower n: the H² term scales as ρ0(μH)², and mobility, ρ0 and thickness (440, 210, 560, 350 nm for A–D) all vary. The abstract's claim that "as theoretically predicted, Coulomb disorder is an essential ingredient" currently rests on qualitative agreement. The authors should report parameter uncertainties and correlations (especially α with β and γ) and residuals; fit with Hc and w free, or show how the results depend on them; compare with alternative backgrounds (classical two-band MR, guiding-centre linear MR of ref. 24, or a polynomial) with matched parameter counts plus the same QQHE term; show that β is not simply explained by ρ0μ²; test whether β, γ and δ agree in magnitude with ref. 25 using each sample's independently estimated eV0 and mobility; and show that the conclusion that "the QQHE component comprises a larger portion … with increasing carrier density" survives these changes.

6. **The theoretical plateaus require a fixed chemical potential, and the calculation is compared with the data only qualitatively (Methods p. 7, Fig. 1e,f, p. 4).** The Methods state that "for fixed charge density calculations, the 3D QQHE plateaus disappear and one recovers the (quasi)classical dependence", while Fig. 1e,f, which motivates the interpretation, fixes EF at 20 meV. A thin film with a fixed number of carriers is closer to the fixed-n case unless μ is pinned by a reservoir (charged defects, surface or interface states, the buffer), the 3D analogue of the localized-state reservoir in the 2D QHE; this decides whether plateaus are expected at all. The calculation also uses only a phenomenological broadening η, does not include LB broadening in the DOS, and does not model screened Coulomb disorder; the text says "our theoretical calculations when including moderate disorder reflect this", but which features are matched, and how well, is not shown. The authors should name the reservoir that pins μ, give evidence for it and its expected capacity; show the fixed-n calculation alongside Fig. 1e,f in the main text; compute ρxx(H) and ρyx(H) at each sample's measured EF (Fig. 2c) and overlay the predicted step fields on the data; state the g-factor, k·p parameters and the η values used for "weak" and "moderate" disorder, and relate η to the measured mobilities; and ideally add a disorder-averaged calculation with a screened Coulomb potential of the estimated eV0.

7. **The bulk 3D character of the effect is assumed from thickness rather than shown in the samples that carry the claim (Discussion p. 6; Methods; Supplementary Note 6).** The paper separates its result from 2D QHE and Weyl-orbit physics in thinner Cd3As2 (refs. 31–35) by noting all films are "well within the bulk-like thickness regime (>100 nm)". At n ≈ 3–6×10^16 cm^-3, however, EF ≈ 20–28 meV, while the confinement subband spacing πħv/d with v ~ 10^6 m/s is about 10 meV for Sample B (210 nm) and about 6 meV at 350 nm, so only a few kz subbands are occupied and the system may be closer to quasi-2D than the bulk k·p model assumes. The 3D Fermi-surface evidence comes from a film "of a slightly higher carrier density", not from Samples A–E, and surface states (predicted to accompany 3D QQHE) and Weyl orbits are excluded only by argument. The authors should measure the field-angle dependence of the ρyx kink and ρxx lump (or the oscillation frequencies) in at least one low-density sample (A–D): a 3D QQHE follows the 3D Fermi surface and kF,z along the field, whereas 2D subbands or surface channels follow the perpendicular field component. They should also estimate the subband spacing against EF and the Landau-band broadening for each film, and explain why thickness (210–560 nm) has no visible effect in the series. Otherwise the 3D claim should be narrowed.

8. **The symmetrization procedure is not described, and contact-misalignment admixture could create the features (Methods; Figs. 3d, 4a–e).** Fig. 4a–e shows raw ρxx for H < 0 only, with fits for H > 0, and the Methods do not say whether ρxx was symmetrized and ρyx antisymmetrized. With a 2 mm × 0.56 mm six-contact Hall bar and very large MR (FMR ≈ 900%), a small longitudinal offset of the Hall contacts mixes ρxx into ρyx (and vice versa). Since ρxx ≫ ρyx at high field, an imperfectly antisymmetrized ρyx would show exactly a kink coincident with the ρxx lump, and admixture could also create or distort the lumps that Eq. 1 attributes to H∂Hρyx. The FMR curves in Fig. 3d also look slightly asymmetric in ±H. The authors should state the procedure, show ρxx(+H) and ρxx(−H) together for each sample, show that the lump and kink persist in the (anti)symmetrized data, and explain any residual asymmetry.
