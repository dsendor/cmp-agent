A. Must address

1. The principal components ε_ab and ε_c come from an isotropic inversion that does not apply to this uniaxial crystal. Methods, Eq. (1) and the Results text on Fig. 2 (p. 2, p. 7) say ε_ab and ε_c were obtained by inserting Ψ and Δ, measured on each facet at a single 70° angle of incidence, into the isotropic two-phase pseudo-dielectric formula. The text calls this a "model-free transformation", says the response is "dominated by the dielectric-function component parallel to the s-polarized electric field", and says the orthogonal component "can be neglected" or is "only weakly sensitive" (citing Ref. 8). Ellipsometry measures ρ = r_p/r_s, and r_p at oblique incidence depends on two tensor components. On the (001) face, r_p involves ε_ab in the plane and ε_c along the surface normal; the uniaxial Fresnel coefficient contains √(ε_ab ε_c) and ε_ab/ε_c terms. On the side facet with c ∥ s, r_s depends on ε_c, but r_p depends on ε_ab, both in-plane and along the surface normal. Eq. (1) therefore returns pseudo-dielectric functions ⟨ε⟩, mixtures of ε_ab and ε_c, not principal components. Vanishing cross-polarization in the Mueller-matrix check (done on the (001) face and on a facet "whose exact Miller index was not determined") only shows that off-diagonal Jones elements are small; it does not show that the pseudo-ε equals the principal component. The two components differ here by up to Δn ≈ 3.7 and Δk ≈ 5.2, and the error is largest exactly where the claims sit: near 3.6–4.0 eV, where ε_ab is large (|ε| ~ 20–50) and varies fast while ε_c is small, and where |Δn|max = 3.67, the n_ab peak, |Δk|max = 5.22, the lower ENZ point and the start of the hyperbolic window are reported; and again near ε_c's 5.5 eV feature and the upper ENZ point. Δn, Δk, Δε1, the ENZ energies and the FOM all inherit this error, so the headline numbers cannot be accepted at the quoted precision. The authors should refit Ψ/Δ (or the full Mueller matrix) with a proper uniaxial bulk model (Berreman/4×4, or a standard anisotropic-substrate layer as in WVASE/CompleteEASE), including a surface roughness/overlayer layer. They should fit both facets jointly, at several angles of incidence (for example 55°, 60°, 65°, 70°, 75°), with the side facet measured at two azimuths (c ∥ s and c ∥ p). They should report the fit quality (MSE), parameter correlations and uncertainty bands on ε_ab and ε_c; report how much each headline number (Δn_max, Δn at 370 nm, Δk_max, both ENZ energies, FOM at 4.01 eV) changes relative to the Eq. (1) values; and show, as the most direct internal test, that ε_ab from the side facet (c ∥ p geometry) agrees with ε_ab from the (001) face.

B. Should address

2. The beam footprint relative to the facet size, and depolarization, are not documented, so the data, especially the c-axis data, may include light from outside the facet. Fig. 1b shows hexagonal pillars 1–2 mm long whose side faces are a few hundred μm across (100 μm scale bar). A standard M-2000 beam at 70° is stretched by about 3× and has a footprint of millimetre order unless focusing probes are used. The Methods give no spot size, focusing optics, alignment procedure, facet dimensions or depolarization spectra, and do not say how overfilling, edge scattering and light from adjacent facets were excluded. Silver-epoxy mounting suppresses back reflections from the mount but not scattering from facet edges, and if the beam overfills the side facet, light from adjacent facets of different orientation, and from the epoxy, would enter the c-axis data, which carry the whole anisotropy claim. Several features fit partial overfilling, a tilted facet or depolarization: the noisy n_ab and ε_ab1 below about 2 eV (Figs. 2a, 3a), the low n_c (concern 4) and the nonzero sub-gap k of about 0.07 for both polarizations. The authors should state the spot size and focusing optics and how the footprint compares with each facet; show depolarization (or the Mueller-matrix depolarization index) against photon energy for both geometries; and show that the extracted ε does not change when the spot is moved or reduced on the facet, or when a second angle of incidence is used.

3. The orientation and azimuthal alignment of the side facet have not been verified, and the cross-polarization check was done on the wrong surfaces. The text says Mueller-matrix measurements validate the absence of cross-polarization mixing (p. 7, SI), but these use the (001) face, where the optic axis lies along the surface normal so the off-diagonal elements vanish by symmetry at every azimuth, and a facet "whose exact Miller index was not determined". Neither tests the side-facet geometry, where c must lie accurately along s. Any in-plane rotation of c away from s, or a miscut of a few degrees from (100)/(110), mixes ε_ab into the "c-axis" spectrum, and this matters most near the strong 3.78 eV ab-plane resonance. The side-facet orientation is only asserted from habit in Fig. 1b, with no Laue or XRD data. The authors should state how the side-facet orientation was determined (single-crystal XRD or Laue on the same crystal, or habit alone) and its misorientation uncertainty; show Mueller-matrix off-diagonal blocks on the (100) side facet itself under azimuthal rotation, demonstrating that they vanish only when c is aligned with s or p; determine the Miller index of the facet used in the existing Mueller-matrix comparison, so that measurement becomes a quantitative test; and propagate the alignment uncertainty into ε_c and |Δn|.

4. Nothing independently checks n_c or Δn in the transparent region, and the absolute indices have not been tested for plausibility or internal consistency. All birefringence numbers come from reflection ellipsometry on as-grown facets with no surface model. The only cross-check, normal-incidence reflectance (Fig. 3c), is unpolarized, covers only ≤ 4.13 eV and depends on ε_ab alone, so it says nothing about ε_c or about whether the anisotropy was treated correctly. n_c, the less certain component that sets the visible Δn = n_ab − n_c, is surprisingly low at about 1.6–2.0 below 3 eV (Fig. 2a), below that of Al2O3 for a Cu-bearing oxide, which is what roughness, an overlayer or depolarization would produce in a pseudo-index. The point-by-point inversion is also not shown to be Kramers–Kronig consistent. The authors should provide at least one independent measurement of Δn on the side facet, for example polarized reflectance with E ∥ c and E ⊥ c checked against the ellipsometric ε_c and ε_ab, transmission retardance through a thinned, polished side-cut plate at 532 or 633 nm, or prism-coupling or interference-fringe data; agreement at the 10% level at one or two visible wavelengths would substantially strengthen the central claim. They should also fit a Kramers–Kronig-consistent oscillator model to each component and compare n_ab and n_c with published CuAlO2 film or powder indices and with the √ε∞ implied by the calculations, discussing any discrepancy.

5. The "low-loss" and "wide-bandgap transparent" framing (abstract, Fig. 2e, p. 5, conclusions, "broadband low-loss birefringence" bridging to UV hyperbolicity) conflicts with the paper's own extinction data and rests on ellipsometric k alone. The paper reports k_ab ≈ k_c ≈ 0.07 at 1 eV (p. 3); at 1.24 μm this is α = 4πk/λ ≈ 7×10³ cm⁻¹, an attenuation length of about 1.4 μm. Across the visible k is similar or larger (Fig. 2b suggests k_ab of order 0.2–0.5; at about 500 nm, k ≈ 0.07 alone gives α ≈ 2×10⁴ cm⁻¹ and an absorption length of about 0.5 μm), and the crystals look black (Fig. 1b). With |Δn| ≈ 0.6–0.8, a visible half-wave retarder needs about 0.3–0.4 μm and would absorb an appreciable fraction of the light, which is not low-loss in the sense used for LiNbO3 or calcite in Fig. 2e. The highlighted |Δn| = 1.66 at 370 nm (3.35 eV), claimed as the largest among "lower-loss" materials, lies only 0.1 eV below the stated 3.45 eV indirect gap, on the Urbach tail, and its k is not given. Reflection ellipsometry on a thick absorbing crystal cannot resolve k below about 0.01–0.05, and a surface overlayer, roughness or depolarization can produce spurious k, so the sub-gap loss is poorly constrained either way. The authors should measure polarized transmission through thinned (001) and (100) plates of known thickness, or oriented films, to give quantitative k_ab and k_c in the visible; report k_ab and k_c (or α) at every quoted wavelength (333 nm, 370 nm, 400–780 nm) and plot them next to Δn in Fig. 2c,e; give a loss-aware figure of merit such as Δn/max(k_ab, k_c) or retardance per absorption length; define "low-loss" numerically; determine whether the sub-gap k is intrinsic or an artifact of the inversion; and scope the abstract and title wording ("low-loss birefringence", "colossal visible birefringence") to the range where the loss-aware figure is favourable.

6. Surface roughness and overlayers are not included in the inversion, and surface preparation is not described. The facets are as-grown, and the crystal is dark from Cu²⁺ defects; Eq. (1) assumes an ideal abrupt interface. Even a few nm of roughness, or an oxidized (Cu²⁺/CuO) or Cu-rich surface layer, reduces the peak ε₂ and the peak n and k at a sharp resonance and adds spurious sub-gap absorption. This bears directly on the quoted n_ab,max = 5.66, |Δk|max = 5.22 and ε_ab2 ≈ 47. The agreement with reflectance in Fig. 3c below 4 eV is reassuring but does not reach the peak region. The authors should give AFM roughness for both facets, state whether the facets were cleaned or polished and how long after growth they were measured, include a roughness (Bruggeman EMA) layer in the uniaxial fit of concern 1, and report how the peak values change.

7. The evidence for the Type-II hyperbolic window between about 3.8 and 6.2 eV is weaker than presented. The reflectance check (Fig. 3c, 300–1050 nm, 1.18–4.13 eV) covers only the first ~0.3 eV of the 2.4 eV window: it confirms the onset of the high-reflectance band but not the band itself or the upper ENZ edge. The loss function (Fig. 3d) is computed from the same ellipsometric ε_ab and is not independent support. The upper ENZ point, where ε_ab1 crosses zero at about 6.1–6.2 eV, and the loss-function peak sit within about 0.25 eV of the 6.46 eV limit of the data, where source intensity, signal-to-noise and the Eq. (1) approximation are worst. In Fig. 3a, ε_c1 dips sharply near the c-axis absorption at about 5.4–5.7 eV (Fig. 3b) and appears to approach or cross zero; if ε_c1 < 0 there, both components are negative and that sub-band is doubly negative (metallic), not hyperbolic, yet the text says ε_c1 "remains positive" without giving a minimum. The lower ENZ crossing near 3.8 eV coincides with ε_ab2 ≈ 40–47, so |ε_ab| there is far from near-zero and the "ENZ" label is nominal. Across the window ε_ab2 stays comparable to |ε_ab1| (FOM ≤ 1.91), so the response is strongly lossy and supports evanescent rather than well-propagating high-k modes. The authors should report min ε_c1 with an uncertainty that includes the model error from concern 1, showing ε_c1 on an expanded scale with error bands over 5–6.5 eV and marking any non-hyperbolic sub-interval; give the uncertainty on the upper ENZ energy and state whether the window is continuous; show |ε| and ε₂ at both ENZ points; describe the window quantitatively as lossy in the abstract and compare with hBN's FOM in its own band; and extend reflectance (polarized, both facets) or ellipsometry toward 6.5 eV (VUV reflectance or synchrotron ellipsometry) if possible, or else state that the upper part of the window rests only on the ellipsometric inversion.

8. The benchmark comparisons (Fig. 2e and text) mix resonant and transparent regimes. |Δn|max = 3.67 occurs at 3.72 eV, at or above the stated 3.71 eV direct gap and on the steep flank of a transition where k_ab is several units; its size is the usual dispersive consequence of a strong absorption line. Yet it, and |Δk|max = 5.22, are described as "among the largest reported for natural anisotropic optical materials" in the UV–visible (p. 3), against comparators plotted only over their own low-loss ranges. The CuAlO2 curve runs through its absorption peak to 200 nm (stars mark sub-gap points), and every material's birefringence rises near its own edge. The selection criterion (gap above 1.55 eV) is also uneven: it excludes several known large-Δn crystals but includes MoS2, whose gap is close to the cutoff. The visible-range claim is better supported and is the useful one. The authors should use the same loss criterion for all materials (for example |Δn| only where max(k) < 0.1, a loss-normalized FOM, or Δn against wavelength normalized to each gap), mark the resonant part of the CuAlO2 curve as such, state the selection rule explicitly, and qualify the "largest" statements (including "largest value among the UV–visible lower-loss anisotropic materials surveyed") as at-resonance values or restrict them to below the gap.

9. The 3.71 eV "direct band gap" from a Tauc analysis (Fig. 2d inset) is not reliable as presented. It is obtained by extrapolating (αħω)² over a narrow window on the steepest rising edge (α ~ 10⁶ cm⁻¹) of the sharp 3.78 eV peak that the paper itself attributes to a strong exciton (p. 2, Refs. 17, 18). A parabolic-band Tauc law does not describe an excitonic line plus a Cu²⁺-related Urbach tail; the value depends on the fit window, the continuum onset would lie above the exciton, and a "gap" 70 meV below the exciton maximum implies a binding energy or broadening that is not discussed. The 3.71 eV value is nonetheless used in the abstract and comparison figures, to argue the gap is wider than earlier reports, and to place |Δn| = 1.66 at 370 nm "below the gap", although the SI's 3.45 eV indirect gap lies between 370 nm (3.35 eV) and 3.71 eV. The authors should report the sensitivity to the fit window and either fit the edge with an Elliott-type model (exciton plus continuum) to obtain the gap and exciton binding energy or call 3.71 eV an optical absorption onset; reconcile it with the 3.45 eV indirect value and literature values; and make the "wide bandgap" statements in the abstract consistent with the indirect gap and the sub-gap absorption.

11. The theory supports the selection rule only qualitatively and omits the excitonic physics that sets the magnitude. The GGA/Wannier calculation (Fig. 4) identifies orbital characters and the D3d selection rule, and the paper states it does not aim to reproduce the spectrum quantitatively (p. 5). But the size of the effect (ε_ab2 ≈ 47 and a sharp line) is excitonic, which GGA without electron–hole interaction cannot capture, and the main-text argument rests on a strong exciton (Refs. 17–19). The calculated ε_ab and ε_c are relegated to the SI, so the reader cannot see whether the predicted in-plane/out-of-plane oscillator-strength ratio and the absence of a c-axis feature match Fig. 2b. The GGA gap underestimate is not stated, nor are spin–orbit coupling or any Hubbard U on Cu 3d. Cu 4s character, which hybridizes with d_z² in delafossites and can carry z-polarized weight, is not shown (only 4p in Fig. 4c, with faint conduction-band projections). The "intra-atomic-like" d→p assignment rests on Bloch-function plots at the single L point, without checking whether the on-site d_z²→p_x/y matrix element dominates over inter-site Cu–O contributions. The authors should show the calculated ε_ab(ω) and ε_c(ω) with a stated scissor shift overlaid on the measured spectra in the main text; quantify the in-plane/out-of-plane spectral weight for the lowest transition; include the Cu s projection; quantify the d_z²/p_x/y weights along the whole L–F segment and decompose the momentum matrix elements in the Wannier basis; show a BSE spectrum for both polarizations (or compare directly with existing BSE results), or explain why the c-axis excitonic weight is suppressed by the same symmetry; say which parts of the agreement depend on excitonic effects; consider a temperature-dependent measurement of the 3.78 eV peak to establish its excitonic character; and temper "providing a microscopic basis" accordingly.
