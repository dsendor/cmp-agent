**A. Must address**

1. **The data and the model do not tell an excitonic YSR state apart from an ordinary impurity state at a band edge of a narrow-gap semiconductor.** The paper argues for an excitonic, pair-breaking origin on three grounds: the states extend 5–6 nm, well beyond atomic size (p. 5); they disappear where the gap collapses, both in the strained stripe and at small tip height (Fig. 3d, 3e); and they move with the gap edges (Fig. 3g, pp. 6–7). A donor- or acceptor-like level of a charged defect in any band insulator with a ~100 meV gap, light Dirac-like carriers and a large dielectric constant would show all three. It would have an effective Bohr radius of several nm (κ⁻¹ = ħ/√(2m*E_b)), it must merge into the continuum when the gap closes, and it follows the band edge it splits from. The model does not discriminate either. In Eq. (1) (Methods, p. 11) Δ is a fixed, spatially uniform hybridization (50 meV) that is not determined self-consistently, so Eq. (1) is mathematically a non-interacting two-band hybridization-gap insulator. A dipole has an attractive lobe that binds a state below the conduction-band edge and a repulsive lobe that binds one above the valence-band edge, while a monopole of one sign binds only one. That is the qualitative result of Fig. 4f, and it needs no exciton condensate. The defining YSR ingredient, local suppression of the pair amplitude by a pair-breaking perturbation ("locally break electron-hole pairing", p. 3; "pair breaking interactions", p. 9), is absent from the calculation, although p. 7 asserts the direct analogy to magnetic impurities. The authors should: (a) solve the impurity problem with Δ_i determined self-consistently from an interband interaction (e.g. a mean-field excitonic gap equation with a local Coulomb perturbation) and show that Δ is locally suppressed; (b) run the same calculation with a rigid non-interacting gap of the same size and potential, and identify an observable that differs between the two, such as the ratio E±/Δ or the dependence of the bound-state energy on Δ, the decay length relative to ħv/Δ, the spatial structure and asymmetry of the electron- and hole-like weights, coherence between E⁺ and E⁻, or the dependence on the sign of the impurity charge; (c) measure that observable, for example by mapping the local gap edges as a function of distance from each non-trivial defect, since a pair-breaking defect should suppress the excitonic gap over the coherence length and a simple potential scatterer in a band insulator should not. Without such a discriminating signature, Fig. 4f supports "charged defects bind in-gap states" but not "YSR states of an exciton condensate", and the title, abstract ("establish the YSR physics in exciton condensates") and conclusions should be reworded to something like "defect-bound in-gap states in an excitonic insulator candidate".

2. **The tip-height "gap tuning" is not shown to change the excitonic order parameter, and the I(Z) data point to a junction or contact artifact.** Figs. 3e–g and the supplementary tip-height figures (I(Z), spectra versus ΔZ) are the main quantitative evidence that the in-gap energies follow the gap, but the mechanism and possible artifacts are not addressed. The gap reduction from ~140 to ~60 meV is attributed to "transient carrier doping induced by the tunneling current" (p. 6), but no injected carrier density or quantitative argument is given. This mechanism is disputed for Ta2NiSe5 in a published Comment (Ref. 55) that the authors cite but do not engage with. At separations reduced by up to 0.5 nm, other causes are possible: tip-induced band bending and changes of voltage division in a semiconducting sample (which would rescale and shift both band edges and any defect level, reproducing Fig. 3g without any change of the order parameter), the strong tip field (the SI caption itself invokes Wannier–Stark effects), mechanical pressure from the tip (itself a strain, the authors' other tuning knob), and tip–sample hybridization. The apparent gap at ΔZ = 0 (~140 meV, p. 7) already exceeds the ARPES gap (~100 meV), which suggests band bending is present. The I(Z) curves do not look like point contact. The current "saturated to a consistent value of about 500 pA", which the authors call "the quantum contact regime", but a contact near G₀ at tens of mV would carry microamperes. A 500 pA plateau suggests an instrumental limit (preamplifier or feedback) or something else. Several I(Z) curves show abrupt steps near ΔZ ≈ −0.25 to −0.3 nm that coincide with the abrupt collapse to the "metallic pseudogap" in Fig. 3e and with discontinuities in the ΔZ colour maps. This points to a change in the tip or junction (mechanical contact, a picked-up adsorbate, local pressure) rather than an intrinsic doping-driven transition. The authors should: state the bias and current setpoint defining ΔZ = 0 and the bias used for I(Z); report the conductance in units of G₀ and explain the 500 pA saturation and the step; show that the gap reduction is reversible on retracting the tip and reproducible with different tips, and compare topography before and after the approach; test whether the reduction scales with current or with ΔZ by approaching at different biases, or by raising the current at fixed height through the setpoint bias (doping and field effects scale differently); and estimate tip-induced band bending for this gap and carrier density. If the tuning cannot be tied to a change in the excitonic order parameter, the "systematic tuning" argument for an excitonic origin ("solidly confirms the excitonic origin", p. 7) does not hold.

**B. Should address**

3. **The scaling of the in-gap energies with the gap has not been analysed in a way that separates the competing pictures.** In Fig. 3g E⁻ barely moves, while the filled-state gap edge is pinned by VB2, and E⁺ follows the conduction-band edge downward. That is what a constant binding energy below the conduction-band edge would produce. Fig. 3g also plots against ΔZ rather than the "gap edge positions" stated in its caption, and the sweeps appear to be single sweeps per defect type drawn as dashed lines without error bars. For a YSR-type state the energy should scale with Δ itself (E = Δ·f(coupling)), so the binding energy shrinks in proportion as the gap closes; for a hydrogenic state it is set by the effective mass and dielectric constant, not by Δ. The authors should plot each defect's binding energies (E_CB − E⁺ and E⁻ − E_VB), or E± directly, against the locally measured gap rather than ΔZ, for all five defect types, with error bars from several defects per type, and compare with both scenarios (see concern 1). Intermediate points from defects near the strained-stripe edge (concern 9) and temperature-dependent data (concern 12) would give independent tuning axes on the same plot.

4. **The in-gap peaks themselves could arise from tip-induced band bending or charge-state switching at charged defects.** In a narrow-gap system at 4.4 K, with defects the authors say are charged (p. 7), STS peaks at defects commonly come from tip-induced ionization or charge-state switching, or from band bending shifting defect levels; such features move with tip height and setpoint and can appear on both sides of E_F. The paper's own SI attributes "panning out spectral features" to the tip field, and the STS gap exceeds the ARPES gap. The authors should show the bias-setpoint dependence of the spectra at fixed current and report how E± depend on setpoint, look for ionization rings or sharp current-dependent features in dI/dV maps, compare constant-height with constant-current spectra, and estimate the expected band bending from the carrier density and screening length.
Raised by: r2, r3

5. **The charge state of the vacancies, on which the dipole model and the Ta/Pd distinction rest, is not established.** The key distinction (Ta vacancy charged, Pd vacancy "non-charged", pp. 7–8) comes from charge-density differences and a rigid ~0.3 eV shift of the Te PDOS near a Ta vacancy in neutral supercell calculations. Removing a Ta atom from a finite supercell also shifts the Fermi level (hole doping), so the PDOS shift relative to E_F may be a doping effect. A Ta vacancy removes a cation and would ordinarily act as a charged acceptor, and removing Pd also changes the electron count, so a Pd vacancy would generically be electrically active too. The charge state is set by charge-transition levels relative to E_F, which a neutral calculation does not determine, and at 4.4 K the vacancy may be ionized. Only dipole moments are reported, not net (Bader or similar) charges. The authors then choose a "simple symmetric dipole" over a monopole because a monopole gives only a filled-state bound state (Fig. 4f). This creates a logical problem: if Pd or Te vacancies are charged, the monopole result predicts filled-state in-gap states at the trivial defects, but none are seen. The authors should report monopole and dipole moments in consistent units for Ta, Pd and Te vacancies, state how the charge-density difference was defined (including relaxation of the reference), compute formation energies and charge-transition levels for Ta and Pd vacancies, map the DFT dipole quantitatively onto V₀ and d of the model, show that the two-bound-state result survives a dipole-plus-monopole potential, and reconcile the absence of in-gap states at the trivial defects with the monopole result.
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

6. **E⁺ and E⁻ do not behave as a YSR particle–hole pair and may be two independent levels.** In a superconductor a YSR state appears at ±E about the chemical potential as particle–hole partners of a single BdG state. Here E⁺ ≠ |E⁻| for every defect (e.g. α: +56/−34 meV; β: +70/−46 meV; SI table), the E⁺ and E⁻ maps have different shapes (Fig. 2k–o), in the authors' model the two arise from two spatially distinct potential lobes, and E⁺ moves strongly with ΔZ while E⁻ barely moves (Fig. 3g). This looks like one donor-like and one acceptor-like state, each bound to its own band edge. The model omits VB2 and is electron–hole symmetric, so the asymmetry is attributed to VB2 (p. 8) only by assertion. The authors should state precisely whether E⁺ and E⁻ are one YSR pair or two separate bound states, and include VB2 and the asymmetric bands in Eq. (1) to test whether the dipole model reproduces the observed energies and their different ΔZ dependence.
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

7. **The DFT argument that the states are many-body is undermined by the DFT gap and the unknown supercell size.** The text argues (p. 6) that "the DFT calculations do not predict the pairs of in-gap LDOS for any of these defects" and that DFT defect states are under 1 nm in extent, and uses this as evidence for a many-body origin. But the SI states that the DFT gap "is much smaller than the experimental excitonic gap" (essentially a semimetal in the monolayer), which would push any shallow state into the continuum, and the defect supercell size is not given; a cell a few nm across cannot host a bound state 5 nm across. The authors should report supercell dimensions, slab model, vacuum and k-mesh for the defect calculations, and redo the defect calculation with a method that opens a gap of the right size (hybrid functional, DFT+U on Ta/Pd d states, or a scissor-corrected tight-binding model fitted to ARPES) to show whether a non-excitonic ~100 meV gap already binds shallow states around a charged Ta vacancy. Otherwise the argument should be removed.
Raised by: r2, r3

8. **The spatial extent of the in-gap states is not quantified or compared with the relevant length scales.** The "much wider extents, up to 6 nm" are taken to "immediately suggest" a many-body origin (p. 5), and the Fig. 2 caption says "approximately 5 nm" instead. A shallow Coulomb-bound state in a small-gap, high-dielectric-constant material also has a Bohr radius of several nm, and the model's Gaussian width σ = 2 nm (p. 11) largely sets the extent of the model states. The maps are taken at constant current over defects that protrude strongly in topography, and the setpoint effect can create apparent halos. The authors should extract decay lengths, with uncertainties, along and across the chains for E⁺ and E⁻ separately from the grids in Fig. 2k–o; compare them with the excitonic coherence length ħv/Δ (Dirac velocity from ARPES) and an effective-mass Bohr radius; look for oscillatory structure (e.g. Friedel-like oscillations at 2k_F of the parent bands) that would favour a condensate-related state; and confirm the extent with constant-height or setpoint-normalized maps.

9. **The strained-stripe control (Fig. 3a–d) is necessary but not discriminating, and is weaker than stated.** The absence of in-gap states at defects inside the stripe is said to "clearly indicate" an excitonic origin (p. 6), but any bound state, hydrogenic or YSR, merges with a metallic continuum once the gap closes. The defect identity inside the stripe rests only on topography (Fig. 3b), whose contrast is itself modified by strain. The 2.4% strain comes from the FWHM of an FFT Bragg peak divided by Q_B over the whole image; that width includes finite-window resolution and inhomogeneous broadening, gives no sign, and is not a strain measure, and the same SI caption quotes a different "local shearing of about 1%" from a 0.57° angle. Other origins of the bright, gapless stripe are not excluded: a subsurface dislocation, stacking fault or domain wall, or a strain-induced band overlap (semimetal) with no excitonic fluctuations. The SI also notes that α′ keeps an in-gap state inside the pseudogap at small tip height, "whose origin is not understood yet", which contradicts the statement that in-gap states vanish in the pseudogap phase and is not discussed in the main text. The authors should provide a real-space strain map across the stripe (e.g. Lawler–Fujita analysis) and state the strain as a range with its method; show defect spectra at intermediate distances from the stripe, where the gap is partly reduced, and plot E± against the local gap there (concern 3); and discuss the α′ observation in the main text.

10. **The normalization and setpoint conditions of the spectra are not stated.** Nearly all spectra are "Normalized dI/dV" (Figs. 2f–j, 3d–f and the SI), without saying whether this is (dI/dV)/(I/V), division by a background, or something else. (dI/dV)/(I/V) is ill-defined near the gap edges of an insulator and can create or sharpen peaks there, which matters because the in-gap peaks sit close to the band edges, and the normalization is critical when tip height is varied (Fig. 3e). The setpoint bias and current for each figure, lock-in modulation amplitude and frequency, and energy resolution are also missing. The authors should state these and show raw dI/dV for representative spectra.

11. **Statistics and reproducibility of the defect energies are not reported.** The paper does not state how many crystals, cleaves and tips were used, how many defects of each type were measured, the spread of E± within a type, or whether the energies depend on local environment (defect density, distance to other defects). The SI table gives single values without uncertainties, and these differ by up to 4–5 meV from the energies labelled in Fig. 2k–o (α: 56/−34 vs 55/−30 meV; β′: 34/−48 vs 35/−45 meV; γ: 36/−22 vs 35/−20 meV), which also needs reconciling. Since the text says the five types have "marginally different energies" (p. 5), this is needed both for robustness and to decide whether the four DFT-assigned vacancy sites are spectroscopically distinguishable. The authors should give E± as mean ± standard deviation (or histograms) for each type, ideally from at least two cleaves.

12. **No temperature dependence is shown, although it would be the most direct link between the bound states and the order parameter.** All STS is at 4.4 K (and 1.26 K for the field data), while the transition is at ~365 K, so the gap varies substantially over a range accessible to variable-temperature STM (e.g. 4–80 K, matching the ARPES temperature, or 77–300 K). A state bound to the excitonic order should follow Δ(T). Tracking E± versus Δ(T) on the same defect type would test the link independently of the tip-field complications of concerns 2 and 4, and would also bear on the preformed-exciton reading (concern 17). If this is beyond scope, the claims should be narrowed (concern 13).

13. **Several claims go beyond the evidence, and the excitonic nature of the gap is partly assumed.** The abstract's "establish both the YSR physics in exciton condensates" and "persisting up to room temperature" (no measurement above 4.4 K, and no in-gap states shown at elevated temperature), and "solidly confirms the excitonic origin" (p. 7), are not supported as the evidence stands. "The presence of the interaction-driven impurity-bound states in turn manifests the excitonic nature of the present insulating phase" (p. 9) is circular: whether the gap in Ta2Pd3Te5 is excitonic rather than structural, phonon-driven or topological is still debated (refs. 42, 43, 49, 60, 61), and the argument both assumes the excitonic nature to interpret the states and claims to confirm it. The authors should reword these statements unless concerns 1–3 are resolved, acknowledge the alternatives for the parent gap, and not claim both premises.

14. **The model calculation is not set up for quantitative comparison.** The 41 × 41 lattice with a = 0.25 nm spans only ~10 nm, comparable to twice the ~5 nm extent of the states, so finite-size quantization may affect the in-gap levels. The Gaussian width σ = 2 nm exceeds the dipole separation d = 1 nm (Eq. 2), so the dipole nearly cancels and the result may depend strongly on these unconstrained choices. The lattice is a 2D isotropic square lattice, whereas the material is quasi-1D and anisotropic. It is unclear how t = 0.5 eV, μ = −1.5 eV and Δ = 50 meV were obtained "with the experimental and DFT parameters as input" or relate to the ARPES dispersions, and the V₀ values in Fig. 4f (legends 0–0.24 eV and 0–0.10 eV) are not explained. Fig. 4f varies V₀ at fixed Δ, while the experiment varies Δ at fixed defect. The authors should show convergence with system size, sensitivity to σ and d, the effect of anisotropic hopping (and VB2, concern 6), and E± versus Δ at fixed V₀ overlaid on Fig. 3g.

15. **The assignment of the non-trivial defects to top-layer Ta vacancies is qualitative.** It relies on visual agreement of topographs (Fig. 4a,b and SI), which the SI calls "reasonable"; its caption concedes that some contrast differs and the sentence is unfinished ("while some contrast as guided by red circles…"). For β and β′ the simulated occupied-state images differ visibly from experiment. Only Te, Pd and Ta vacancies were considered, not antisites (e.g. Pd_Ta, Te_Ta), interstitials, intercalants or extrinsic impurities. The mapping of α, α′, β, β′ onto sites (1)–(4) is justified only by mirror symmetry, and γ is assigned to a subsurface Ta vacancy without simulation (p. 6). No formation energies explain why Ta vacancies would be about as common as Pd vacancies, and the observed defect density is not compared with the Ta deficiency expected from the off-stoichiometric flux growth (2.0:4.5:7.5) or with EDX, which in any case cannot resolve vacancy concentrations at the relevant level. The authors should include antisite and interstitial candidates, give formation energies, quantify image agreement (e.g. cross-correlation), simulate the subsurface vacancy for γ, and compare defect density with stoichiometry.

16. **The magnetic-field test cannot exclude the topological defect-state alternative.** The absence of splitting at 2.5 T (p. 8, SI) is used to rule out quantum spin Hall defect states, but for g = 2 the Zeeman splitting is ~0.3 meV, far below the ~10–20 meV width of the in-gap peaks, and the lock-in modulation and energy resolution are not given. The field data were also taken at 1.26 K rather than 4.4 K, mentioned only in the SI. The authors should quote the expected splitting against the linewidth and resolution and either show it would be resolvable (larger fields, narrower features) or remove the argument.

17. **The pseudogap and "preformed exciton" interpretation is asserted.** The ~30 meV pseudogap in the strained region and at small ΔZ is called evidence of "strong excitonic fluctuation, the preformed excitons" (p. 6). A soft gap at 4.4 K in a strained or disordered metallic region can also come from Altshuler–Aronov or Coulomb-gap physics, or from tip-induced effects. The authors should support this reading (for example with temperature dependence) or present it as speculation.
