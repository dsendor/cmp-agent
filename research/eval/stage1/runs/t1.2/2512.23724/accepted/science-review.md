A. Must address

1. **The data and the model do not distinguish an excitonic, pair-breaking bound state from an ordinary defect level in a gapped semiconductor.** In the Methods Hamiltonian (Eq. 1), the excitonic order enters only as a uniform, fixed interband term −Δ Σ(c†v + v†c). Δ is not computed self-consistently and is not suppressed near the impurity. Mathematically, the model is therefore a non-interacting, band-hybridized semiconductor with a local electrostatic potential V_i acting on both orbitals. In any such insulator, a charged donor binds a state below the conduction-band edge and a charged acceptor binds one above the valence-band edge. A local charge dipole (the authors' own DFT picture, Fig. 4b) has a positive and a negative end, so it would generically bind one donor-like and one acceptor-like level: a pair split from the two band edges, as in Fig. 2f–j, whether or not excitons condense. Nothing in the calculation of Fig. 4e–g uses the many-body nature of Δ. The defining physics of the YSR problem, local suppression of the order parameter by the impurity, is absent. The two experimental tests the paper relies on do not discriminate either. (i) The states track the gap edges under tip tuning (Fig. 3g), but hydrogenic or deep levels tied to the band edges, or any level whose binding energy scales with the gap, would do the same. (ii) The states vanish when the gap collapses into a metallic pseudogap (Fig. 3d,f), but any localized level is screened or hybridized into the continuum once the host becomes metallic. A ~5–6 nm envelope is also comparable to an effective-mass Bohr radius in a narrow-gap semiconductor, so the authors should explain why a Ta-site defect state has the extent and lattice-periodic modulation seen in Extended Data Fig. 3a. As a result, the claims that the result "solidly confirms the excitonic origin" (p. 1554), that the states arise "through a charge-exciton version of the YSR mechanism" (abstract) and the YSR analogy in the title do not yet follow, and the Conclusion's claim that the bound states "manifest the excitonic nature of the present insulating phase" is circular. The authors should: (a) solve the impurity problem self-consistently for Δ(r) around the dipole, show the local suppression of the order parameter, and look for its signature in the data, for example a spatial map of the local gap edges near the defect compared with Fig. 2k–o; (b) repeat the Fig. 4e–g calculation with Δ = 0 and a single-particle hybridization or structural gap of the same size, the same bands and the same dipole, and state which observables differ (number of states, energies relative to the edges, spatial extent, scaling with the gap, dependence on dipole strength including any level crossing analogous to the YSR quantum phase transition); and (c) identify and test at least one experimental feature that the excitonic scenario predicts and a conventional dipole-bound level does not. The most direct is how the binding energy scales with the gap: for YSR-type states, (E± − gap centre)/Δ_EI is set by the impurity strength and the energies move in proportion to Δ, whereas for band-edge-bound levels the binding energy measured from each edge should be roughly constant. The authors should plot E± measured from the gap edges, and E±/Δ_EI, against Δ_EI for each defect type from the Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 8 data, with error bars. Other candidates are the relation between bound-state energy, gap and coherence length, and whether the wavefunction decays and oscillates with the Fermi wavevector of the parent semimetal bands rather than with the lattice.

B. Should address

2. **The tip-height "gap tuning" may reflect tip-induced artefacts rather than a reduced excitonic order parameter, and the gap collapse coincides with a visible discontinuity in the data.** The reduction of the apparent gap from ~140 to ~60 meV and the abrupt collapse (Fig. 3e–g, Extended Data Figs. 7 and 8) are attributed to carrier injection by the tip field. At ΔZ down to −500 pm, with the tip "virtually touching" the surface, several other effects become strong, and each would make in-gap levels move towards E_F together with the band edges: voltage division (on a low-carrier-density gapped surface part of the bias drops inside the sample, and the fraction changes with tip–sample distance, rescaling every bias-referenced feature; the authors cite precisely this debate for Ta2NiSe5, refs 42, 43, but do not address it); tip-induced band bending; screening of the defect potential (the authors themselves impose a drop of the dipole to 0.6V0 in Fig. 4f, which is already an admission that the defect potential changes with tip height); a change in the defect's charge state; tip–sample force inducing local strain (which the authors show separately closes the gap); and large current density and local heating. The Methods claim that the excitonic Stark effect is "an order of magnitude smaller than the effect of carrier doping", but give no estimate of it, of the field, or of the induced carrier density, while Extended Data Fig. 7 attributes fanning features to a Wannier–Stark ladder, implying a strong field acting on the electronic structure. In addition, every panel of Extended Data Fig. 8a,b (and Fig. 3e) shows a sharp horizontal seam at ΔZ ≈ −0.25 to −0.3 nm, where the colour scale and spectra change abruptly, and the "abrupt collapse into a metallic pseudogap" happens exactly there, close to what Extended Data Fig. 8 calls the "quantum contact regime". The authors should: state whether the seam reflects a change in acquisition (stitched datasets, setpoint or gain change, tip change) or a physical transition, and explain why a carrier-density-driven suppression would be so abrupt; show raw I(V) and I(z) curves and the junction conductance across the ΔZ range; demonstrate reversibility on retraction and repeat the sequence on several nominally identical defects; quantify field, induced carrier density and band bending versus ΔZ (for example by Poisson modelling with the known carrier density and tip radius for the PtIr tip); estimate tip-induced force and strain at the closest approaches; show whether features outside the gap (the VB2 edge and the Wannier–Stark features) rescale by the same factor as the gap, which would indicate voltage division; and plot E± against midgap and against each band edge separately, since band bending shifts both edges in the same direction while order-parameter reduction moves them towards each other. Because the excitonic-origin argument depends heavily on this series, it must be shown to reflect a controlled change of Δ_EI at fixed defect potential.

3. **Tip-induced charging of the defects is not ruled out as the origin of the sharp in-gap peaks and their spatial extent.** In narrow-gap semiconductors and insulators, sharp dI/dV peaks near defects often come from the tip field switching the defect's charge state; they appear as disks or rings around the defect whose radius grows with bias and setpoint, and they shift with setpoint. The ~5–6 nm extent (Fig. 2k–o, Extended Data Fig. 3) and its pronounced growth with tunnelling current (Extended Data Fig. 9: 30 → 100 → 200 pA) are what that mechanism would give. The paper reads the growth as YSR-like broadening with a smaller gap, but at 200 pA the maps show saturated, ring-like features, and the growth is shown at fixed bias (−35 and +40 meV) while the state energies themselves move. Several spectra in Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 8c at ΔZ = −0.2 to −0.3 nm also show dip and derivative-like line shapes. The authors should show dI/dV maps at several energies across each resonance to test whether a ring disperses in radius with bias; give point spectra at fixed lateral offsets from the defect as a function of setpoint (charging peaks shift strongly with lateral distance, true bound states do not); take grid spectroscopy at more than one setpoint in the unperturbed-gap regime; and report whether hysteresis, telegraph noise or negative differential conductance appears at the peaks.

4. **No temperature dependence is reported.** All STM data are at 4.4 K. The proposed condensate has a known temperature scale (transition at ~365–370 K, Fig. 1d). If the in-gap states are tied to the excitonic order, their energies relative to the gap edges should follow Δ(T), as predicted by the model with fixed V0, whereas ordinary defect levels would follow the single-particle band edges. Spectra of E± and the gap edges on the same defects at one or two higher temperatures (for example 77 K and above 200 K, as high as the STM allows), compared with the ARPES gap at the same temperatures, would give a gap-tuning knob free of the tip-field concerns in item 2. Absent such data, the authors should note this as a limitation.

5. **The structural and charge assignment of the defect is not unique, and the formation energies are inconsistent with the observed defect populations.** The caption of Extended Data Fig. 4 states that "the Pd substitution on a Ta site, a Ta vacancy, and the Ta-Pd interchange defect exhibit topographic features consistent with the experimental observations", while the main text says the Pd substitution "explains the observed STM images excellently". The dipole argument is shown only for Pd_Ta versus the Pd vacancy (Fig. 4b,d). Extended Data Table 1 also has internal problems. Under Pd-rich/Te-rich conditions, which match the flux composition (Ta:Pd:Te = 2.0:4.5:7.5 against stoichiometric 2:3:5, Methods), Pd_Ta has −0.948 eV (spontaneous) and the Ta vacancy 0.148 eV, both below the Pd vacancy (0.756 eV), yet the Pd vacancy is the majority defect (77.8%, about 4.6 times more numerous; Extended Data Fig. 1, 67 : 308). The Pd-vacancy energy is identical (0.756 eV) in the generic, Pd-rich/Ta-rich and Pd-rich/Te-rich columns and lower (−0.577 eV) only in the Te-rich/Ta-rich column, although a Pd vacancy should be most costly under Pd-rich conditions; the column headings differ from the conditions in the caption, and it is unclear how "Pd-rich/Ta-rich" chemical potentials are chosen simultaneously under the stability limits. Only neutral defects appear to be considered, although the mechanism rests on the defect charge and the formation energy of a charged defect depends on the Fermi level. Methods describe only the bulk DFT setup. The authors should give the charge distribution and dipole for the Ta vacancy and the interchange defect and state whether they would also give two in-gap states in the model (if several defects with different charge distributions give the same pair, this should be stated: it would weaken the specific dipole mechanism but could support a more generic pair-breaking claim); define the chemical-potential limits for each column and reconcile the predicted and observed populations; calculate charge states of Pd_Ta and transition levels relative to the gap; add discriminating evidence for the assignment, for example a quantitative comparison of the bias dependence in Extended Data Fig. 5 with simulations for each candidate; and give the supercell or slab size, surface termination, k-mesh and relaxation used for the defect and STM simulations.

6. **The model is qualitative and its parameters are not tied to the material.** The Hamiltonian is an isotropic 2D square lattice with electron–hole-symmetric bands (t = 0.5 eV, μ = −1.5 eV, Δ = 50 meV, a = 0.25 nm), whereas Ta2Pd3Te5 is quasi-one-dimensional, with a Dirac crossing of CB1/VB1 and an extra valence band VB2 (Fig. 1d, Extended Data Fig. 7d), and the bound states are visibly elongated along the chains (Fig. 2k–o, Extended Data Fig. 3). The dipole strength V0 = 0.24 eV and the geometry (±2a, Gaussian width 2a) are neither derived from the DFT charge distribution nor fitted to the data. The particle–hole asymmetry, with E− sharper and more robust than E+ (Fig. 3e,g), is reproduced only by adding a five-Gaussian charge distribution and unequal hoppings (t_c = 1.0 eV, t_v = 0.5 eV, Fig. 4g) chosen ad hoc, while the band-edge asymmetry is attributed to VB2, which is absent from the model. The screening of the dipole with gap reduction (linear down to 0.6V0, Fig. 4f) is also imposed by hand, and it is unclear whether the reductions to 0.6Δ_EI and 0.6V0 are tied by assumption. As it stands, the agreement in Fig. 4e–g carries little weight. The authors should state which parameters are fitted and which are fixed by DFT or ARPES; derive V0 and the charge geometry from the DFT result for Pd_Ta; use anisotropic band parameters fitted to the ARPES and DFT dispersions, including VB2 or showing robustness to it; show the sensitivity of E± to these choices; and compare bound-state energies relative to the gap edges, and spatial extents along and across the chains, quantitatively with Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 3.

7. **The strained-stripe control is limited.** The statement that non-trivial defects in the stripe lack in-gap states, used to argue that "the in-gap states originate from the coupling of the defects with the excitonic order" (p. 1553), rests on "a few" defects in one stripe, with three shown (one each of α, β, β′; Fig. 3b,d). How the 2.4% strain is extracted from a 0.3 nm corrugation is deferred to the supplement (Supplementary Fig. 10). Strain, local carrier density and possibly the defect's own structure and charge all change together with the gap, so the absence of in-gap states could come from metallic screening or a changed defect rather than loss of excitonic order, and broadened states inside a ~30 meV pseudogap may be unresolvable rather than absent. The authors should give the number of defects and stripes examined; show that the defect topography (and the DFT dipole) is unchanged under 2.4% strain; estimate the resolution needed to see the predicted states inside the pseudogap; and, most usefully, show spectra for each type at several positions across the stripe boundary, where the gap varies continuously, to test whether E± shift continuously with the local gap independently of the tip.

8. **The excitonic character of the gap in Ta2Pd3Te5 is taken as established.** The paper treats it as settled from refs 33–36, but the cited literature also considers a topological-insulator and a structural or band-hybridization origin (refs 33–36, 38, 39, 47, 48). The YSR analogy requires an exciton condensate. The Introduction and Conclusion should acknowledge the open debate, and the authors should state which of their observations would also hold if the gap were mainly lattice- or hybridization-driven. This sharpens concern 1.

Extended Data Fig. 8, however, notes that for α′ "an in-gap state appears within the pseudogap in the quantum contact regime, whose origin is not understood yet", and several panels of Extended Data Fig. 8c at ΔZ = −0.3 nm show sharp features near E_F. The authors should discuss this in the main text, say whether it is consistent with the YSR picture, and tone down "solidly confirms".
