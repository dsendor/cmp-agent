**A. Must address**

None. The concerns below would narrow or firm up the claims, but the reviews show that the paper already gives evidence a reasonable referee would accept for its main result: a dense, model-independent violation of Kohler's rule, a single anisotropic-scattering model that reproduces ρ(H,T), Δρ/ρ(0) and dρ/dH at x = 0.159, and a hotspot T-linear amplitude g(x) that falls together with Tc across the series.

**B. Should address**

1. **The step from a g(x)–Tc correlation to "spin fluctuations are the pairing glue" is qualitative, and part of the data sits awkwardly with a simple link.** In the main text after Fig. 5 ("we conclude that SC pairing in electron-doped cuprates is mediated by the exchange of low-energy spin fluctuations"), in the title and in the abstract, the pairing claim rests on g(x) and Tc both decreasing with x. Across 0.116 ≤ x ≤ 0.188, however, almost everything changes monotonically with x: carrier density, the AFM correlation length, the distance from Fermi-surface reconstruction, the hotspot positions, t′/t, ρ0 and A1. Any of these would correlate with Tc on the overdoped side of the dome. The introduction frames the result through λ_ph, λ_tr and the McMillan formula (Supplementary Fig. 2), but the paper never extracts a λ. Using 1/τ = 2πλk_BT/ħ with α1 = 0.75 ps⁻¹ K⁻¹ (Extended Data Table 2), the hotspot-peak coupling is λ ≈ 0.9–1 at x = 0.159, about 6 at x = 0.116 and about 0.5 where Tc = 0; the Fermi-surface average is smaller. In Extended Data Table 3 and Fig. 5c, g stays finite (0.66 at x = 0.174, 0.55 at x = 0.184–0.188) where Tc is already zero, and Tc seems to vanish near x ≈ 0.175 while g is still about 0.6. Between x = 0.141 and 0.188, g falls only about 3.4-fold. Superconductivity therefore disappears at finite coupling, and this needs an explanation: a threshold set by μ*, pair breaking by disorder in the films, or a falling spin-fluctuation energy scale. Spin-fluctuation theories make Tc depend on the characteristic fluctuation energy (refs. 46, 61, 62) as well as on the coupling, so g(x) alone does not fix Tc. For d-wave pairing the relevant quantity is the d-channel projection of the interaction, which weights (π,π) scattering differently from λ_tr, so the s-wave phonon near-equality λ_ph ≈ λ_tr in Supplementary Fig. 2 does not carry over automatically. The authors should (i) give λ_tr(x) from g(x)α1⟨β(φ)⟩, both at the hotspot and averaged over the Fermi surface, and plot Tc against it for each channel, including the Tc = 0 channels; (ii) give at least an order-of-magnitude weak-coupling or Eliashberg-type Tc estimate from the extracted anisotropic kernel and literature spin-fluctuation energies; (iii) explain why g stays finite past the end of the dome; (iv) show that the correlation is specific to the anisotropic channel, for example that the isotropic T² coefficient α2, held fixed here, would not correlate equally well if freed, and argue against g and Tc merely co-varying with another x-dependent quantity. Otherwise the title and conclusions should be reworded from "evidence for spin-fluctuation-mediated superconductivity" to "a correlation between Tc and the strength of anisotropic inelastic scattering at the AFM hotspots".

2. **Most of the "order-of-magnitude" drop in g(x) comes from channels below x_FSR, where the model's unreconstructed Fermi surface may not apply.** In Extended Data Table 3, g falls from 6.5 at x = 0.116 to about 2.1 at x = 0.138 and 1.46 at x = 0.146; above x_FSR ≈ 0.14 it falls only from about 2.1 (or 1.46) to 0.55, a factor of about 2.7–3.5, while Tc goes from about 15 K to 0. For x < x_FSR the authors' own data depart from the model's assumptions: a negative MR component attributed to spin scattering (ref. 21), mixed-state curves in Fig. 2h–j, an MR maximum at 50–75 K, a Hall coefficient that changes sign at low T (Extended Data Fig. 4b, x = 0.117), a negative high-field slope below 20 K and a γ1 already strongly suppressed below about 40 K at x = 0.116–0.131 (Extended Data Fig. 7), and the Fermi-surface reconstruction in Fig. 1. Yet g(x) at these dopings is fitted at one temperature and field (40 K, 20 T) with the unreconstructed large Fermi surface and tight-binding parameters of Fig. 3 and Extended Data Table 1, and with no isotropic negative-MR term. The statements that 40 K is "above T_FSR" and that at 40 K "signatures of AFM order are absent or weak across the doping range" (Fig. 5 caption) are asserted rather than shown; no T_FSR(x) is given for x = 0.116–0.138, and in Fig. 1 T* near x ≈ 0.12 appears to lie above 40 K. The authors should state T_FSR (or T*) for each channel and show that 40 K lies above it; show that the 40 K data at x < 0.14 are free of the negative MR component (for example from the low-field curvature or from the longitudinal MR at 40 K); justify the unreconstructed Fermi surface there; show full ρ(H,T) fits of the Fig. 4 type for at least one low-x (≈ 0.116–0.125) and one high-x (≈ 0.18) channel with only g varying, to show the one-parameter description holds across the series; and give uncertainties on g(x) in Fig. 5c and Extended Data Table 3. They should also state how much of the g–Tc correlation survives when the analysis is restricted to x > x_FSR, and if the quantitative claim holds only there, state the smaller dynamic range plainly in the abstract, the main text and Fig. 5.

3. **As extracted, g(x) largely restates the zero-field resistivity and A1(x), so the extra constraint from the MR is not demonstrated.** For S1, g(x) is the only free parameter and is fixed per channel by matching just two numbers, ρ(0, 40 K) and Δρ/ρ(0) at 20 T and 40 K (Fig. 5a,b; Extended Data Table 3), with τ_imp⁻¹ (Γ0), α2, the hotspot height and width, the β(φ) exponent and the hotspot position all held at their x = 0.159 values (Extended Data Table 2). ρ(0, 40 K) falls from about 44 to 24 μΩ cm across S1, and with Γ0 fixed most of that drop must be absorbed into g(x); any change in residual resistivity across the composition-gradient film (oxygen content, disorder) therefore goes straight into g. For S2, g(x) is not obtained from the MR at all; it is the T-linear coefficient A1 of ρ(0,T) (Extended Data Fig. 1c), normalized to S1 at x = 0.159 (Extended Data Table 3 caption). The g(x) curve in Fig. 5c is thus stitched from two different methods, and the high-x flattening comes from resistivity alone. The correlation of A1 with Tc is already established (refs. 8, 10; Fig. 1 inset), and the paper's claimed advance is that the MR isolates the anisotropic inelastic part, which Fig. 5 does not show. The authors should fit the full Δρ/ρ(H,T) data for each channel, at least at x = 0.116, 0.131, 0.146 and 0.159, with Γ0 (and preferably the hotspot strength) free alongside g(x); show that the MR by itself, without the absolute ρ(0, 40 K), fixes g(x) and gives the same trend; model the S2 MR curves (Fig. 2k,l) in the same way to confirm the A1 route; and give uncertainties on g(x) that include the effect of letting τ_imp vary.

4. **The identification of the scatterer as (π,π) spin fluctuations rests on a semiquantitative Hall calculation that is not tied to these samples and does not test all alternatives.** The paper concedes that the MR alone cannot separate spin hotspots from antinodal charge scattering (Fig. 4b vs 4c), so the discrimination rests entirely on the Jones–Zener plus current-vertex-correction (CVC) calculation of R_H (main text after Fig. 4; Methods "Incorporation of CVCs"; Extended Data Figs. 4–6). That calculation has several weaknesses. R_H is given as single numbers (8.9, 5.6, 10.3, 4.3 and 11.8 × 10⁻¹⁰ m³ C⁻¹) for fixed Γ(φ) shapes, not as R_H(T,x), even though the main text says it "captures the key features of R_H(T,x) beyond x > x_FSR, most notably the reduction in R_H with increasing T". The CVC strength ε(φ) is an ad hoc Gaussian with σ = 0.07 and fixed height (eq. 12), its prefactor 1/(1 − ε²) is dropped, and its amplitude is not tied to the spin-fluctuation parameters used for the MR. The expansion is low-field, whereas the R_H data were taken at 14 T. The Hall data come from other films (non-combinatorial films of ref. 27 and combinatorial films of ref. 10), not from S1 or S2, and the two data sets disagree near x ≈ 0.15–0.16 (about 8–9 × 10⁻¹⁰ versus about 13–15 × 10⁻¹⁰ m³ C⁻¹, a factor ≈ 1.6–1.7); the computed isotropic value of 8.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ agrees "excellently" only with the former, and the discrepancy is as large as the differences between the scenarios being distinguished. By the authors' account (Extended Data Fig. 6d), what lowers R_H is a broad nodal-leaning component Γ_N together with the CVCs, and nothing shows that a T-linear Γ_N must come from spin fluctuations rather than, for example, anisotropic electron–phonon scattering above about Θ_D/5 (the Methods themselves cite the −45 meV antinodal kink and possible phonon coupling). The charge alternative is rejected using only Γ_AN plus charge hotspots (Extended Data Fig. 6e), without CVCs tied to the (δ,0) charge-ordering wavevector or an anisotropy peaked where the curvature changes, so it may not have received an equally favourable treatment. The authors should compute R_H(T) at several x above x_FSR with exactly the Γ(φ,T,x) used for the MR, including g(x) and the T dependence, at the measurement field, and compare quantitatively with Hall data, ideally measured on S1/S2 or films from the same growth; show the sensitivity of the sign and trend of R_H to σ, the CVC amplitude, the omitted prefactor and the hotspot width; give the charge scenario an equivalent best effort; show that a nodal-peaked non-magnetic T-linear component cannot reproduce MR and R_H together; and explain the factor ≈ 1.7 discrepancy between the Hall data sets. Otherwise the spin-fluctuation assignment should be described as the most natural interpretation rather than as established.

5. **The angular form of the anisotropic scattering is described inconsistently between the MR fit and the Hall argument.** The main text and Fig. 4b(i) place the T-dependent component at the hotspots on the AFM zone boundary, with β(φ) = sin¹²[2(φ ± (π/4 − HS))]. The Methods describe "eight broad peaks, each located at the intersection of the underlying FS and the AFM BZ boundary", and also say the anisotropy "most likely" broadens toward the nodes. The Hall argument instead uses Γ_N, "peaked along the zone diagonals" (Extended Data Fig. 6d caption), apparently as an additional component, and the Methods say "the inclusion of an associated Γ_N with a magnitude that leads to a doubling of the MR (Fig. 4) also causes R_H to halve". A reader cannot tell whether one Γ(φ) explains both MR and R_H, and the spin-fluctuation argument depends on exactly that. The authors should state the angular function used in each calculation explicitly, with HS in defined units, plot the MR-fit Γ(φ) and the Γ_N of Extended Data Fig. 6d on the same axes, and if possible reconcile them into one form.

6. **That the T-linear term is anisotropic is assumed rather than tested.** Eq. (1) multiplies both g(x)α1T and α2T² by the same β(φ) "for simplicity", so there is no isotropic T-linear component; the choice is motivated by A1(x) decreasing with x, but it builds in that the T-linear coefficient is entirely anisotropic and tracks A1. The headline "anisotropic T-linear scattering" therefore follows from the model choice. The authors should fit an isotropic T-linear term alongside the anisotropic one (and the alternative of isotropic T-linear plus anisotropic T²) at x = 0.159 and at least one other doping, and show what the MR data constrain about their ratio as a function of x. This is what would carry the MR-based claim beyond the existing A1–Tc correlation.

7. **Non-orbital and superconducting-fluctuation contributions at the fitting temperatures are not quantified.** The anisotropy is inferred from the MR shrinking as T falls, but a negative isotropic spin-scattering MR (invoked below x_FSR and possibly present more weakly above it) or superconducting fluctuations could also reduce the MR. The longitudinal-MR control (Extended Data Fig. 2) is at 75 K only, for three channels with x ≥ 0.159, and is already slightly negative; the Methods say that "at lower temperatures a non-zero MR appears that we attribute to the emergence of SC fluctuations". Yet g(x) is fixed at 40 K, Δρ/ρ curves are fitted down to 20 K (Fig. 4), and Tc is 20–25 K at x = 0.116–0.13 and still finite at x = 0.159. The authors should show H ∥ I MR at 20–40 K across x, including x < 0.15; estimate the paraconductivity contribution and its field suppression at 40 K and at the lowest temperatures in Figs. 4 and 5 (for example from the field scale at which dρ/dH becomes linear); and either subtract these contributions or show they are negligible compared with the orbital signal used to fix g(x).

8. **The absolute resistivity of the combinatorial films, which enters g(x) directly, has an uncertain geometric calibration.** The S2 resistivity had to be divided by 1.5 to match S1 at the shared composition x = 0.159 below 70 K (Methods "Zero-field resistivity"; Extended Data Fig. 1a), and the combinatorial-film R_H values in Extended Data Fig. 4b are about 1.6 times those of non-combinatorial films (Extended Data Fig. 4a). Both point to a thickness or geometric-factor uncertainty of that size. Because absolute ρ(0, 40 K) is one of the two targets that set g(x) for S1 (concern 3), and the S2 values are normalized to S1 at one point, channel-to-channel thickness variation along the gradient maps directly into g(x). The authors should give the thickness profile across S1 and S2 and its uncertainty, state how the geometric factors were determined, and show how a channel-to-channel geometric error of, say, 10–20% propagates into g(x).

9. **Doping, oxygen state and Tc of each channel are not documented.** The nominal Ce content per channel comes from its position on a composition-spread film; how x was verified (c-axis lattice parameter, EDX) and its absolute uncertainty are not given, and it is unclear whether the quoted Δx ≲ 0.003 is a step size or an accuracy. In electron-doped cuprates the effective doping depends strongly on oxygen content and annealing, and uniformity of the reduction across the film is not described (the paper refers to ref. 10). The Tc values on the top axis of Fig. 5c are not shown as data, and zero-field ρ(T) through Tc is shown only for S2 (Extended Data Fig. 1b); S1's ρ(T) is known only at discrete temperatures, so A1 for the S1 channels, where most of the g variation lies, is not measured directly and the comparison with Fig. 1 relies on ref. 10. The authors should show ρ(T) through Tc for all channels, state the Tc criterion, tabulate measured Tc alongside g(x), and give the x calibration with its uncertainty.

10. **The fits are not unique, and the sensitivity of g(x) to the fixed model parameters is not shown.** The authors call the parameterisations of τ_HS⁻¹(φ) and β(φ) "somewhat arbitrary": the sin¹² exponent, HS = 0.48, a hotspot height of 50 ps⁻¹ and a width of 0.05 (rad). The hotspot term is taken as T-independent without physical justification, though the Methods mention a √T dependence as one possibility, and it is held fixed in height and width across all x even though the spin correlation length, and hence hotspot strength, should change with doping. Fig. 4 shows a fit only at x = 0.159 over 20–50 K, and in Fig. 5b the simulated Δρ/ρ(0) falls systematically below the data at x ≈ 0.12–0.14. The authors should report how the exponent and HS were chosen; show how g(x) and its trend change when the exponent, hotspot width, height and position are varied within ranges that still fit x = 0.159, when the hotspot term is allowed to vary with x, and when it is given a T dependence; give a goodness-of-fit measure for Fig. 4 and Fig. 5a,b; and comment on the systematic shortfall in Fig. 5b.

11. **The conclusion about hole-doped cuprates in the abstract goes beyond the evidence.** The statement that the higher Tc of p-type cuprates "cannot be attributed solely to an increase in the coupling strength" to AFM fluctuations (abstract; final section) rests on the absence of a successful Boltzmann parameterisation of hole-doped MR, which is an absence of a model, not a measurement. The final paragraphs also invoke larger U/t, a different Fermi-surface geometry and a dual coherent/incoherent response, any of which could break the analogy without bearing on the coupling, and Tc also depends on the spin-fluctuation energy scale. The authors should present this as a hypothesis in a clearly speculative outlook and reserve the abstract for what the LCCO data establish, or support it with a direct test, for example applying the same model to Tl2201 or Bi2201 MR data and showing quantitatively where it fails, or comparing the hotspot λ inferred in LCCO with what the same analysis gives for a p-type compound.

14. **A composition gradient within each channel could contribute H-linear MR that biases the hotspot parameters.** The Methods note that compositional variation along a channel can produce H-linear MR through Hall-voltage inhomogeneity, and answer that H-linear MR also appears in non-combinatorial films (ref. 24). The hotspot term τ_HS⁻¹ was introduced specifically to keep the MR H-linear, so if part of that linearity is extrinsic, the fixed hotspot parameters, and hence g, are biased. The authors should estimate Δx along a single channel and the expected size of the inhomogeneity-induced linear MR, and compare it with the measured slope; a comparison of MR(H) with a single-composition film would also address this.
