**A. Must address**

1. **The step from a correlation to "the primary pairing glue" has no quantitative coupling behind it.** The abstract and conclusions (pp. 1, 8–9) say that the correlation between α, λsf and Tc "identifies low-energy spin-fluctuations as the primary pairing glue". However, λsf is never defined or extracted. g(x) is dimensionless and normalised to 1 at x = 0.159. No McMillan-, Eliashberg- or spin-fluctuation-exchange estimate shows that the inferred coupling gives a Tc of the right size or the right d-wave symmetry. The relation between g and Tc is also not proportional. In Fig. 5c and Table 4, g(x) saturates at about 0.55 for x = 0.184 and 0.188, where Tc reaches zero, so the coupling does not vanish with Tc. No plot of Tc against g appears, and threshold behaviour (a μ*-like term) is not discussed. The symmetry question is not trivial either. In the authors' own picture the hotspots lie much closer to the nodes than to the antinodes (p. 21, "the node is three times closer to the AFM boundary"). For a d_{x²−y²} gap, (π,π) scattering between near-nodal points couples regions where the gap is small. Finally, a correlation cannot rule out a common driver, such as proximity to the AFM/FSR critical point controlling both the scattering and Tc through the density of states or another channel. The paper's motivating analogy (Allen, Fig. 7) compares absolute λtr with λph, and that comparison is missing here. The authors should convert g(x)α1 into a dimensionless, Fermi-surface-averaged transport coupling, λtr(x) = ħ⟨dΓ/dT⟩/(2πkB), quoted both at the hotspots and averaged, with uncertainties. They should plot Tc against g(x) and λtr directly, alongside the Allen-type comparison of Fig. 7, and compare with weak-coupling spin-fluctuation expectations (Refs. 13, 55, 56). They should estimate the d-wave-projected pairing coupling from the same Γ(φ), compare the implied Tc(x) with the measured dome, and discuss whether a finite coupling at Tc = 0 is consistent with the proposed mechanism. Failing this, the title, abstract and conclusions should be reworded to a correlation between the anisotropic T-linear scattering and Tc that is consistent with spin-fluctuation pairing.

2. **The (π,π) spin-fluctuation origin of the anisotropic T-linear term is put into the model, not drawn from the data.** The hotspot positions at the AFM zone-boundary crossings are imposed in Eq. (1), with HS = 27.5° fixed. The envelope exponent ν = 12 is borrowed from hole-doped Tl2201 fits [19] and called "somewhat arbitrary" (p. 6). The hotspot height and width are fixed (Table 2), and the T² term is made anisotropic "for simplicity". The alternatives in Fig. 9 are single, apparently unoptimised trial forms, tried at one doping (x = 0.159) and 20–50 K only, and judged "inferior" by eye with no goodness-of-fit measure. Other T-dependent anisotropies are not excluded. Near-nodal scattering is dismissed on p. 15 only because no wavevector connects the nodes. Anisotropic electron–phonon scattering, or an angle-independent T-linear rate combined with a T-dependent hotspot width, are not tested at all. The MR on its own does not discriminate the microscopic boson: as the authors note on p. 23, Boltzmann transport "provides no direct information" on spin versus other bosons. As things stand, the data show that "an anisotropic T-linear rate somewhere between node and antinode" fits, not that AFM hotspots do. The authors should treat the peak position of the anisotropic term and ν as free parameters, and report the best-fit position with an uncertainty at several x, including whether it tracks the Fermi-surface/AFM-zone-boundary crossing as that moves with x. They should optimise the parameters of each Fig. 9 alternative, and of the near-nodal and phonon-like forms above, then give χ² or residuals for Fig. 4 and for every alternative at several dopings and temperatures. This would show whether the hotspot-peaked solution is a well-defined optimum or one of a family of adequate fits.

**B. Should address**

3. **g(x) is close to a renamed A1, so the MR's independent contribution to the g(x)–Tc result is not shown.** The paper calls g(x) "the first key result" (p. 7) and "an effective coupling parameter for both transport and superconductivity" (Fig. 5 caption). But g(x) is the only parameter allowed to vary with x, and it is assigned by construction to the T-linear term (p. 10: "This is the main reason why the coupling strength g(x) was assigned uniquely to the T-linear term"). All other parameters stay at their x = 0.159 values (Table 2). For S1, g(x) is set by matching ρ(0, 40 K) and Δρ/ρ(0) at 20 T with one free parameter (Fig. 5a,b; Table 3), and in practice mainly by ρ. For S2 it is the T-linear coefficient A1 of ρ(0,T), normalised at x = 0.159 (Fig. 6e, Table 4). The A1–Tc correlation in LCCO was already reported by Yuan et al. [5] (Fig. 1 inset). The MR fixes the form of the anisotropy at one doping, but it does not independently fix the trend in strength. The authors should state explicitly what is new relative to Ref. 5. They should determine g(x) for S1 separately from (i) the MR alone, with ρ0 free, and (ii) A1 alone, and compare the two. They should also overlay the Ref. 5 A1/Tc data on the same normalised axes as g(x) against Tc.

4. **The Hall-effect argument for placing the scattering at the (π,π) hotspots is only qualitative.** It is not compared with R_H(T, x). The main text (p. 8) claims the model captures "the key features of R_H(T,x) beyond x > x_FSR, including the tendency of R_H to go negative with increasing T", and the Hall analysis is offered as "the second key finding" (p. 7). Yet Fig. 13 gives only four single R_H values, for four forms of Γ(φ), at x ≈ 0.16 and an unstated temperature. No R_H(T) curve and no x dependence is computed, no sign change is produced, and nothing is overlaid on Fig. 11. The CVC model also has several uncontrolled ingredients:
   - ξ_AFM and c are unknown;
   - ε(φ) is fixed as a Gaussian of unit height and width σ = 0.07 (Eq. 13), so the size of the vertex correction is effectively a free choice;
   - the 1/(1−ε²) prefactor is dropped;
   - Eq. (2)/(11) writes ε ≈ 1 − cξ²_AFM, which decreases as the correlation length grows, the opposite of the physical expectation (presumably a typo for ξ⁻², but it matters for the claimed T dependence);
   - the low-field Jones–Zener expansion is used, although R_H was measured at 14 T;
   - the Hall calculation uses an analytic k_F(φ), v_F(φ) (Eqs. 7–8, with a modified antinodal v_F), while the MR fits use the Table 1 tight-binding Fermi surface.

   The case against antinodal (charge-fluctuation) scattering rests on a single comparison (1.03×10⁻⁹ vs 4.3×10⁻¹⁰ m³/C), whose outcome depends on these choices. The Hall data in Fig. 11 also come from other films, with an unexplained multiplicative factor between data sets. The authors should compute R_H(T) over 20–100 K at two to four dopings above x_FSR. The calculation should use the same Fermi surface and the same Γ(φ,T,x), including g(x)α1T + α2T², that fit the MR, and the result should be overlaid on Fig. 11b. They should show how sensitive the sign change is to ε amplitude, σ, ξ_AFM and the dropped prefactor, state how ε and σ were chosen, and say so if only a narrow band of CVC parameters works. They should also show that no reasonable antinodal (charge or phonon) scenario with CVCs reproduces the trend. Ideally, R_H would be measured on the same S1/S2 channels during the same high-field runs and fitted jointly with ρ(H,T) and the MR.

5. **The MR magnitude, the quantity that fixes g(x) and the anisotropy, is not reproducible between films.** Only two films are measured, and each composition is measured on one channel of one film. The films overlap only at x = 0.159. There, the S2 resistivity has to be divided by 1.5 to match S1 (Fig. 6a), and the S2 MR is about 3.5 times smaller than the S1 MR (Fig. 10c,d). The S2 MR also carries a low-field negative component of unspecified origin, in a range beyond x_FSR where long-range AFM is absent. Absolute resistivities are about one third of those in Ref. 5 (Fig. 6b). The central modelling (Fig. 4, Fig. 5b, Table 2) fixes the anisotropy parameters from the S1 MR magnitude at x = 0.159. If the same nominal composition gives a 3.5× different MR in a second film, then either some sample-dependent extrinsic contribution affects the MR, or one film is not representative. Possible extrinsic sources include compositional inhomogeneity within a bridge, oxygen content, the negative MR term and geometry. In addition, the S2 half of g(x), which carries the approach to Tc → 0 between 0.159 and 0.188, rests on A1 alone and cannot be checked against the MR. The authors should identify the origin of the S2 negative MR, using its T and field-orientation dependence, and explain why S1 lacks it or has it less visibly. They should report the channel-to-channel spread within S1. They should also measure MR on at least one more film, ideally non-combinatorial (for example the x ≈ 0.15–0.16 films of Refs. 29, 31), or on a film covering part of 0.12–0.16, to establish which magnitude is intrinsic and that g(x) is reproducible.

6. **Most of the order-of-magnitude drop in g(x) comes from dopings where the model's assumptions fail.** The paper places x_FSR at 0.14. Below it there is a negative isotropic MR from AFM order (Fig. 2, Fig. 15) and a reconstructed Fermi surface (Fig. 1). Yet the S1 fits over 0.116–0.159 use the unreconstructed tight-binding Fermi surface of Table 1, a hotspot height fixed at all x, and one free parameter. In Table 3, g rises from 1.87 at x = 0.141 to 6.50 at x = 0.116, which accounts for most of the "order of magnitude". The authors themselves suggest (p. 16) that sharper or stronger hotspots below x = 0.14 could produce this rise, so g(x) at low x may not measure the same quantity as at high x. The Fig. 5 caption's statement that the fit is restricted to where AFM signatures are "absent or weak" does not hold for x < 0.14. The authors should refit the x < x_FSR channels with a reconstructed or partially gapped Fermi surface, or with the hotspot parameters free, and report how g(x) changes. They should also state the variation of g(x) over x > x_FSR alone (roughly 1.9 to 0.55, a factor of about 3.4) and say whether the correlation with Tc holds there by itself.

7. **The identifiability of the parameters and the uncertainty of g(x) are not reported.** At x = 0.159 the model has τimp, α1, α2, the hotspot height and width (Table 2), HS = 27.5° and ν = 12. 8. **The fixed τimp and the uncertain absolute resistivity are absorbed into g(x).** τimp⁻¹ is held at 27 ps⁻¹ for all x, and g(x) is tuned to reproduce ρ(0, 40 K), which depends on film thickness and channel geometry. Any doping dependence of the residual resistivity, or of the geometric factor along the film, therefore ends up in g(x). There is no reason the residual resistivity of a combinatorial film should be independent of x, and Fig. 6d indicates that ρ0 varies across the S2 channels. For S1, no continuous ρ(T) exists (p. 10) and ρ0(x) is not shown. Absolute values are uncertain as well: S1 is about one third of Ref. 5, S2 is rescaled by 1.5, and the Hall magnitudes differ similarly between film sets (Fig. 11). The absolute scattering rates in Table 2, including α1 and the Planckian comparison, therefore carry a factor of roughly 1.5–3 uncertainty. In addition, Fig. 5b shows the simulation systematically underestimating Δρ/ρ(0), so the MR is in practice fitted less faithfully than ρ. The authors should state the thickness uniformity along the composition gradient and the uncertainty of each channel's geometric factor. They should extract ρ0(x) and A1(x) for each S1 channel from the zero-field points in Fig. 6b and refit with ρ0 (or τimp) free per channel, or following ρ0(x). They should then show g(x) with uncertainty bands that include the geometric-factor uncertainty, and give the ratio of fitted to measured MR for each channel.

9. **An isotropic negative MR component may be mistaken for a temperature-dependent scattering anisotropy.** The feature that drives the ansatz (point 2 on p. 5) is that the MR shrinks on cooling below about 60 K. The paper also documents a negative, isotropic spin MR below x_FSR. It also shows a low-field negative component in every S2 channel (Fig. 10c,d), and mentions a non-zero longitudinal MR at lower temperatures (p. 12), attributed to superconducting fluctuations but not shown. The longitudinal MR is shown only at 75 K (Fig. 8), whereas the fits use 20–50 K. A negative component that grows on cooling and persists, weakened, beyond x_FSR would reduce the low-T MR without any change in the anisotropy of the inelastic scattering. The authors should show the longitudinal (H ∥ I ∥ ab) MR at 20–50 K for several x in both films. They should subtract any isotropic component and repeat the Fig. 4 and Fig. 5b analysis on the corrected transverse MR.

10. **Superconducting fluctuations may contribute at the lowest fitted temperatures.** Tc reaches about 25 K for the lowest x. The fits use 20–50 K MR (Fig. 4), the doping-dependent fits use 40 K throughout Fig. 5, and MR isotherms extend to 30 K. For x = 0.116 these temperatures lie within about 1.2–1.6 Tc. Paraconductivity and fluctuation MR are positive and strongly T dependent near Tc, and they are not in the Boltzmann model. The paper itself attributes the low-T longitudinal MR to superconducting fluctuations (p. 12). 13. **The Planckian statement needs numbers.** The paper states (p. 8) that the T-linear scattering rate varies by almost an order of magnitude across the series and therefore "cannot be tied to the Planckian limit". The Planckian bound concerns the Fermi-surface-averaged transport rate, ħ/τ = αkBT with α ~ 1, so a relative change in g(x) or A1 does not test it. In Refs. 14 and 15, A1 also varies, through m* and n. From Table 2, α1 = 0.75 ps⁻¹K⁻¹ is about 5.7 kB/ħ at the hotspot peak. The sin¹²(2φ) envelope averages to about 0.23, which gives an averaged slope of about 0.17 ps⁻¹K⁻¹ (≈ 1.3 kB/ħ) at x = 0.159, and about 6.5 times that at x = 0.116. The authors should report the Fermi-surface-averaged and nodal T-linear coefficients α = ħ⟨dΓ/dT⟩/kB for each x, with the band mass and with the thickness-calibration uncertainty (concern 8). They should then state precisely what is and is not inconsistent with Planckian scattering, in comparison with Refs. 14 and 19.

15. **The persistent H-linear MR may be partly extrinsic, from the composition gradient across each bridge.** The model attributes the high-field H-linearity to hotspots impeding cyclotron motion, and H-linearity is one of the ingredients that motivates the hotspot term (point 3, p. 5). The authors acknowledge (p. 8) that it is not obvious why the H-linearity persists to the lowest T, and they raise compositional variation (the Bruls/Herring mechanism, Refs. 50, 51) as an alternative. In a combinatorial film, each microbridge necessarily spans a finite Δx (the paper suggests Δx ≲ 0.003). If part of the H-linear slope is extrinsic, the hotspot parameters fitted to it are biased. The authors should give Δx across each voltage-probe pair and estimate the inhomogeneity-induced MR for that gradient. They should also compare the high-field slope quantitatively with that of a uniform film at the same x (Ref. 29), rather than only asserting agreement.
