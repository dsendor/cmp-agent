## A. Must address

1. **The region swept by the tip is not shown to still be in the vortex phase, so the LPFM boundary is not shown to be a vortex boundary.** The identification of dark and bright LPFM phase regions as zigzag vortex arrays of opposite net in-plane polarization (Fig. 1a, Fig. 2l) rests on LPFM phase contrast alone, and Fig. 1a was taken on the pristine sample. The vortex period is ~14 nm and the tip radius is 25 nm (Methods), so LPFM cannot resolve individual vortices; the phase reports only the net in-plane polarization along one cantilever axis. After PD1–PD7 the swept area is uniformly dark (Figs. 2e, 3d–e; uniform bright band in Fig. 3h). That is equally consistent with a single-sense zigzag vortex array, a conventional in-plane (a-type) or a/c ferroelectric domain, or a mixed vortex/a-domain state. A +5 V bias across a ~60 nm trilayer on SrRuO3 gives a nominal vertical field approaching 1 MV/cm, above the fields at which PTO/STO vortex phases are known to transform into polar phases. The only structural evidence for the vortex picture under field is the in-situ STEM on a FIB lamella (Fig. 2j), which differs from the PFM case in electrostatic boundary conditions, field direction relative to the tubes, field magnitude (+17 V from a tungsten probe), lamella thickness and field of view (~100 nm); it does not show the state of an AFM-written region. In addition, the STEM shows zigzag converting to a "near-flat" configuration (Fig. 2j-II/III), whereas the PFM interpretation and Fig. 2l require switching between two zigzag states of opposite net in-plane polarization; a flat arrangement would carry roughly zero net in-plane polarization, so it is unclear which state each LPFM contrast represents. The authors should cut a cross-sectional lamella across a PFM-written boundary (for example from the Fig. 4 region) and show by polarization mapping, side by side with an unwritten region, that vortices persist on both sides with opposite zigzag sense or opposite net in-plane polarization; or provide plane-view STEM, RSM/micro-area nano-diffraction or SHG of a written area showing that the ~14 nm periodicity survives. They should state which core configuration corresponds to each LPFM contrast, and quantify the net in-plane polarization from the STEM polar maps (averaged P_x over regions of each zigzag phase) to show its sign matches the LPFM contrast. Without this, "polar vortex boundary motion" in the title and abstract is an interpretation, not an observation, and the boundary could equally be a tip-written ferroelectric domain wall or a vortex/non-vortex phase boundary.

2. **The data do not separate collective motion of a pre-existing boundary from local writing of in-plane polarization along each tip path.** In Figs. 2a–h and 3, each successive path (PD2, PD3, …; ND1, …) appears to be drawn at or just beyond the boundary left by the previous pass, and after PD1 the whole area between the left edge and the boundary becomes uniform (Figs. 2b, 3b). The reported displacement then grows with the stepped path position. This is what is expected when a biased tip writes a stripe whose width is set by its trailing in-plane field, a known effect in BiFeO3 and other films that needs neither vortices nor a collective response. Displacements of 256 nm (Fig. 2b) to 660 nm (Fig. 3b) per pass from a tip whose field, by the paper's own FEM (Supplementary Fig. 4) and the 25 nm radius, is confined to tens of nanometres imply a strongly non-local response, which the Discussion asserts ("long-range modifications across hundreds of nanometers, reflecting the inherently collective and nonlocal nature") but the figures cannot test. The claimed novelty, controlled motion of a topological boundary as an entity, depends on this distinction. The authors should give, for every pass and with a sketch, the x-position of the tip path and of the boundary before and after the pass, and state where each displacement arrow is measured from (the arrows in Figs. 2–3 begin at different positions). They should run single passes at fixed offsets from the boundary (in front of, on, behind, and far from any boundary) and report displacement as a function of tip–boundary distance, showing whether the boundary moves beyond the swept line or untouched regions reorganize. Alternatively they should provide a physical argument or simulation for the long-range propagation.

## B. Should address

3. **Vortex specificity is not tested against a non-vortex reference.** The claim that vortex topology underlies the behaviour ("topologically protected motion", Results p. 3; "owing to the topological protection", p. 6) needs the same trailing-field protocol, under matched tip, bias, speed and humidity, on a film without vortices: a thicker PTO film or single PTO layer with a1/a2 or a/c domains, or a PTO/STO superlattice in the a1/a2 phase. Trailing-field, tip-direction-dependent writing and motion of in-plane domain walls is known in conventional ferroelectric films, and this prior work is not cited or discussed. If the same behaviour appears in the reference, the vortex-specific interpretation should be scaled back.
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

4. **The origin of the LPFM contrast after writing is not pinned down, and electrostatic and electrochemical artifacts are not excluded.** No vertical PFM is shown, although the tip applies a strong out-of-plane field that could convert regions to c-domains or to a polar or a1/a2 state. Only one cantilever orientation is used; vector PFM with the sample rotated 90° is needed to establish the in-plane polarization direction relative to the vortex-tube axis and the tip path. Amplitude images after writing are not shown (Fig. 1a gives amplitude only for the pristine state). Biased-tip passes at ±5 V in air inject surface charge and can drive electrochemical surface changes, either of which can alter LPFM phase and could contribute to the retention. The authors should show vertical and vector PFM, amplitude, topography before and after writing, KPFM or surface-potential maps after writing, LPFM at several drive amplitudes and frequencies to show the contrast is independent of them, and the leakage current through the conductive tip during writing (also relevant to Joule heating).

5. **"Deterministic" and "controlled" motion are claimed without parameter dependences or null controls, and the displacement per pass is not reproducible.** All motion data use ±5 V at 50 nm/s (Methods). Under nominally identical conditions the first positive pass gives 256 nm in Fig. 2b but 660 nm in Fig. 3b, and increments are strongly non-uniform (660 → 720 nm after PD2, then 1460 nm after PD6); the text quotes ranges ("~1170–2000 nm", "~928–1400 nm") without saying what sets the spread. The authors should report displacement per pass versus bias magnitude (including the threshold below which no motion occurs), tip speed, contact force, path length and number of repeated passes along the same line, with error bars from several runs and regions. They should add a 0 V pass along the same path (to exclude mechanical or flexoelectric switching by tip loading) and an imaging-only control in which the region is scanned repeatedly at the 0.6–1.25 V drive. At present only the sign symmetry supports the trailing-field mechanism; these data would give the quantitative relation between trailing field and displacement that "deterministic control" implies and that any device claim needs.

6. **The in-situ STEM experiment is a different process from the PFM experiment, is internally inconsistently described, and lacks controls.** The STEM uses a cross-sectional lamella, a +17 V single-polarity field from a tungsten probe essentially along [001] at the lamella edge, whereas the PFM trailing field is in-plane along the scan direction. Fig. 2j mainly shows a local flattening of the zigzag (red → yellow cores) beneath the probe over a few vortex pairs, with a boundary at the edge of the flattened region, not a reversal of zigzag sense propagating laterally; the flattened segment's displacement is not quantified. The text describes the change as "CCW/up–CW/down to a near flat-configuration with CCW/down–CW/up", which is both "near flat" and a reversed zigzag, while the caption calls the initial state "up-down" and says the switching "reverses the zigzag vortex arrangement". Reversal by negative bias is shown only in simulation (Fig. 2k IV–V). The 5 V vs 17 V difference is attributed to "differences in the sample environment" and a dielectric gap (refs. 25, 26) without an estimate, and FIB damage, which the text concedes may alter boundary conditions, and beam effects raise the question of whether the rearrangement is intrinsic. The authors should reconcile the descriptions, quantify the vertical core displacement across the boundary before and after biasing, show experimental reversal under negative bias and repeated cycles in both polarities, report the number of lamellae and events and the probe apex used for Fig. 2j, estimate the local field in the PTO layer in both geometries, and qualify the statement that STEM "confirmed the controlled motion".

7. **"Topological protection" is inferred from retention alone, and the retention is not quantified.** The 120-day retention (Fig. 4) is presented as evidence of topological protection "unlike conventional ferroelectric domains", which "readily relax back", and the motion is called "topologically protected". But tip-written in-plane and out-of-plane domains in epitaxial PTO and BiFeO3 films often persist for months at room temperature, and a zigzag reversal does not change vortex winding; no topological invariant or protection test is presented. Fig. 4 is a single region, storage conditions (atmosphere, humidity, temperature) are not given, boundary position is not plotted versus time, image registration and drift are not addressed, and unmoved domains elsewhere in Fig. 4a–f also appear to evolve. The authors should plot boundary position versus time with uncertainty, compare it with the natural evolution of unwritten domains, repeat in another region or sample, run the same retention test on a written conventional-domain reference, and test thermal stability (anneal or thermal cycling, which would also help separate trapped injected charge from intrinsic stability). Otherwise the protection and "conventional domains relax" statements should be removed.

8. **Reproducibility and statistics are not reported.** Only one trilayer sample is described, Figs. 2, 3a–h, 3i–p and 4 appear to be single realizations from three regions (only "a new region" is mentioned for Fig. 4), and tips used are not stated. Displacements carry no uncertainty although the boundaries are rough (Figs. 2e, 3d) and drift between scans is not addressed. The authors should report the number of samples, regions and tips; success and failure rates, including failed or irreversible attempts and whether full restoration (Supplementary Fig. 6) works every time; displacement-per-pass histograms; and how boundary position was defined (for example the mean of the traced contour relative to fixed topographic markers) with its error. A second sample would strengthen the result.

9. **The orientation of the tip path, cantilever axis and vortex tubes is not stated.** Fig. 2l depicts a field along the tubes and Fig. 2m claims motion for fields perpendicular to them, but for Figs. 2–4 the paper does not give the crystallographic direction of the tip path, the tube axis ([100] or [010] on anisotropic DyScO3), or the cantilever axis. The trailing-field mechanism and the claim that tip trajectory controls the motion depend on this. The authors should mark crystallographic axes, cantilever orientation and tube direction on each LPFM figure, state which in-plane component the LPFM phase reports, and if possible show motion for paths both parallel and perpendicular to the tubes.

10. **The finite-element tip model does not describe the sample and puts the trailing-field asymmetry in by hand.** The model treats the film as a 100 nm isotropic dielectric with ε_r = 100 on a grounded substrate, whereas the stack is ~24 nm (60 uc) of active film on an SrRuO3 electrode with large, strongly anisotropic permittivity in the vortex phase; the modelled tip radius is 20 nm (vs 25 nm) with a 0.1 nm tip height. The leading/trailing asymmetry comes from "a small perturbation … applied to the tip boundary potential based on the probe's motion direction", so the direction dependence follows by construction and the FEM cannot independently support a trailing field of a given sign or magnitude. The authors should model the real stack and identify and estimate the physical origin of the trailing component (for example from the field history given finite switching time and tip speed, injected surface charge and screening left behind the tip, or water-meniscus effects), ideally testing its velocity dependence; otherwise they should present the FEM as qualitative only.

11. **The phase-field model has fitted, undocumented parameters, too small a domain, and a mislabelled energy barrier.** The DMI-like coefficients D_N = 0.1 and D_B = 0.3 have no units and were chosen "to provide a quantitatively consistent description" of experiment; it is not shown whether the zigzag ground state and the boundary motion survive with D = 0 or over a range of coefficients. Landau, elastic and electrostrictive coefficients are deferred to the literature. The representation of the moving tip (field magnitude, spatial profile, timescale relative to 50 nm/s) is not given. The 160 × 80 nm box reproduces shifts of only tens of nanometres, so it does not address the micrometre-scale motion that is the headline result, and the comparison with experiment is qualitative. The "energy barrier" in Supplementary Fig. 9 is the energy along a field-driven TDGL trajectory, not a minimum-energy path, and should not be called a barrier between "nearly degenerate configurations" without a string/NEB calculation. The authors should list all parameters in the SI and state which were fitted, show results with and without the DMI term, specify the simulated field and compare it with the FEM estimate, simulate a moving tip over a domain comparable to the experimental displacement, and compare at least one quantity (threshold field or displacement per pass) with experiment.

12. **The SS-PFM coercive-voltage evidence (Fig. 1b,c) is internally inconsistent, lacks statistics and is only loosely connected to the mechanism.** The Fig. 1b map is on a −50 to 200 mV scale, while the loops in Fig. 1c switch at roughly −1.5 to +2.5 V; a quantity on the mV scale looks like an imprint, (V_C+ + V_C−)/2, which does not by itself imply easier switching at boundaries. Only two loops are shown, each averaged over 10 points with no spread. If boundary coercive biases really were tens to hundreds of mV, imaging at 0.6–1.25 V drive and SS-PFM at 650 mV would themselves move the boundaries. The loops presumably probe out-of-plane switching, whereas the motion concerns in-plane polarization. The authors should clarify the mode, quantity and units in Fig. 1b, show distributions of V_C+ and V_C− for boundary and interior pixels with standard deviations, state which branch defines the "lower coercive bias", address electrostatic contributions to the loops (off-field loops or KPFM), show that repeated imaging at 1.25 V does not shift the boundary, and explain why a lower out-of-plane coercive bias at boundaries governs in-plane boundary motion.
