A. Must address

1. The "vortex boundary motion" is not separated from ordinary tip-induced domain writing. In Figs. 2a–i, 3a–f and 4a–b, the region the biased tip has swept becomes uniformly bright in LPFM amplitude, with uniform phase and no dark texture, and the edge of that band is then called the "vortex boundary". The channels (CHP1…CHP6 in Fig. 2a–e; CHP1, CHP2, CHP4 in Fig. 3b–d) sit at successively more rightward positions, and the new boundary appears close to the most recently written channel. The rightward "motion" may therefore simply be the written region extending to wherever the tip was dragged next, as in conventional PFM lithography, with the −5 V reverse sweep writing the opposite state. The text and Methods say channels were used "one at a time" but give no channel coordinates. The Conclusion's claim of "long-range modifications across hundreds of nanometers, reflecting the inherently collective and nonlocal nature" requires showing that the boundary moves beyond the tip trajectory, or moves when the tip is not over it, and that vortices displaced from the swept area reappear beyond it; otherwise "motion" cannot be distinguished from "erasure and re-nucleation". The in-situ STEM sequences ending with "all vortex cores in a single orientation" (Fig. 3g IV) or a reoriented array (Fig. 4g) also look like reorientation or conversion rather than translation. The authors should: state the channel coordinates relative to the boundary for every step (Supplementary Figs. 4–7) and overlay the tip path on each post-write image; report boundary position against channel position and displacement per step against channel spacing; apply the bias repeatedly through the same channel position to see whether the boundary keeps advancing; place a channel at a fixed distance (several hundred nm) from, or parallel to, an existing boundary and report boundary displacement against tip–boundary distance; and perform the same writing on a uniform a1/a2 or c-domain region, or a reference PTO layer without vortices, to show what ordinary domain writing looks like in this system.

2. What the PFM contrast represents, and what the written region contains, is never established. The vortex period is about 10–14 nm (RSM, Fig. 1a,b), while the PFM tip radius is 25 nm (Methods), yet the PFM "vortices" and "vortex boundaries" in Figs. 1c–d, 2, 3 and 5 are dark meandering lines and patches hundreds of nanometres wide, imaged in 2–5 µm scans. Individual vortex cores cannot be resolved, so the contrast must report a mesoscale property: arrays of vortices of a given orientation, axial polarization or chirality (net in-plane polarization of the array, as in refs. 1, 23), or boundaries between vortex and a1/a2/FE phase regions. The text mixes these levels ("the low amplitude of these vortices results in their distinct dark contrast in phase images"; "the dark contrast in the amplitude represents the boundary of the vortex"). After writing, the featureless region could be a vortex array of different orientation or chirality, an a1/a2, in-plane or out-of-plane c-type ferroelectric phase created by the field (field-driven vortex→FE conversion is known in this system, ref. 23), or a region dominated by injected surface charge. No data show that the swept region still contains vortices with the original ~10–14 nm period, and the negative sweeps leave a disrupted texture (Fig. 3f; Fig. 2i differs from Fig. 2a) rather than the original one. Since the headline claim depends on this assignment, the authors should state explicitly which polar degree of freedom the contrast tracks; acquire vertical PFM alongside LPFM for every step and LPFM at two sample rotations (0° and 90°) to reconstruct in-plane and out-of-plane components inside and outside the swept region; support the assignment with co-located high-resolution PFM; and, most decisively, cut a FIB lamella across a PFM-written edge (and from a pristine region) and image it by STEM with polarization mapping or DF-TEM, or use local XRD/nano-diffraction satellite peaks, to show whether vortices are present on both sides of the boundary and what distinguishes the two sides.

3. Reversibility, a central claim, is overstated and the numbers are internally inconsistent. The abstract and Conclusion say reversing the bias "successfully returned the boundaries to their initial positions", and p. 7 says it can "effectively move the boundary to its original position". But the trilayer moves ~1170 nm forward and ~890 nm back (Fig. 2e vs 2i), and the superlattice ~1170 nm forward and ~643 nm back (Fig. 3 caption), with the boundary "disrupted" after CHN3 (Fig. 3f) and "disruptions observed after several field cycles" (p. 8). Full return is not demonstrated in either sample. The identical ~1170 nm forward figure for two different samples with different numbers of steps (CHP6 vs CHP7) needs explaining, including whether it is a coincidence of channel layout. The authors should plot boundary position against channel index for every forward and reverse step in both samples, with the measurement method (line profiles) and error bars from boundary tracing and image registration (drift correction against topographic markers); overlay pristine and final boundary positions and quantify the residual offset; compare the restored texture with the pristine one (e.g. 2D FFT period and orientation of the PFM amplitude); take at least one region through several full forward/back cycles, in at least one further region or sample, and report the number of cycles and success rate. Otherwise the claim should be reworded as "partially reversible".

B. Should address

4. The claim that bias polarity sets the direction of boundary motion has no mechanism or control. The field under a biased PFM tip is mainly out-of-plane and radially symmetric, and the paper does not explain how a vertical field of one sign pushes a lateral boundary right and the opposite sign pushes it left. The direction may instead come from the order and position of the channels, which the authors step rightward (or leftward) at each step. The authors should apply negative bias with channels stepped rightward and positive bias with channels stepped leftward, report the result, and give the scan direction of the tip within each channel.
Raised by: r1

5. Charge injection, electrostatic and electrochemical artefacts in PFM are not excluded. Writing at ±5 V for one minute (and up to 7 V for 1–3 min in Fig. 5) under ambient conditions can inject surface, interface or bulk charge, cause electrochemical changes (especially in Pb-containing oxides) or leave water-meniscus residues, all of which produce strong DART amplitude and phase contrast, including lateral signal via cantilever buckling and electrostatic crosstalk. The 120-day retention argues against purely transient charge but does not show the contrast is vortex-related. Humidity, surface-potential maps and topography before and after writing are not reported for any channel. In addition, the Methods give two imaging drive amplitudes (0.6–1.25 V and 0.75–1.25 V); 1.25 V may itself perturb a low-coercivity vortex state. The authors should provide KPFM of written areas over time; PFM at several drive amplitudes and frequencies showing the contrast is piezoelectric; repeated imaging with no writing (e.g. 0.6 V versus 1.25 V on the same area) to show imaging does not move boundaries; a write experiment in controlled atmosphere, after deliberate surface discharging, or followed by surface cleaning or a mild anneal; humidity values; and topography before and after writing.
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

6. The in-situ STEM experiments use a different field geometry, boundary conditions and length scale from the PFM experiments, and the link is asserted rather than argued. They apply +17 V (Fig. 2j), +20 V (Fig. 3g) and +15 V (Fig. 4g) through a tungsten tip on thinned FIB lamellae against an SRO ground; the authors acknowledge that thinning removes the a1/a2 superdomains (p. 5). The field distribution, its in-plane component and any current are unknown. Fig. 2j shows a rearrangement of core heights from zig-zag ("up-down-up") to linear over ~10–50 nm; Figs. 3g/4g show a change of vortex orientation ([100] vs [010]) over tens of nm. Neither shows the µm-scale displacement measured by PFM, nor that the PFM contrast boundary is a zig-zag/linear core boundary, yet the text calls the STEM data "consistent with the PFM data". The electron beam, current flow and tip-contact damage could also drive changes, and only a few selected frames are shown. The authors should estimate the field and current in the lamella; discuss beam dose rate and charging and show a no-bias control under identical beam exposure; show more than one event and time-resolved series or videos; state how the 20-day stability (Fig. 4g IV) was measured, including how the lamella was stored and whether exactly the same area was relocated; and argue explicitly how the nanometre-scale STEM changes relate to the micron-scale PFM boundary.

7. The phase-field simulations are under-specified and partly contradict the stability claim. The tip is "modelled by a Lorentz distribution", but its width, applied potential or field magnitude, the channel geometry, the domain-wall mobility L and the electrical boundary conditions are not given; nor is it stated whether the SRO bottom electrode (short-circuit condition) and the actual stack (see concern 10) are modelled, or how "Step 2 … Step 6" map onto the experimental steps. The simulation boxes (e.g. 400×200 uc, ~160 nm, for the trilayer; ~100 nm in Figs. 2k–p and 3h) are far smaller than the ~1 µm displacements claimed, and the simulated "boundary" is a phase shift within a vortex array over ~20 nm. The text (p. 8) says simulated boundaries "maintain their displaced positions" after field removal, while the Fig. 2 caption says "upon field removal, the vortex boundary returns to a configuration similar to the initial state (p)", with panel p labelled "Step 6" and described in the text as continued negative-field motion. The abstract's mechanism—boundary motion "controlled through the switching of the zigzag patterns of the vortex core"—rests on these simulations and one STEM sequence, with no figure quantifying it and no test against the PFM data. Supplementary Fig. 8's "switching between two energy equivalent states" needs the two states identified, their total energies with units, what sets the barrier, and a clearer link between the energy maximum "when the electric field direction switches from negative to positive" and the protocol. The authors should fully specify the model, show field-off relaxed states, provide core-position or tilt maps, energy versus step and a field-threshold scan, simulate at (or argue the transfer to) the PFM length scale, and make a quantitative prediction (threshold bias, its thickness dependence, or displacement per pass) that the experiments test.

8. Sample and region statistics are not reported. Each main protocol appears to be shown once per sample type: one trilayer and one superlattice boundary-motion sequence, one retention region (Fig. 4, "a new region" of the trilayer) and one pulsed-field site (Fig. 5), on one sample of each structure. The paper's selling points—"deterministic control", "reproducibility and reliability" (p. 7) and "reliable memory devices"—require statistics. The authors should report how many samples, regions and tips were used; how often the boundary moved as reported, did not move, or moved irregularly; whether any channel failed; the spread of displacement per step; and whether a second growth of each structure behaves the same, ideally as a table over all attempted experiments including failures.

9. The dependence on writing parameters is missing. All trailing-bias experiments use ±5 V, 1 min per channel and ~60 nm/s. The threshold voltage for boundary motion, and the scaling of displacement per pass with voltage, tip speed, write time and number of passes, would test the claimed "reduced energy barrier" mechanism and separate it from domain writing, which has its own characteristic voltage and time scaling. No velocity or time constant is reported. The authors should provide these dependences, at least in the trilayer.

10. The trilayer layer sequence is described inconsistently. The main text (p. 4: "20 uc STO/20 uc PTO/20 uc STO"), the Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 titles, the phase-field set-up (p. 16, STO/PTO/STO on DSO with no SRO) and the cross-sectional STEM (Fig. 2j) indicate one PTO layer between STO layers, while the Methods (p. 14, "20 uc PbTiO3/20 uc SrTiO3/20 uc PbTiO3") and the Fig. 4 caption give PTO/STO/PTO. Fig. 1g averages loops from 10 "representative" points per category with no selection criterion or statistics, and the Vc maps look noisy at the pixel level. 12. The "vortex rings", "pair of rings" and "hidden topological phases" lack structural evidence. The PFM "rings" (Fig. 5) are closed contours in amplitude, hundreds of nanometres across, which tip-written bubble domains or a1/a2 islands also produce. The STEM "rings" (Fig. 5o–q) are closed loops in the stripe contrast, or white boxes around bright point features, with no polarization mapping, and it is not stated whether they were field-induced or present in the pristine film. Neither is shown to be a vortex ring in the topological sense (a closed vortex-core line, its winding, its 3D toroidal structure). The proposed origin (p. 11: "charge injection ... strain gradients, field gradient, broken symmetry, or boundary constraints") is a list rather than a tested mechanism. The authors should show the polar texture of a ring (atom-resolved displacement maps, 4D-STEM/DPC or 3D polarization mapping) and before/after imaging of the same STEM region, or tone down the wording to "closed-loop textures".
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

13. The "stochastic" behaviour under pulsed fields rests on a single cumulative voltage ramp at one site. Fig. 5 shows one sequence (0–7 V in 0.5 V steps on the same spot), so voltage dependence and cumulative history are confounded, and stochasticity cannot be separated from a deterministic but history-dependent response. Fig. 5m–n draws smooth curves through 5–9 points from 0 to 4 V (displacements up to ±200 nm) without error bars, implying a deterministic trend that contradicts the stochastic interpretation, and omits the data beyond 4 V. The authors should repeat pulses at fixed voltage at several equivalent sites and report the distribution of outcomes, give displacement uncertainties (pixel size, drift correction), include the data to 7 V, and remove the curves or state that they are guides to the eye only.
