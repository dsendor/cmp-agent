A. Must address

1. The Wigner polaron (WP) resonances are not shown to be specific to the Wigner crystal, and the data suggest they persist where the crystal is absent. The central claim is that WP_T and WP_S are attractive polarons (APs) dressed by WC phonons, so they "originate from WCs" and "probe the dynamics of a Wigner crystal" (pp. 4–5, title, abstract). By the paper's own criterion the WC melts at n_c ≈ 7×10^11 cm^-2, where the umklapp disappears. Yet in Fig. 2d the dashed WP traces (WP_S in particular) extend to top gate ≈ 1.1–1.2 V, roughly 8–9×10^11 cm^-2 on the top axis, and in Fig. 1c both WP branches are drawn to about 1.4 V; the paper does not state whether the fading of the WPs around 1.0–1.2 V coincides with n_c. In the optical-melting data (Fig. 4b, p. 6) the WPs are said to "remain robust" after the umklapp has vanished. The authors rationalize this as the WPs probing "local short-range correlations" in a "partially melted regime", but that argument concedes the WPs do not require long-range order, which undercuts the interpretation of the WP–AP splitting as a crystal phonon frequency; and it is invoked only for the optically melted case, not for density-driven melting. Neither the density dependence nor the polarization selectivity (Fig. 3) fixes the origin: any satellite or excited state of T⁻ and S⁻ would inherit the same valley selection rules, and Fermi-sea processes (trion shake-up/recoil satellites leaving an electron–hole excitation, trion–plasmon or exciton–electron scattering continua, bound or excited trion complexes) give a splitting that grows with E_F ∝ n — consistent with the linear fit the authors concede is also possible (Fig. S5). The authors should: (i) extract WP energies, spectral weights (normalized to the parent AP) and linewidths by lineshape fits and plot them against density through and beyond n_c, on the same axis as the umklapp weight, stating where each feature disappears; (ii) measure the temperature dependence of WP and umklapp together at fixed density through the thermal melting temperature (the Methods describe an ATC100 heater, and Figs. S8–S9 are cited only for exciton energies) and show whether the WPs vanish with the umklapp; (iii) state what the theory of Ref. 34 predicts for the AP spectrum in the electron liquid at the same densities, and compare quantitatively with a Fermi-liquid shake-up/recoil satellite, explaining why such a satellite would be absent or different above n_c. If the WPs persist in the liquid, the claims, title and abstract should be reframed as probing short-range correlations rather than WC phonons.

2. The quantitative link between the WP–AP splitting and WC phonon frequencies is weak, the model is not given, and gate screening is omitted. The paper calls the WP–AP splitting "a direct measure of the characteristic phonon frequency" (p. 4) and fits ΔE_WP = a n^{3/4} with a = 4.16 meV/(10^11 cm^-2)^{3/4} (Fig. 2d), claiming "good quantitative agreement" although a is about 1.5 times the minimal-model value, attributed to a "stiffer crystal". Problems: (i) the authors concede the power law "cannot be unambiguously extracted" and a linear fit also works (Fig. S5), because WP energies are unresolved at low density; the dashed lines in Fig. 2d are overlaid on a colour map rather than fitted to extracted peak positions with uncertainties, and the fitted density range and treatment of the weak low-density signal are not stated. (ii) The fit gives ΔE_WP ≈ 13–16 meV at 5–6×10^11 cm^-2 and ~18 meV at n_c, whereas a naive unscreened WC plasma/Debye scale, ħ(e²/4πε0 ε m* a³)^{1/2} with m* = 0.4 m0, ε = 4.5 and lattice constant a ≈ 13 nm, is only a few meV (~5 meV at 7×10^11 cm^-2). (iii) The n^{3/4} law assumes unscreened 1/r interactions, but the graphite gates sit 20 nm and 31 nm away while the WC lattice constant runs from ~13 nm at n_c to >30 nm at 1×10^11 cm^-2, so gate screening modifies both the phonon spectrum and its density scaling, especially at low density. (iv) The theory (Ref. 34, Supplementary Discussion 1) is an arXiv preprint by overlapping authors with no identifier, so the reader cannot check which parameters enter a or whether any are adjusted. (v) It is not explained why phonon dressing produces a resolved peak rather than a broad sideband/continuum, whether one- or multi-phonon processes are involved, which phonon branch and wavevector (zone-boundary, Debye average, weighted spectral function) the peak tracks, which mass (electron or trion) enters, or why the repulsive polaron is "relatively insensitive" to phonon dressing and shows no analogous sideband. The authors should give the full model expression, Hamiltonian, approximations (classical versus quantum phonons, exciton–electron interaction, trion binding, dielectric screening, disorder) and parameter values in the paper; include gate screening for the actual geometry; give independently computed phonon energies at a few densities with the ε and m used; show extracted peak positions with error bars against both n^{3/4} and linear fits with residuals, plus the parameter-free model prediction; and give the arXiv number of Ref. 34. Ideally, devices with different hBN thicknesses would test the screening-dependent density scaling. Until then, "a direct measure of the phonon frequency" and "good quantitative agreement" are overstated.

3. The optical-melting conclusion, stated as established in the abstract ("we show optical melting of the Wigner crystal"), rests on a threshold criterion and on insufficient exclusion of heating, photodoping and probe nonlinearity. (a) The critical density in Fig. 4c is defined where dR_C/dV crosses a fixed value (−0.005 to −0.0065; Methods p. 15), and the error bars reflect only the choice of threshold. This quantity depends on umklapp oscillator strength, linewidth and gate-derivative processing; power-induced broadening (stated for the WPs, Fig. S11), inhomogeneous broadening, saturation or bleaching of the RP, or reduced reflectance contrast would lower the cutoff density without any change in crystalline order. (b) Heating is excluded only via the 1s exciton energy staying "essentially unchanged" (Fig. S10). The WC melting temperature and the corresponding expected 1s shift (~2 meV; Figs. S8–S9) are not given in the main text with the 1s fit resolution; the WSe2 gap is very flat at low T (Varshni), so a sub-resolution shift does not exclude lattice heating to 10–20 K; and the electron temperature, which the authors concede may exceed the lattice temperature, is the most direct alternative cause of melting and is unconstrained. (c) The 650 nm edge of the pump band (1.91 eV) lies at the quoted free-carrier gap (~1.9 eV) and overlaps the 2s/3s states, and the graphite gates and contacts also absorb, so photodoping, photogating and photo-heating are not excluded; the statement that RP/AP energies do not shift does not bound the photodoped density. (d) The same pulsed supercontinuum appears to serve as pump and probe (40 nW standard, 40–8000 nW for melting; "while simultaneously measuring their reflectance spectrum"), so nonlinear probe response cannot be separated from changes in the crystal; this should be stated explicitly. The authors should fit the umklapp spectral weight (normalized to the RP) and linewidth at each power and density and define n* from the integrated weight extrapolated to zero, as for the density-driven case; measure n_c(T) and compare power-induced with thermally induced loss of umklapp at equal weight to bound an effective electron temperature; show that the doping onset voltage, the RP blueshift–density relation and the 2s energy are unchanged with power; and use a separate weak probe, with a pump tuned to the 1s resonance, below the trion energies and above the gap at matched absorbed power. Without these controls the result should be described as power-induced suppression of the umklapp resonance under pulsed excitation, stated as a hypothesis for melting.

B. Should address

4. The WP assignment is not tested against the alternative of AP/trion umklapp. If the WC folds the exciton band for the RP, it should also fold the dispersion of the attractive polarons, producing satellites above T⁻ and S⁻ that blueshift with density, are identical for the two branches and inherit their valley selectivity (Fig. 3) — the very properties used to identify the WPs (pp. 4–5, Fig. 2d). The pictures differ in scaling (umklapp ΔE ∝ n/m*, with m* ≈ m_X + m_e, versus phonon n^{3/4}) and in magnitude: ΔE_WP ≈ 16 meV at 6×10^11 cm^-2 exceeds the RP umklapp splitting at the same density (~12 meV, Fig. 2b), whereas a heavier-mass AP umklapp should lie below it. The authors should overlay on Fig. 2d the parameter-free AP-umklapp prediction using the trion mass from the fitted m_X and m_e, fit both models over the same density window with χ² or residuals, and state explicitly why AP umklapp is excluded.

5. The "all-optical control of WC spins" experiment does not isolate a WC-specific effect and does not quantify the polarization. Under σ+ pumping the co-polarized probe favours T⁻/WP_T and the cross-polarized probe S⁻/WP_S (Fig. 3) — the same valley selection rule T⁻ and S⁻ obey in the electron liquid (Ref. 49 reports optically pumped polarization in doped WSe2 without a WC). This shows each WP shares its parent AP's valley character, not that the crystal plays a role. Also: the degree of electron valley polarization is never extracted (e.g. from the T⁻/S⁻ weight ratio versus the unpumped case, or Kerr/Faraday); the comparison in Fig. 3d is between a 4 μW CW 635 nm (1.95 eV, above the free-carrier gap, three orders above the 40 nW probe) pump and no pump, so heating and photodoping are confounded with polarization; Fig. 3d shows a single density and the maps cover gate ranges below ~1 V; the statement that the umklapp is "largely unaffected" is not quantified; and the abstract phrases "directly probing valley-dependent Wigner polaron scattering well above the magnetic ordering temperature" imply a link to magnetic order that is not tested. The authors should show WP_T/WP_S weights under co- and cross-polarization as a function of density across n_c, quantify the valley polarization, include σ⁻-pump helicity-reversal and linearly polarized equal-power pump controls, and give U_RP amplitude and energy with error bars under co, cross and unpumped conditions. If the effect does not change across n_c, the claim should be reframed as valley pumping of the electron system probed by the WP.

6. The exciton-density estimates behind the exciton-screening mechanism (Fig. 4c, p. 7, Methods p. 15) are underspecified, internally inconsistent and likely wrong in magnitude. The conversion n_X = PαΓ/(Aħω) uses an absorbance of "10% for example"; spot size, the bright/dark lifetimes Γ, the absorbance across the 650–800 nm band and the absorbed spectral fraction are not given, so the axes (up to ~1.5×10^9 cm^-2 bright, ~6×10^11 cm^-2 dark) cannot be reproduced. The text says the dark-exciton density is "approximately 10^11 cm^-2", but the Fig. 4c top axis runs to ~6×10^11 cm^-2, comparable to n_c; such densities should shift or broaden the RP and AP, which is not reported. Conversely, it is hard to see how ~10^9 cm^-2 bright excitons reduce n_c from 6.7 to 0.8×10^11 cm^-2. The peak-power formula is valid only for lifetimes short compared with the 200 ps pulse; dark-exciton lifetimes (Refs. 52–53) are ns and may exceed the 25 ns period, so per-pulse density is bounded by absorbed pulse energy and inter-pulse accumulation matters. A rough estimate (4000 nW at 40 MHz = 0.1 pJ/pulse, ~1 μm² spot, 10% absorbance) gives an absorbed photon density of order 10^12 cm^-2 per pulse, well above the quoted values. The authors should list all parameters with sources, treat pulsed versus steady-state and inter-pulse accumulation explicitly, propagate uncertainties onto the Fig. 4c axes, and then reassess whether the mechanism is plausible.

8. The melting density, r_s ≈ 12 and the "enhanced stability" (stronger than MoSe2) claim rest on inconsistent parameters, lack error analysis and ignore what the umklapp criterion actually detects (pp. 4–5, Methods p. 14). The density uses ε_hBN = 3.5 (or 3.1), but r_s uses ε = 4.5 and m* = 0.4 m0, neither justified; WSe2 conduction-band mass values span ~0.3–0.5 m0, and the density has a ~10% systematic uncertainty. Since r_s ∝ m*/(ε√n), these move r_s by several units. The comparison with clean-limit Monte Carlo r_s ≈ 31–38 assumes unscreened Coulomb interactions in strict 2D at T = 0, whereas gates sit 20–31 nm away (interparticle spacing ~13 nm at n_c) and the measurement is at 5 K. Moreover, n_c is where the umklapp becomes "indiscernible": a correlated liquid with a strong structure-factor peak can give a broadened umklapp-like feature, and a weakening feature can fall below noise before melting. The authors should give r_s_c with an error band, justify ε (in-plane versus out-of-plane hBN) and m*, show umklapp amplitude/linewidth versus n near n_c, discuss which way each bias goes and how gate screening and finite temperature change the expected critical r_s, and ideally give a temperature-dependent n_c or compare devices of different disorder (Fig. S1), before attributing the factor ~3 discrepancy to disorder or Berry curvature.

9. The pump conditions of the spin and melting experiments are inconsistent. Fig. 3 uses a 4 μW CW pump at 635 nm (1.95 eV, above the quoted free-carrier gap), and the umklapp remains clearly visible (Fig. 3b–d); Fig. 4 reports that a pulsed white-light pump of ~4000 nW average power nearly eliminates it. The authors should explain why comparable average powers have such different effects (peak intensity, photon energy, absorption, spot size, duty cycle, or a different device/spot), and characterize pump-induced heating and photodoping in Fig. 3 as well. If melting depends on resonant exciton generation rather than absorbed power, this comparison is itself a useful test of the heating versus exciton-density explanations in concern 3.
