A. Must address

1. The Wigner-polaron (WP) assignment does not exclude umklapp replicas of the attractive polarons (APs). The central new claim (title, abstract, pp. 4–5, Figs. 1c, 2c,d) is that WP_T and WP_S are APs dressed by phonon pairs of the Wigner crystal (WC), with ΔE_WP = 2ω_T ∝ n^(3/4). But a trion-like AP of mass m_T ≈ m_X + m_e ≈ 1.25 m0 moving in a periodic WC has its own folded band, so it should show an umklapp satellite at ΔE = h²n/(√3 m_T). Such a satellite would sit above T− and S−, blueshift with n, and inherit the triplet/singlet valley selectivity of its parent, matching every property of WP_T and WP_S used in the paper, including the helicity data of Fig. 3, which therefore cannot discriminate. The paper's own numbers favour this alternative. The repulsive-polaron (RP) umklapp slope in Fig. 2b is ≈2.0 meV per 10¹¹ cm⁻² for m_X ≈ 0.85 m0, so an AP umklapp should have a slope of ≈2 × 0.85/1.25 ≈ 1.35–1.4 meV per 10¹¹ cm⁻². The linear fit to ΔE_WP in Fig. S4 gives A ≈ 1.50 meV per 10¹¹ cm⁻², and the slope ratio (~0.75) is close to m_X/(m_X + m_e) ≈ 0.7. The ~7 meV intercept of that linear fit is roughly the T–S splitting itself (the WP_S line in Fig. S4 extrapolates to the T− energy), so a feature tied to the other trion or another fine-structure satellite is also a candidate. The argument on p. 4 that "there exists only one repulsive polaron and one umklapp peak because these resonances originate from exciton-electron scattering states" explains why the RP has no separate umklapp per spin channel; it does not explain why bound trions would have no umklapp satellite. Other candidates (hybridization with the RP, inhomogeneous regions, Fano or interference artefacts of the dispersive line shape in a derivative map) are not discussed either. Because the paper concedes (p. 5; SI Section 1, Fig. S4) that the n^(3/4) and linear-plus-offset forms cannot be told apart over the density window shown, the power-law fit does not support the assignment. The authors should: (i) state the expected AP-umklapp energy and its oscillator strength from the theory of Ref. 41, relative to the phonon sideband, and explain quantitatively why the observed lines are not it; (ii) extract WP peak positions with error bars against calibrated density (Fig. 2d is a colour map against V_TG with theory curves overlaid, while Fig. 2b uses n) and plot them with the RP-umklapp data from the same spectra on a common axis; (iii) fit the umklapp form (fixed m_T, zero intercept) and the n^(3/4) form over the same window with AP energies and doping onset treated consistently, and compare goodness of fit; (iv) show whether the WP splitting extrapolates to zero at the onset of doping, with data or at least an upper bound on the feature at the lowest densities, where the two forms differ most; (v) use observables that separate the pictures: the WP/AP oscillator-strength ratio and linewidth ratio against n (Franck–Condon/Huang–Rhys factor versus structure-factor weight), whether ΔE_WP/ΔE_U follows the m_X/m_T scaling of umklapp or the n^(−1/4) scaling of phonons, and the trion-mass dependence (for example T− against S−, or another TMD). Without this, the claim that the satellites "reveal Wigner crystal dynamics" is not established.

2. The WP features do not track the umklapp signal across temperature, optical pumping and density, which undercuts the claim that both report the same WC. If WPs are APs dressed by WC phonons, they should disappear wherever the crystal melts. Instead: under pulsed pumping (p. 6, Figs. 4a,b, S6, S10) the umklapp "nearly vanishes" at ~4000 nW, "suggesting optical melting", while the WPs "remain robust", surviving with broadening and a redshift up to 8000 nW; with temperature (Fig. S7) the WP features and labels are already gone at 10 K, while the umklapp persists to 15 K and n* is still ~6×10¹¹ cm⁻² at 10 K (Fig. S8); along the density axis, the WPs are traced in Fig. 2d only to V_TG ≈ 1.0–1.3 V, WP_S fades near ~1.2 V in Fig. 1c while the umklapp persists to ~1.5 V, and it is not stated whether the WPs disappear at n_c ≈ 7×10¹¹ cm⁻². A feature that survives the loss of long-range order but vanishes under modest heating is hard to reconcile with "a direct measure of the characteristic phonon frequency of the Wigner crystal" (p. 4). Either the umklapp loss is not melting, or the WP is not specific to the crystal and could reflect short-range correlations or local vibrations in a correlated liquid, or something unrelated. The authors should plot WP energy, linewidth and amplitude against temperature (5–15 K in fine steps), against density up to and beyond n_c, and against pump power, on the same axes as the umklapp amplitude; explain within the model of Ref. 41 why a phonon-dressed polaron would survive melting of the lattice that supplies the phonons, yet be more fragile thermally than the umklapp; and temper the title and abstract if the WP cannot be shown to require long-range crystalline order.

B. Should address

3. The evidence that the umklapp feature signals a long-range-ordered WC, rather than a strongly correlated liquid or disorder-pinned glass, is thin, and the melting criterion is arbitrary. The only diagnostic is the RP umklapp, and its disappearance is defined by a fixed threshold on dR_C/dV (−0.005 to −0.0065 in Methods; ±0.005 in the Fig. S8 caption). That amplitude scales with the RP oscillator strength and linewidth, the exciton–electron coupling and the Debye–Waller factor, all of which change strongly with n and pump power (Fig. S11c), not only with the presence of order. The inferred melting at r_s ≈ 12 (p. 4) is about three times below the clean-limit value (~31–38) with κ = 4.5 and m_e = 0.4 m0. The umklapp survives to ~15–25 K (Figs. S7, S8), while a classical estimate with the paper's parameters (Coulomb energy ~18–20 meV, Γ ≈ 130, k_BT_m ≈ 0.3 meV) gives T_m of only a few kelvin at n ≈ 5×10¹¹ cm⁻². Both discrepancies are attributed to disorder without a test (pp. 4–5; SI Section 2). A correlated liquid with a sharply peaked structure factor can also produce an umklapp-like replica whose weight falls off gradually with n and T. The authors should normalize the umklapp amplitude to the fitted RP oscillator strength and width and show the umklapp-to-RP weight ratio as a continuous function of n, T and pump power (does it fall sharply, as at a transition, or gradually, as in a crossover?); show that n_c, n* and T_m are robust to the choice of threshold; show the umklapp linewidth against n and T as a measure of correlation length; compare T_m(n) with classical and quantum melting estimates and discuss quantitatively why the crystal survives to 25 K; state what umklapp intensity a correlated liquid would produce; and scope "first observation of WC in WSe2" and "zero-field Wigner crystals" accordingly. The paper follows the umklapp criterion of Ref. 5, so this does not overturn the WC reading, but it narrows how firmly it can be stated.

4. The "optical melting" claim does not exclude contrast loss, heating or photodoping, and the measurement design limits what it can show (p. 6, Fig. 4, Methods). The pulsed pump is the same filtered supercontinuum used as the reflectance probe, so increasing "pump" power also changes the probe, the measurement is time-integrated over the pulse, and there is no independent pump-off reference; this should be stated clearly. Pump-induced broadening or bleaching of the RP, or saturation at the ~0.5 mW peak power reached at 4 µW average, lowers the umklapp contrast below a fixed threshold without melting (see concern 3). The 650–800 nm window extends to 1.91 eV, at or above the ~1.9 eV free-carrier gap quoted on p. 6, and spans the 2s/3s Rydberg states, so photo-carrier generation and photodoping (for example via hBN or graphite defects) are not excluded; the "no appreciable shift in RP and AP energies" argument is qualitative. The 1s-energy thermometer (Fig. S9) is measured on the 1s exciton, presumably near charge neutrality rather than in the WC regime; the 1s energy barely moves below ~8 K, its ~0.2 meV resolution corresponds to ~5–10 K, and a ~0.2 meV drop is already visible at the lowest powers, comparable to the temperatures at which n* starts to fall (Fig. S8). Electron heating, which the authors acknowledge, is not bounded at all. The authors should overlay RP energy against gate voltage at several powers and bound the implied density shift; map the power dependence of n* onto an equivalent temperature using Fig. S8 and compare it with the 1s bound; use an electronic thermometer in the doped regime (RP or T− energy, or T−/S− or umklapp linewidth calibrated against Fig. S7); compare the umklapp line shape at a given power with a thermal data set matched in RP linewidth; and, ideally, separate pump and probe spectrally or in time (a weak delayed probe and a narrowband, strictly below-gap pump, resonant versus off-resonant) so that a nonequilibrium electron temperature can be distinguished from a change in the ground state. Otherwise "optical melting" should read "pump-induced suppression of the umklapp resonance".

5. The phonon-model comparison is only semi-quantitative. The SI theory (Section 1) is a single-oscillator (Einstein) model: the trion sits in a harmonic well with the electron's frequency, phonons are created in pairs, and ΔE_WP = 2ω_T is compared with the data. The WC, however, has a gapless phonon continuum, and a sideband coupled to it would generally be broad and asymmetric rather than sharp. Near r_s ≈ 12, close to melting, zero-point motion is large and anharmonicity should be strong (ħω_e ≈ 9 meV against a ≈20 meV Coulomb energy per electron at 5×10¹¹ cm⁻²). The fitted prefactor a is 1.5 times the model value, yet this is called "good quantitative agreement" (p. 5); the crystal appears stiffer than predicted near melting, where it should soften, and the discrepancy is attributed to disorder and stiffness without a calculation. The fit has one free parameter but also fixes the AP reference energies and the doping onset: the fitted curves in Fig. 2d start near V_t ≈ −0.3 V, while the onset in Figs. 1b and S11 appears near 0 V. The inputs (m_e, m_T, κ, ζ = 11.034 in Eq. S1, the predicted a) are not all given and each is uncertain at the tens-of-percent level, including gate screening (concern 7). The authors should list all parameters and the error on a; show residuals and fit statistics for the n^(3/4) and linear fits; fit a separately for WP_T and WP_S to test the claimed equal splittings; clarify the density origin; discuss the harmonic approximation (for example the Lindemann ratio); state which WP properties (sharpness, weight, equal T/S splitting) follow from the full model of Ref. 41 rather than the Einstein approximation, and give its predicted WP/AP weight ratio and linewidth against the data; show how a changes over plausible ranges of m_e, κ and gate distance; and explain why the 7 meV intercept of the linear fit is unphysical rather than the offset any AP-derived satellite would have. Ref. 41, the unpublished co-author theory on which the interpretation rests, lacks an arXiv identifier; its key results should be summarized in the SI so that the predicted a can be reproduced.

6. The number of devices and spots is not reported. The Methods describe "a device", and every figure appears to come from one sample at one spot. The WP features are weak (a few percent of the T− dip in Fig. 3d), and disorder is invoked to explain the anomalous r_s and T_m (p. 5), so device-to-device variation of n_c is directly relevant. The authors should state how many devices and locations show the umklapp and WP features, show the key maps (Figs. 1c, 2d) for at least one more device or spot, and quote the spread in n_c, T_m, the lattice constant and the WP prefactor a. Spatial mapping of the umklapp and WP amplitudes would also address whether the satellites come from regions with different local doping or dielectric environment. A second device with a different gate distance or disorder level would test the gate-screening and disorder explanations.

7. The density calibration, gate geometry and derived quantities are under-specified (Methods, p. 4). The hBN thicknesses, gate-to-sample distances and onset voltage are not given, although they enter n. The Methods state a "single-gate scan" while Fig. 1a shows two gates and the density formula includes both; state which gate was swept in each figure and how the other was held. The density uses ε_hBN = 3.5 while r_s and the phonon model use κ = 4.5, and m_e = 0.4 m0 is not justified; these choices directly set r_s ≈ 12. The ±10% uncertainty comes only from ε_hBN (3.5 versus 3.1) and omits hBN thickness, onset voltage, quantum capacitance and trapped or localized carriers near onset. Gate screening matters if either gate lies within a few tens of nm of the sample (the WC lattice constant at n_c is ~13 nm): it would soften long-wavelength phonons and change both the melting density and the WP stiffness, yet Eq. S1 uses an unscreened Coulomb constant. Using the fitted m_X as a consistency check is partly circular with the calibration, and the RP is a dressed exciton, so the umklapp splitting probes the RP mass rather than the bare exciton mass. The authors should give the thicknesses, show the Fig. S11 onset determination quantitatively, include gate screening in the phonon estimate, propagate the full uncertainty into r_s, n_c, m_X and a, discuss the RP-versus-exciton mass, and ideally calibrate density independently with magneto-optics (Landau-level or Shubnikov–de Haas features, as in Refs. 59, 62).

8. The spin-control experiment (Fig. 3, p. 5) does not isolate anything specific to the WC, and the polarization is not quantified. Valley pumping of resident electrons in WSe2 by circularly polarized light is established (Refs. 53, 54), and the T−/S− helicity dependence reproduces it; that WP_T and WP_S follow their parent APs shows they are AP-derived, but any AP-derived satellite, including an AP umklapp (concern 1), would do the same. The 635 nm (≈1.95 eV) CW pump at 4 µW lies above the ~1.9 eV free-carrier gap quoted on p. 6, so photodoping or heating could shift density between the "pumped" and "no pumping" traces in Fig. 3d. The authors should extract the electron valley polarization from the T−/S− weight ratio, calibrated against a magnetic-field measurement where it saturates, with pump-power and gate dependence; show that the RP energy (and hence n) is matched in the compared traces; quantify, with uncertainty and against the model, the statement that the umklapp is "largely unaffected" (Fig. S5, at only one density); compare with the same measurement above n_c or above the melting temperature, where the electrons are a liquid; show that the effect reverses with pump helicity and is absent under linear pumping; specify the probe polarization and the "no pumping" detection configuration; and rephrase "all-optical control of spins in the Wigner crystal" and "directly probes valley-dependent WP scattering well above the magnetic ordering temperature" as control of the electron valley polarization. Field dependence of WP_T against WP_S would also test their spin character.

9. The injected exciton densities in Fig. 4c are not reproducible and look internally inconsistent (p. 6, Methods). The formula n_X = PαΓ/(Aħω), applied to peak power P = P_avg/(f_R t_p) for a 200 ps pulse, assumes a lifetime short compared with the pulse. That holds for bright excitons but not for dark excitons with lifetimes of hundreds of ps to ns, for which the per-pulse density is bounded by the pulse energy times α/(Aħω), plus inter-pulse accumulation over the 25 ns period; as written the dark density is misestimated. The absorbance ("10%, for example"), spot size, lifetimes and the spectral overlap of the 650–800 nm pump with the absorption are not given, nor is the power of each Fig. 4c point. The text gives a dark-exciton density of "approximately 10¹¹ cm⁻²" at threshold, "still lower than n_c", while the top axis of Fig. 4c reaches ~6×10¹¹ cm⁻², comparable to n_c, and at the highest power n_c itself has dropped to ~1×10¹¹ cm⁻². Because the conclusion that "bright excitons alone are unlikely to melt the WC" rests on these numbers, the authors should correct the formula, state every input with uncertainty, add a pump-power axis and error bars to Fig. 4c, and reconcile the numbers.
