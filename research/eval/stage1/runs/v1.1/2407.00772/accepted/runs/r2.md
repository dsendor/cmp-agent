Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper uses few-femtosecond broadband XUV transient absorption (UBXAS) at the Ti M2,3 and Se M4,5 edges of 1T-TiSe2 to follow how fast photoexcitation creates Se 4p holes, as a function of fluence and temperature. It reports two things: a 1/√F scaling of the initial response time both below and well above Tc ≈ 200 K, which it reads as short-range excitonic correlations persisting in the normal state; and a low-fluence response time with a minimum near Tc, which it reads as an enhanced (diverging) excitonic susceptibility. If these readings hold, the paper gives a direct, element-specific observable for excitonic fluctuations above Tc. That would bear on the long-running debate over whether the CDW in TiSe2 is excitonic or lattice-driven, and it could be applied to other candidates such as Ta2NiSe5 and Ta2Pd3Te5. The method is new and impressive. The physical conclusions, however, rest mainly on interpreting a single fitted timescale.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The technique is new for this problem. Sub-100-fs timescales are resolved with element and orbital selectivity, and the probe does not need long-range order, unlike ARPES band folding.
- The choice of Se M5 peak ③ is well motivated. Its lack of A1g phonon modulation (Fig. 2e) makes it a cleaner electronic reporter than the other features.
- The temperature-dependent transient amplitudes at both edges (Fig. 3d,e) show onsets near Tc, which helps establish sensitivity to the phase transition.
- Both fluence and temperature are mapped systematically, and the low- and high-fluence data are cross-checked between Fig. 4d and Fig. 4e (23 K and 300 K values agree between the panels).
- The Discussion addresses the main competing explanation, CDW phonon hardening.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The 1/√F scaling is not shown to be specific to excitonic correlations, yet it is the main evidence for "persistent excitonic fluctuations in the normal state" (p. 1792, Fig. 4b,d,g). The argument runs: τ_int ∝ 1/ω_pl ∝ 1/√n_tot, and because this scaling appears at 300 K as at 23 K, excitonic correlations must be present above Tc. But a build-up time set by the inverse plasma frequency is a generic property of screening in any photoexcited electron–hole plasma. It describes how fast the Coulomb interaction is screened, and it does not by itself require bound or correlated electron–hole pairs. The constant offset τ0 is then assigned, without independent support, to "the time needed to create mobile Se 4p holes in the absence of excitonic interactions." As it stands, the same fluence dependence would be expected from any carrier-density-dependent screening, from band renormalization, or from thermalization of carriers into the Se 4p states, whether or not excitonic correlations exist. Three further points add to this:
   (a) In the normal state the semimetal has a sizeable equilibrium carrier density. If n_tot = n_eq + n_photo, the 1/√F form holds only when n_photo ≫ n_eq. The authors should estimate the photoexcited density at 0.2 mJ cm⁻² (absorbed fluence, penetration depth) and compare it with n_eq at 300 K.
   (b) The fluence range is about a factor of 15 (0.2 to 3 mJ cm⁻²). The 1/√F shape is set mostly by the one or two lowest-fluence points (Fig. 4d), and with τ0 free a 1/F or logarithmic form might fit equally well. The authors should give the fit parameters at each temperature, compare the alternative functional forms, and state whether 23 K and 300 K share one prefactor.
   (c) No null case is shown, i.e. a system or regime where excitonic correlations should be absent and the scaling should therefore fail.
   The authors should give a quantitative argument or a control that tells the excitonic reading apart from generic plasma screening. Options include: a reference compound measured under matched conditions with the same probe (for example 1T-TiS2, a doped or Cu-intercalated TiSe2 with suppressed CDW, or a much higher temperature); a demonstration that the prefactor (not only the exponent) carries information specific to excitons; or a calculation showing that single-particle screening would give a different fluence dependence. Without this, the normal-state claim should be stated as "consistent with" rather than as evidence for excitonic correlations.

B. Should address

2. The phenomenological model (Eq. 1) contradicts the high-fluence data in Fig. 4e. Eq. 1 sets n_tot ∝ F[χ0 + χex(T)], with τ_int − τ0 ∝ 1/√n_tot. This makes the temperature dependence enter as a factor that multiplies F, so at every temperature the ratio (τ_low − τ0)/(τ_high − τ0) should be the same, √10 ≈ 3.2. At 23 K the data agree: (190 − 25)/(75 − 25) ≈ 3.3. At 190 K, however, the low-fluence value (~70 fs) equals the high-fluence value (~75 fs), so the ratio is about 1. The model would predict a high-fluence value near 40 fs at Tc. The red line in Fig. 4e is a plain average and is not tied to Eq. 1. The behaviour therefore needs a mechanism the model lacks, such as saturation of the excitonic contribution (only a finite number of excitons can be broken) or a lower bound on τ_int near 70 fs. Either changes how the low-fluence dip should be read: a response that has saturated or hit a floor near Tc is not the same as a diverging susceptibility. The authors should extend the model so it describes both fluence branches together (for example with a saturable χex term), fit them jointly, and report the fitted Tc, α and χ0 with uncertainties in the main text.

3. The minimum at Tc is defined by a single point, and the effective sample temperature near Tc is not documented (Fig. 4e, p. 1792–1793). The approach to Tc from both sides is spread over several points (150 K, 250 K), but the sharp minimum rests on the one 190 K point, and there are no measurements between 150 and 250 K apart from 190 K. Because the claim is a divergence ∝ |T − Tc|^−α, the authors should (i) add low-fluence points at about 5–10 K spacing between 170 and 230 K, as Fig. 3 already does with a continuous temperature ramp; (ii) state the repetition rate and estimate the steady-state heating at both fluences, so the real sample temperature relative to Tc is known; and (iii) explain why the minimum sits at 190 K while Tc ≈ 200 K is quoted, and whether this offset is consistent with heating. A related issue is the transient one: near Tc the order-melting threshold fluence falls, so 0.2 mJ cm⁻² at 190 K may already be above threshold. That would naturally make the response fluence-independent there (Fig. 4c,d). The authors should show the fluence dependence of the transient amplitude, not only of τ_int, at 190 K and at 23 K to test this.

4. It is assumed, but not shown, that the transmission decrease in window (iii) measures only the Se 4p hole population (p. 1791–1792). The selected spectra in Fig. 3c show a derivative-like shape (positive and negative lobes around 52.5–54 eV) at both 25 K and 310 K. That shape is the signature of an edge shift or broadening (core-hole screening, band renormalization), not of pure state filling. If part of the signal in window (iii) comes from a shift, τ_int may partly measure how fast screening builds up rather than how fast holes are created, which feeds directly into concern 1. The main text points to Supplementary Note 7 for robustness to the window choice. The authors should show in the main text how filling and shift/broadening contributions are separated: fit the transient spectra at several delays with a model of state filling plus edge shift, and show τ_int for each component.

5. Reproducibility across samples and the source of the error bars are not reported (Fig. 4d,e). The main text does not say how many samples or thin flakes were measured, whether the fluence and temperature series come from the same sample, or whether the key results (the 1/√F scaling at 300 K and the dip near Tc) were seen in more than one sample or after thermal cycling. The error bars are 1 s.d. fitting uncertainties only, which leave out scan-to-scan and sample-to-sample variation. Given that the central claims depend on differences of roughly 50–100 fs between single fitted points, the authors should report the number of samples and the repeatability of τ_int at a fixed condition.

6. Sample thickness and the excitation-depth profile are not given. XUV transmission requires thin samples, but the main text does not give the sample thickness, substrate, or the pump penetration depth at 750 nm compared with the thickness. If the pump is absorbed non-uniformly through the probed volume, the probe averages over a range of carrier densities. That changes how τ_int relates to F and affects concerns 1 and 2. Substrate strain or thin-film effects could also shift Tc, so the Tc determined in this sample (Fig. 3) should be stated explicitly and compared with bulk.

7. The argument that excludes phonon contributions is limited (Discussion, p. 1793–1794). The 20% estimate uses the photoinduced renormalization of the CDW amplitude mode below Tc from ref. 28. Above Tc, and near Tc where the soft L-point phonon is strongly damped and its frequency depends strongly on temperature, that estimate does not obviously apply. Yet those are exactly the regimes where the excitonic claims are made. The authors should explain why soft-mode dynamics near and above Tc cannot produce the temperature-dependent τ_int, for example by comparing with the soft-phonon frequencies and damping measured by X-ray or electron diffuse scattering (refs. 28, 40, 44).

8. The "order-parameter-like onset" in Fig. 3d,e is stated more strongly than the data show. In Fig. 3d the Ti window (ii) signal changes roughly linearly from about 50 K to 200 K and does not show a sharp onset. In Fig. 3e the Se window (iii) signal also changes gradually below Tc. The authors should fit these curves (for example with a power law in Tc − T) or describe them less strongly. They should also say whether normalizing by the static spectrum at each temperature affects the trend.

C. Minor

9. Fig. 4e: the high-fluence point at 350 K was taken at 1.1 mJ cm⁻² rather than 2.0 mJ cm⁻². Following the 1/√F fit in Fig. 4d, that alone would raise τ_int by about 20 fs. The point should be marked or corrected before it is included in the high-fluence average.

10. The main text gives no values for the fitted Tc, α, χ0 and χex prefactor (Fig. 4e), nor for the 1/√F prefactors (Fig. 4d). Please give them, together with the number of free parameters compared with the number of data points.

11. Fluences are quoted as incident values. Please also give absorbed fluence and the estimated photoexcited carrier density per formula unit, so the results can be compared with the time-resolved ARPES literature (refs. 20, 23).

12. The interpretation of the low-fluence sign reversal of the Ti many-body continuum (Fig. 2b,d) as an "additional dynamical process" linked to excitonic order is suggestive but left qualitative. Its temperature dependence across Tc at low fluence (Fig. 3b,d) could be linked more explicitly to the Se-edge results.

13. Fig. 4d: the 190 K low-fluence points (~0.15–0.25 mJ cm⁻²) appear to sit at roughly 70–90 fs, while Fig. 4e gives ~70 fs at 0.2 mJ cm⁻². Please confirm that these are the same data and explain any difference.

14. Fig. 4f,g are schematics. The caption should say clearly that the short-range excitonic correlation length above Tc is not measured. If it can be estimated, for example from the magnitude of χex, please give that estimate.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Add a null-control measurement with the same UBXAS probe and fluence series on a material or doping level without excitonic correlations. This would make the 1/√F scaling a discriminating signature rather than a consistent one.
- Fit both fluence branches in Fig. 4e jointly with a saturable or bounded extension of Eq. 1, and add dense temperature sampling around Tc.
- Decompose the Se M5 transient spectra into state-filling and edge-shift components, and show τ_int for each.
- Report the sample count, the repeatability of τ_int, the steady-state heating estimate and the excitation-depth profile in the main text or Methods.

5. Verdict

Verdict: major revision
