B. Should address

1. A purely thermal explanation of the nanosecond recovery is not quantitatively excluded (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Note 3, Methods, Discussion). The sample is held at 190 K, only ~23 K below T_N1 = 213 K, where M(T) is steep and thermal critical diffuse scattering around (1/2 0 −1/2) is strong. The main data use 3 mJ cm⁻² at 400 nm with ~450 nm penetration. A rough estimate (absorbed ~67 J cm⁻³, C ≈ 3.4 J cm⁻³ K⁻¹) gives a lattice temperature rise of order 15–20 K, which would bring the probed volume close to T_N1 after equilibration. Part of the RDS upturn and RXD suppression at ps–ns times could therefore be an equilibrium spin response at a raised lattice temperature, with recovery set by heat transport out of the excited layer (pump penetration ~450 nm, probe depth ~18 nm) rather than by a magnon–phonon bottleneck. The nonlinear decrease of the RDS signal at high fluence "at temperatures closer to the AFM transition" (p. 4) points the same way, RXD has not fully recovered at 12 ns (residual ~−0.1 in Fig. 4d), and the −59 µeV coupling is "estimated from the magnon relaxation timescale", so it assumes the 7 ns constant is intrinsic. The argument that RXD recovers in ~7 ns while the lattice stays hot for µs (Fig. S13) needs to be made quantitatively. The authors should (i) state the estimated magnon and lattice temperatures after full thermalization in the main text, (ii) compare the RXD reduction and RDS intensity at the longest delays (10–12 ns and later) with static RXD/RDS or equilibrium M(T)² at that temperature, (iii) give the asymptotic value of the 7 ns fit, (iv) estimate the heat-diffusion time out of the probed volume, and ideally (v) show how the ~7 ns constant depends on base temperature or fluence, since a bottleneck and thermal transport predict different trends. If the long-time plateau matches the static values, the bottleneck interpretation is much stronger; if not, the 7 ns component needs re-examination.

3. The separation of the RDS upturn from the RXD tail is model-dependent and least reliable at the small |q| that carry the key claims (Fig. 3a–f, i, Supplementary Note 2). All RDS amplitudes and time constants come from a two-exponential decomposition. The momentum resolution is ~5 × 10⁻³ r.l.u. while the smallest offsets are 0.010 and 0.015 r.l.u. (at 0.010 the resolution is half the offset); the region nearer q_RXD is excluded because of saturation; there the RXD-tail reduction is 10–20 times larger than the upturn (Fig. 3a); and the Fig. 3 caption attributes a slight ±q asymmetry to an error in the definition of q = 0, which shifts all |q| values. Yet the near-Γ points (τ_RDS ~3–4 ps) set the low-q end of the 1/τ_RDS ∝ |q| law and support the claimed accumulation at Γ. The authors should show fit residuals for representative q; report the correlation between the fitted RXD-tail and RDS parameters for |q| ≤ 0.02; state whether τ_RXD was shared or free across q, and show τ_RDS(q) with τ_RXD fixed at its q-independent value (Fig. 3d) and left free; show the fit after re-centring the origin; and propagate the resolution function and origin uncertainty into τ_RDS and A_RDS, testing whether a resolution-convolved fit changes the slope or intercept in Fig. 3i.

4. The tr-NRDS evidence for magnon-to-phonon energy transfer is taken under conditions that do not match the tr-RDS data, and the timescales called "similar" differ by a factor of ~3 (Fig. 4d, p. 6, Methods). The NRDS data come from ESRF ID09 with ~100 ps X-ray pulses, a picosecond 800→400 nm pump at 1 kHz (against 35 fs at 100 Hz at SwissFEL), a pump incidence of ~88° (which, if measured from the surface normal, greatly lowers the absorbed fluence per unit area relative to the nominal 3 mJ cm⁻²), a different cryostat, and an X-ray probe depth of ~1.5 µm, far beyond the ~500 nm pump penetration, so weakly excited bulk dominates and cumulative heating at 1 kHz may be larger; the tr-RDS probe depth is ~18 nm. The text says the ~2 ns NRDS enhancement occurs on "a similar timescale to the RDS signals", but the RDS/RXD recovery is ~7 ns, and the AFM NRDS signal peaks at ~1–2 ns and then decays. The PM control is necessarily at a different base temperature (>230 K) that is not stated, with a different equilibrium phonon population and specific heat. The authors should give the temperatures of the AFM and PM traces (also in the Fig. 4d caption), state whether the quoted fluence is in the beam plane or on the sample surface and compare the absorbed energy density in the probed volume for both experiments, reconcile the 2 ns and 7 ns timescales (for example with a depth-weighted model or a common fit), and show that the AFM/PM contrast is not due to different deposited energy, heat capacity or thermal diffuse background rather than the presence of order. A tr-NRDS trace in the AFM phase at a second temperature, or below the demagnetization threshold of Fig. 2f, would strengthen the link.

5. "Non-thermal magnons throughout reciprocal space within femtoseconds" (abstract, Fig. 3j) comes from the model, while the data show a magnetic diffuse increase developing over picoseconds. A simpler picture also predicts a q-dependent rise and increased diffuse magnetic intensity: fast electron-to-spin energy transfer raising a thermal magnon population at 190 K, close to T_N1. The authors should show how the data at the earliest delays (<0.5 ps) favour a broad non-thermal distribution over a hot Bose–Einstein one, and whether the measured τ_RDS(q) differs from that of a heated thermal population relaxing through the same collision integral. A model run with an elevated-temperature thermal initial state, compared with the data in Fig. 3a and 3i, would settle this.

6. "Quasi-thermalization … fulfilling Bose–Einstein statistics" goes beyond what is measured (abstract, p. 4–5). The experiment covers only |q| ≲ 0.08 r.l.u. along b* around one magnetic reflection; zone-boundary populations and the Bose–Einstein form of the late-time distribution come from the model. If magnon number is approximately conserved by leading-order magnon–magnon scattering, the quasi-equilibrium should have a finite chemical potential; the accumulation near Γ (f up to ~13, Fig. 3h, n) suggests this, but no effective temperature or chemical potential is given or tested. The authors should report the effective T and µ of the simulated late-time distribution, test whether a Bose–Einstein form with these parameters, convolved with A_µ(q) and the momentum resolution, fits the measured RDS q-profile at 3–4 ps, and word the abstract to separate what is measured from what is modelled.

8. The magnon–phonon channel assignment and the "unexpectedly small" coupling depend on a fitted parameter (p. 6–7, Fig. 4a–f, Supplementary Note 10). The effective coupling of −59 µeV is "estimated from the magnon relaxation timescale", so the calculation fixes the absolute rate to the data and only ranks the three channels. The conclusion that anomalous scattering dominates, and the comparison with the −6.2 meV spin–phonon coupling of Ref. 46, depend on how each vertex is derived from the Heisenberg Hamiltonian, on the phonon modes included (taken from Ref. 70), and on whether the two numbers are the same physical quantity. The authors should give the estimation procedure and its uncertainty in the Methods; give the calculated rates for all three processes with the same microscopic vertices and no fitted rescaling, showing what is parameter-free; show the sensitivity to the phonon branches included; explain why channel (i) is negligible beyond the qualitative phase-space argument; and state whether the −59 µeV and −6.2 meV couplings are directly comparable.

9. All resonant data are from one crystal at one base temperature (190 K), and the momentum-resolved data at one fluence (3 mJ cm⁻²). Magnon–magnon scattering rates should depend strongly on the thermal magnon background, which is large at 190 K (k_BT ≈ 16 meV, comparable to the magnon energies probed, Fig. 3j–n), and the spin gap and phonon occupations also change with T; the model's quasi-thermalization time and ns bottleneck should therefore shift in a calculable way. Eq. (2) uses f̄ = f + 1, but it is not stated whether the simulated initial state includes the thermal population at 190 K. The authors should state this; add τ_RDS(q) and ns recovery data at a second base temperature in the commensurate AFM phase (e.g. well below 190 K) or a fluence series if available, which would directly test the magnon–magnon and bottleneck assignments; and state how many samples, spots or beamtimes the Fig. 2–3 results represent.

10. The generality claims exceed the evidence (abstract, Discussion). The statements that the framework "transcends phenomenological models and generalizes across antiferromagnetic insulators, establishing design principles", and that "materials with strongly overlapping dispersions of magnons and phonons will exhibit faster recovery", are not tested: only CuO is measured, and its channel selection depends on its particular spin gap and phonon spectrum. The authors should present these as hypotheses, state which parts of the hierarchy (zone-wide magnon population, Bose–Einstein form, channel dominance) come from the model rather than the data, state which features of CuO the conclusions depend on, and give at least one concrete, testable prediction for another material, or support them with a tunable test such as temperature dependence.

11. The explanation of the missing self-absorption dip in the RDS energy dependence conflicts with the stated depths (Fig. 2e, p. 3). RXD shows a dip at resonance due to self-absorption, while RDS peaks at 931.9 eV without one; the text says this is "likely" because a larger magnon density is created near the surface. But the pump penetration (~450 nm) is ~25 times the X-ray probe depth (~18 nm), so excitation is nearly uniform over the probed depth. Since the energy dependence is a main argument for the resonant magnetic origin of the upturn, its lineshape should be understood. 12. Possible magneto-vibrational contributions to the resonant diffuse signal are not considered. Photoexcited phonons, seen directly in tr-NRDS (Fig. 4d), can produce diffuse intensity around a magnetic Bragg peak through magneto-vibrational scattering. Such a signal would be resonant and follow the magnetic π/σ selection, so neither Fig. 2e nor Fig. 2g excludes it. The authors should estimate its size relative to the measured upturn, for example from the measured NRDS amplitude scaled by the ratio of magnetic to charge structure factors.
