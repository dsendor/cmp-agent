A. Must address

1. **The RDS upturn has not been separated from a pump-induced change in the magnetic Bragg-peak line shape.** The headline novelty is that tr-RDS measures magnon diffuse scattering, but every control offered (Cu L3 resonance in Fig. 2E, π/σ ratio like the magnetic Bragg peak in Fig. 2G vs. Fig. S5B, fluence threshold coinciding with demagnetization in Fig. 2F, symmetry about q_RXD in Fig. 3F, b*/[h 0 h] anisotropy in Fig. S9) would equally be satisfied by a change in the shape of the (½ 0 −½) peak itself, since any such change inherits the same resonance and polarization dependence. Candidate causes include inhomogeneous strain, lattice-parameter or mosaic changes, a change in absorption length (which sets the peak width for an 18 nm probe depth), a depth profile of the order parameter, or a reduced correlation length. Picosecond strain is a specific worry: the pump deposits energy within ~450 nm while the resonant probe samples ~18 nm (Methods), so a strain front crosses the probed layer in a few ps at typical CuO sound velocities, the same time scale as the RDS rise; strain broadens the peak mainly along the surface normal, close to the specular (½ 0 −½) direction, which is not scanned and which the APD acceptance may integrate over. The only direct argument against a profile change, that "the RXD peak width remains nearly constant" (p. 4, Fig. 2C), is weak: the peak top is saturated (Fig. 2C caption), the region within ~±0.0125 r.l.u. is not measured (hatched in Fig. 3), and the profile is shown at a single delay (3 ps) and only along b*, so neither width nor integrated intensity can be determined. In addition, the linear-in-q rate in Fig. 3I has a slope at q ≈ 0.075 r.l.u. along b* (≈0.14 Å⁻¹, 1/τ ≈ 5 ps⁻¹) corresponding to ~4 km/s if 1/τ is an angular frequency, in the range of acoustic sound velocities, or ~24 km/s with a factor 2π, closer to the magnon velocity of the model dispersion (Fig. S13C); the authors should state which mechanism the slope is consistent with. The APD response near the peak is also relevant: if the detectors are nonlinear at those count rates, I_ON − I_OFF is distorted exactly where the largest RDS amplitudes are reported. The authors should: (a) use the 0.5M Jungfrau detector already used for high-fluence RXD (Methods; Fig. S7), or an attenuated APD scan, to show the full unsaturated profile around (½ 0 −½) along b*, [h 0 h] and the specular direction at several delays from 0 to 10 ps at 3 mJ/cm², reporting fitted position, width and integrated intensity against delay and testing whether the tail gain is balanced by a change of the core (integrated-intensity conservation); (b) state the momentum acceptance of each APD in r.l.u. along all three directions and show that points at |q| = 0.01–0.02 r.l.u. are not dominated by the resolution tail of the saturated peak; (c) confirm APD linearity at the relevant count rates; (d) measure the same q-resolved traces around a structural (charge) Bragg reflection to test for strain-induced tails; (e) provide a null measurement at the same offset where the commensurate magnetic peak is absent (PM phase above T_N2, or the ICM phase at the commensurate position); and (f) estimate the strain-induced profile change expected from the known sound velocities and the pump and probe depths and show that it is small compared with the RDS amplitudes in Fig. 3F.

2. **The upturn is not separated from a thermal (critical) increase in diffuse scattering, so "non-thermal" is not demonstrated.** All RDS data are taken at 190 K and 3 mJ/cm². The authors estimate the heating as "below 20 K" (Supplementary Text, "Transient incommensurate state"), and T_N1 is 213 K (Methods) or 214.5 K (Fig. S3), so the probed 18 nm surface layer, where excitation is strongest, may end up within a few kelvin of T_N1. There, equilibrium critical diffuse scattering around (½ 0 −½) grows steeply and would be resonant, π-enhanced and symmetric about q_RXD, the same signatures used in Figs. 2E–G and 3F. The fall of the upturn above 3 mJ/cm² (Fig. 2F) is itself attributed to the approach to T_N, so heating is already part of the authors' reading. The non-thermal character of the initial distribution is the paper's central claim, yet no equilibrium reference exists against which to judge it. The authors should measure the static diffuse intensity at the same q points, energy and polarization as a function of temperature from ~100 K to above T_N1, along both b* and [h 0 h], and compare it quantitatively with the pumped q-profiles at early and late delays (up to 10–100 ps, Fig. S8): a late-delay profile matching an equilibrium profile at some elevated temperature would show quasi-thermalization, and a mismatch at early delays would show the distribution is non-thermal. The same series calibrates RDS intensity against equilibrium magnon occupation (see concern 7). The heating estimate, with the reflectivity and heat capacity used, should be given explicitly in the main text, since it bears on Fig. 2F and on the incommensurate-phase argument.

3. **The nanosecond recovery is assigned to anomalous magnon–phonon scattering into the lowest optical phonons without excluding heat transport, and the supporting evidence is indirect.** RXD and RDS recover with τ ≈ 7 ns (Fig. 4D), which the paper attributes to an intrinsic magnon–phonon bottleneck limited by dispersion mismatch. A simpler alternative is untested: after picosecond quasi-equilibration, the order in the 18 nm probed layer may follow the local lattice temperature, which relaxes as heat diffuses out of a ~450 nm excited layer that is strongly graded within the probe depth. The experimental link is weak on several counts. (a) The tr-NRDS was taken at ESRF with ~100 ps pulses at 1 kHz, 8.7 keV in grazing incidence, a picosecond (800 nm-derived) pump and a ~1.5 µm probe depth against a ~500 nm pump depth, while tr-RDS probes ~18 nm at 100 Hz; the two average over very different and partly unexcited volumes, and excitation densities and fluence calibrations are not matched. (b) The "AFM vs PM" control compares 190 K with 300 K (Fig. 4D, Fig. S11), which also changes the phonon population, Debye–Waller factor, heat capacity, thermal expansion, thermal conductivity, absorbed energy and Bragg intensity, not only the magnetic order. (c) The kinetics do not obviously agree: the NRDS enhancement peaks at ~1–2 ns and has largely decayed by 10 ns, whereas phonons fed by a 7 ns magnon decay and removed on microseconds (Fig. S11) should keep rising over ~7 ns; some intermediate process (e.g. phonon redistribution out of the ROI) is needed but not discussed. The "broad enhancement around 2 ns" also rests on a handful of delays (≈1, 3, 5, 7, 10 ns in Fig. S10C), and the fit function for the blue curve in Fig. 4D is not given. (d) In the model, all mode-specific couplings are replaced by a single c^x = 1 eV/Å for both normal and anomalous terms, all masses by the Cu mass, and only the initial rate at t = 0 is computed (Supplementary Text after Eq. S25, pp. S17–S18). Absolute rates scale as c², so any agreement with ~7 ns follows from that choice, and the dominance of the lowest optical phonons (Fig. 4E–F, Fig. S17) reflects phase space only; real polarization-vector and exchange-gradient factors could reorder the channels. (e) The ~59 µeV "effective magnon-phonon coupling" (p. 9), "estimated from the magnon relaxation timescale", has no stated definition or derivation, and its comparison with the 6.2 meV Raman spin-phonon coupling (ref. 44) compares different quantities. The authors should: estimate the lattice temperature in the probed layer against delay from absorbed fluence, heat capacity and thermal diffusivity, and compare the expected quasi-equilibrium RXD suppression with Fig. 4D; build a rate-equation model with magnon, phonon-in-ROI and bath reservoirs that fits RXD, RDS and NRDS rise and decay together with measured excitation densities and a magnon decay rate ≈ 1/7 ns; specify the NRDS fit model; add tr-NRDS delays between 1 and 5 ns; add a PM reference just above T_N2 (~235–250 K), or an AFM measurement below the demagnetization threshold, under conditions matched to the RXD; derive or bound c^x independently (e.g. from ref. 44) and show how the predicted recovery time and the optical-versus-acoustic and anomalous-versus-normal ranking change when c^x varies by mode or c^anomalous/c^normal varies; and give the formula behind the 59 µeV value and its relation to c^x. Otherwise the "recovery bottleneck from dispersion mismatch" should be presented as a hypothesis consistent with the data.

5. **The model's excitation strength does not match the measured demagnetization.** B₀Δn(t) was chosen to "reproduce the experimentally observed reduction of the AFM order by approximately 50%" (p. S13, below Eq. S13). At the 3 mJ/cm² used for Figs. 3 and 4, Fig. 2D shows a ~28–30% drop in RXD intensity; since intensity scales roughly as the square of the sublattice moment, that is a ~15% moment reduction. The model may therefore inject several times too many magnons, which matters because magnon–magnon scattering rates depend strongly on occupation. The authors should state which quantity was matched, re-run the simulation with the excitation set by the measured RXD drop, and show how sensitive the simulated distribution and τ_RDS(q) are to this amplitude.

6. **The RXD/RDS decomposition and the fitted time constants are underdetermined where they matter most.** Eq. S3 as written is a sum of decaying exponentials, A·exp(−t/τ), which cannot describe a step drop plus a rising component; the actual function (presumably a step at t₀ convolved with the 60 fs resolution times [1 − exp(−t/τ)] terms) and which parameters were free, fixed or shared across q (e.g. τ_RXD) should be stated. τ_RDS reaches the top of the axis (~4 ps) near q = 0.01 r.l.u. in Fig. 3F and ~13 ps along [h 0 h] in Fig. S9E, while the fit window is 4 ps; over such a window the rise is nearly linear, so only A/τ is constrained, and the small-q points in Fig. 3I that carry the "∝ q" claim are extrapolations. Near q_RXD the RXD component is several times larger than the RDS component, so the two amplitudes are likely strongly covariant. Fig. S8 shows the trace at (0.48, 0.01, −0.52) is non-monotonic, peaking at ~5 ps and plateauing after ~10 ps, which a single rising exponential cannot capture. The authors should give fit covariances or uncertainties, refit with longer delay windows where data exist, report how τ_RDS changes with the fit window, mark points whose τ exceeds the window, and either fit 1/τ ∝ |q| with a stated uncertainty (the outermost points have large error bars) or soften the statement.

8. **The scope of the claims exceeds the evidence.** The abstract and Conclusions state as an observation that non-thermal magnons are generated "throughout reciprocal space" (citing Fig. 3J), but the data cover only |q| ≤ 0.08 r.l.u. around q_RXD along two directions, excluding the hatched region; the zone-wide distribution comes from the model (Fig. S14B). Statements that the framework "transcends phenomenological models and generalizes across materials", is "generalizable to any gapped magnetic insulators", establishes "design principles" and "complete hierarchical energy transfer pathways", and enables "in principle, attosecond-scale investigations" (pp. 2, 9, 10) rest on one compound at one temperature, and the "faster recovery with overlapping dispersions" principle is not tested. The authors should mark which statements are measured and which modelled, reword the abstract accordingly, and move the cross-material and design statements to an outlook (or add a material with a different magnon–phonon overlap). The claim that tr-RDS of magnons "has not yet been experimentally demonstrated" should be supported by a discussion of prior time-resolved magnetic diffuse or small-angle scattering work.

9. **The momentum-resolved data cover one crystal, one temperature and one fluence.** All tr-RDS data are from one CuO crystal at 190 K, and the q-resolved maps are at one fluence. The magnon picture predicts that the RDS amplitude and τ_RDS(q) vary with the equilibrium Bose population, since magnon–magnon rates depend strongly on thermal magnon density; repeating the key q-scans at a lower base temperature (~100–150 K) would sharply test the quasi-thermalization mechanism and also move the measurement away from critical fluctuations (concern 2). The crystal has twin domains (Fig. S4), and the statement that other domains do not contribute is plausible for the Bragg peak but not demonstrated for diffuse intensity within ±0.08 r.l.u. The authors should state how many crystals and beamtimes contributed and confirm that the RDS results were reproduced at a second spot or on a second sample.

The orthorhombic cell with one Cu per layer (Fig. S13A) makes the two magnon branches degenerate (p. S15) exceeds the acoustic gap that controls accumulation at Γ and the claimed bottleneck; convergence as it is reduced should be shown.

12. **The model is used to explain the b*/[h 0 h] amplitude anisotropy it may not contain.** The main text attributes the much larger RDS amplitude along [h 0 h] than along b* (Fig. S9E vs. Fig. 3F) to "anisotropy in magnon dispersion as expected from the symmetry", but the geometric factors F_αα′ (Eq. 13. **Long-lived photocarriers are excluded only by citing other cuprates.** Magnon–electron scattering is ruled out "since the electrons are already thermalized within femtosecond timescales" (refs. 30, 31), a ~200 fs doublon lifetime from other copper oxides, not CuO. Photo-doped O 2p holes, polarons, excitons or trapped carriers may persist for picoseconds, and the Supplementary Text itself invokes significant electronic thermal conductivity from defects; continued magnon generation by carrier recombination would also give a picosecond RDS rise. The authors should measure transient optical reflectivity or absorption on the same crystal at 400 nm pump and 190 K, and/or measure the fluence dependence of τ_RDS(q): magnon–magnon rates should increase with magnon density, roughly with fluence below saturation, while relaxation dominated by phonons or carriers would scale differently (Fig. 2F already gives the amplitude at 4.5 ps).
