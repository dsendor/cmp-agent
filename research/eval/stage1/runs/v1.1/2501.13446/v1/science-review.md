A. Must address

1. The measured stacking fraction does not follow the magnetic evolution, and structure and magnetism were measured on different samples under different conditions. The central claim (abstract, Discussion pp. 17–18, Conclusions) is that a growing fraction of A-A stacking progressively lowers T_C and the bulk moment; p. 17 says the process "gradually decreases the Curie temperature". The paper's own data do not show this. Fig. 5b gives an R-3 volume fraction of about 0.55 near ambient pressure, about 0.5 at 3 and 5.7 GPa and about 0.42 at 6.4 GPa, essentially flat up to 5.7 GPa, with the drop only at 6.4–8.4 GPa. Over the same range T_C falls from 33 K to about 15–19 K (Fig. 3), and the moment at 10 K and 2 T falls from 2.8 to about 2.1 μB/f.u. by 1.1 GPa and to about 0.85–0.9 μB/f.u. by 5.1 GPa (Fig. 2). Conversely, crystals that are already about 45% P-3m1 at ambient pressure (and up to about 20% even in the best sample) show the ideal CrBr3 values (T_C = 33 K, M_s ≈ 2.8 μB/f.u.), a sharp ferromagnetic transition, no metamagnetic step in the 0 GPa loop of Fig. 2, and, according to the Introduction (p. 2), "no antiferromagnetic contribution"; if P-3m1 regions carried AFM interlayer coupling, the ambient isotherms should reveal them. The T_C values of the different runs in Fig. 3 also agree, although cutting is said to produce varying P-3m1 fractions from sample to sample. The authors' own Monte Carlo simulation (Fig. 13a) keeps M/M0 at T = 0 equal to 1 for every AA fraction up to 45% and lowers T_C only from 39 K to 28 K, so it reproduces neither the moment reduction in Fig. 2 nor a T_C collapse. In addition, all XRD and Raman data were taken at room temperature (Methods, Table 1, Fig. 5) on one crystal, while all magnetic data were taken at 2–50 K, in a different cell (Be/Cu turnbuckle DAC), on other crystals that were cut to fit the cell, a step the authors say generates P-3m1 (pp. 3–4). Stacking energetics in CrX3 depend strongly on temperature (CrBr3 itself has a 420 K structural transition, and CrI3 retains monoclinic stacking at low temperature), so the pressure at which R-3 converts at 300 K need not apply at 10 K, and the stacking state of the magnetization samples and its evolution on cooling under pressure are unknown. Read together, the data suggest that the P-3m1 fraction measured by room-temperature XRD does not control T_C, which undercuts the claim that ferromagnetism is suppressed "due to pressure-induced layer stacking variation". The authors should: report the phase fraction of the actual magnetization samples, before loading and after unloading; determine the stacking state at low temperature, ideally under pressure (low-temperature high-pressure XRD or neutron diffraction at one or two pressures), or at least at ambient pressure and low temperature on a crystal of known phase fraction; plot T_C(p), M_s(p) and the measured P-3m1 fraction on a common pressure axis; explicitly reconcile the near-flat phase fraction below 6 GPa and the 45% ambient P-3m1 fraction with the magnetic data; and, ideally, take one crystal through the whole sequence (XRD stacking fraction at ambient pressure, then magnetization under pressure). A simple ambient-pressure test is also available: measure magnetization (high-field M(H) and low-field M(T)) of deliberately cut or deformed crystals with a large P-3m1 fraction next to as-grown R-3 crystals. If the reconciliation fails, the mechanism should be presented as a hypothesis, not as the explanation.

2. The claimed antiferromagnetic high-pressure state with intact Cr moments is not tested by any magnetic measurement. The abstract, Discussion (pp. 17–18) and Conclusions state that above p_c ferromagnetism is lost "despite the individual Cr ion moments remaining intact" and is replaced by an "antiferromagnetic phase" of AFM-coupled layers. The only data above p_c are a featureless M(T) at 9.2 GPa in 0.01 T (Fig. 1) and a linear M(H) loop to 2 T at 10 K reaching about 0.5 μB/f.u. (Fig. 2). In an A-type state (ferromagnetic layers coupled antiferromagnetically), intralayer order survives, so M(T) should show a Néel anomaly at a temperature comparable to T_C, because the intralayer J dominates. M(H) should show a spin-flip or spin-flop transition toward about 3 μB/f.u. at a field set by J_L; with the authors' J_L = −0.35 meV per Cr and S = 3/2 that field is several tesla, so the 2 T range in Fig. 2 cannot distinguish this scenario from a paramagnet or a reduced Cr moment. The same applies to the missing moment at intermediate pressures (2.1, 0.85 and 0.5 μB/f.u. at 1.1, 5.1 and 9.2 GPa): if it comes from antiparallel-coupled layers it should be recovered at high field. The authors should: measure M(H) up to the 7 T limit of the MPMS at 2–5 K and several temperatures, at 9.2 GPa, at about 6–7 GPa and at intermediate pressures; measure M(T) at 0.5–1 T, or analyse dM/dT, for a Néel-type anomaly above p_c; and compare the high-field slope and any saturation with the moment expected from J_L. A cheap and decisive test is also available: Table S1 shows the P-3m1 phase is retained after unloading, so the authors can measure a fully transformed, decompressed crystal at ambient pressure with standard SQUID/VSM and no cell background; the model predicts it should no longer be a bulk ferromagnet with 3 μB/f.u. They should also report whether ferromagnetism recovers on decompression in the magnetization runs. Without such data the paper establishes a suppression of the ferromagnetic signal, not its mechanism or the nature of the high-pressure state; otherwise the "antiferromagnetic phase with intact moments" should be reduced to a hypothesis in the abstract and conclusions.

3. The step from "the R-3 satellites vanish and an averaged P-3m1 cell with 2/3 Cr occupancy fits" to "A-A stacking" is not demonstrated, yet the sign of the interlayer coupling depends on it. The refined P-3m1 cell (a = 3.644 Å, c = 6.116 Å, Cr 2/3 on 1a; Tables 1 and 3) is a CdI2-type subcell with a = a(R-3)/√3, in which the honeycomb Cr/vacancy order is averaged away. That average arises when the honeycomb vacancy positions of successive layers are uncorrelated, i.e. random lateral stacking, as the authors themselves describe on p. 11 ("the placement of the adjacent layer is randomized..., which would lead to seemingly 2/3 occupancy"). Losing the 1/3 reflections means losing ABC order; it does not identify A-A registry. Coherent A-A stacking would keep the honeycomb registry and index in an ordered one-layer P-31m-type cell with a ≈ 6.3 Å and c ≈ 6.1 Å, with sharp in-plane superstructure reflections at integer l, not a 2/3-occupied 1a site in a 3.64 Å cell. The argument on pp. 11–12 that "the only viable option" is the A-A stacking fault therefore does not follow, and alternatives (random shifts, monoclinic C2/m-type faults, which favour AFM coupling in CrI3, and mixtures) are not tested. This matters because J_L = −0.35 meV and the AFM coupling taken from Ref. [36] are specific to A-A registry; Refs. [36] and [74] show that the coupling sign differs between stacking types, so a random mixture need not give net AFM coupling. The authors should: report whether reflections or diffuse rods at h − k ≠ 3n (6.3 Å setting) are present above 8.4 GPa and in the unloaded sample, and whether they are sharp or streaked along c*, using intensity profiles along L in the DAC data and in the reciprocal-space maps (and explain the unexplained histograms in Fig. 4); refine or simulate the competing models (A-A P-31m, random stacking, monoclinic faults, mixed R-3/A-A faulting) against the same data, for example with a DIFFaX/FAULTS-type model, to estimate the probability of each stacking vector; compare the measured c-axis spacing with the DFT interlayer distance for AA versus ABC stacking; and state which stacking the DFT J_L = −0.35 meV refers to. If only random stacking can be established, the magnetic model should use the corresponding distribution of stacking-dependent couplings rather than treating all faults as AFM.

4. The proposed mechanism and the spin model do not account for the collapse of the ordering temperature. Interlayer coupling of either sign (J_L = 0.83 and −0.35 meV, p. 16) is small compared with the intralayer J1–J3. In a quasi-2D magnet, changing the sign of interlayer coupling mainly changes the relative orientation of ferromagnetic layers; each layer still orders near the bulk ordering temperature, so pure A-A stacking should give an A-type antiferromagnet with T_N comparable to the ambient T_C, and switching interface signs mostly produces compensated or ferrimagnetic layer sequences with a similar ordering temperature, not loss of order. The authors' own simulation shows this: at 45% AA fraction T_C is still 28 K (Fig. 13b), and simulations stop at 45%, so the model has not been shown to reach the experimental regime (T_C ≈ 6 K at 6.4 GPa, no transition at 9.2 GPa). Moreover, the simulation assigns AA or AB coupling to randomly chosen atoms (p. 16), whereas a stacking fault flips the coupling across a whole layer interface, so the model does not represent the proposed structure. The intralayer J1–J3 are taken from the ambient-pressure neutron study (Ref. [73]) and held fixed, although the Introduction cites DFT predicting that J1 grows with pressure, which would raise T_C. The simulated T_C at 0% AA (39 K) also exceeds the measured 33 K. The authors should simulate interface-wise (planar) faults over the full range up to 100% AA in, e.g., a ten-layer or larger stack, include pressure-dependent intralayer exchange, report the ordering temperature (for example via the staggered layer magnetization) and the T = 0 net moment separately, and compare T_C/T_C(0) and M(0) against fault fraction quantitatively with Figs. 2 and 3. Without this, the experimental T_C collapse may need an additional cause, such as weakened intralayer exchange or non-hydrostatic stress, and the stacking scenario is at most part of the explanation.

5. The high-pressure magnetization normalization, background and protocol are not documented well enough to support the quantitative moment reduction (2.8 → 0.9 → about 0.5 μB/f.u.). The empty-cell reference was measured separately (p. 4), and at 9.2 GPa the residual signal is small and linear (Fig. 2), which is what an imperfect background subtraction would leave; at 6.4–9.2 GPa the signal at 0.01 T is only about 0.01 μB/f.u. The paper does not state how the sample mass in the DAC was determined or its uncertainty, whether the sample stayed intact (Fig. S1a shows fragmentation in some media), whether the curves in Fig. 2 come from one sample and loading or from different samples and runs (Fig. 3 mentions "several different runs"), or which pressures were measured on increasing and which on decreasing pressure. The 0 GPa curves in Figs. 1–2 appear to come from a different setup (presumably PPMS VSM outside the cell) and possibly a different sample, so the pressure dependence mixes samples and instruments; this may explain the 10% drop of M_s between 0 and 0.4 GPa (2.8 versus about 2.5 μB/f.u.). The ambient M_s of about 2.8 μB/f.u. is itself below the 3 μB expected for Cr3+. It is not stated whether M(T) is ZFC or FC or how T_C was extracted (onset, inflection, dM/dT minimum); arrows appear only for the AC data in Fig. S2. Low-field (0.01 T) M(T) is dominated by domains and demagnetization and should not be read as tracking M_s. The 5.9 GPa curve in Fig. 1 rises near 14–16 K and falls below about 12 K, a re-entrant shape unusual for a ferromagnet that could signal competing AFM interlayer order or a background or temperature artifact. The authors should show raw sample-plus-cell and empty-cell moments for at least one high-pressure field sweep, state the sample mass, its determination and uncertainty, give the normalization procedure with error bars, show a same-sample ambient reference, define T_C with an error bar for each point, comment on the ambient 2.8 μB value and the 0–0.4 GPa drop, and discuss the 5.9 GPa curve.

6. Pressure determination at low temperature and hydrostaticity are not specified, although the proposed mechanism is stress-induced stacking faulting. The text does not say whether the ruby pressure in the Be–Cu turnbuckle cell was read at low temperature or only at room temperature for each point in Figs. 1–3; such cells typically change pressure by several tenths of a GPa on cooling. The magnetization runs use an unspecified "liquid pressure medium". The 16:3:1 methanol–ethanol–water medium freezes on cooling, and its stated hydrostatic limit of "~14 GPa" (p. 4) is above the commonly quoted ~10 GPa at room temperature; the XRD transition (6.4–8.4 GPa) and the Raman p_c (~10 GPa) lie close to where it becomes non-hydrostatic, and some XRD points lie beyond it. The paper itself shows that shear from cutting is enough to create P-3m1 (p. 3), that CrBr3 disintegrates in non-hydrostatic media (Fig. S1a), and that the transformation is irreversible on unloading (Table S1); all suggest that deviatoric stress, not hydrostatic pressure alone, may drive the stacking change, and non-hydrostatic stress on cooling could alter the stacking of the magnetization samples relative to room-temperature XRD. The authors should state how and at what temperature pressure was determined, name the medium used for each magnetization run, give pressure uncertainties as horizontal error bars in Fig. 3 and report pressure gradients (several rubies or ruby linewidths), discuss the role of non-hydrostatic stress, and ideally repeat a key XRD or magnetization point with a gas medium (He or Ne).

10. The three probes give different critical pressures, and the Raman phase assignment is weak. Magnetization gives p_c ≈ 6.5 GPa (low temperature), XRD 6.4–8.4 GPa (300 K) and Raman ≈ 10–11 GPa (300 K; Fig. 10, Conclusions "≈10 GPa"), yet all are presented as one transition, and the text says the Raman intensity loss above 11 GPa "corresponds to the critical pressure p_c detected by X-ray diffraction", which it does not. The assignment of soft modes to R-3 and hard modes to P-3m1 (pp. 14–15, Fig. 10) rests only on pressure slopes, with no factor-group analysis, polarization selection rules or calculated phonons. The CdI2-type P-3m1 average cell with one formula unit has only A1g + Eg Raman modes, whereas four or five "red" modes are attributed to P-3m1; conversely, since the averaged cell with fractional Cr is not a real periodic structure, if the local structure is honeycomb the average-structure mode count does not apply and the phases may not be separable by Raman at all. The 181.8 cm⁻¹ mode is labelled A2g, which is not Raman active in D3d and not an irrep of C3i (R-3, whose Raman modes are Ag and Eg). The intensity loss above ~11 GPa is attributed to a dielectric change and "gradual metallization", but the DFT gap at 10 GPa is still 1.39 eV, and pressure-driven changes in resonance with the 2.41 eV (514.5 nm) excitation (CrBr3 Raman is known to be resonant, Ref. [37]) are a more direct candidate. The authors should give mode counts for both structures, use the polarized spectra they say were recorded to check symmetries, compute Γ-point phonons and pressure coefficients for R-3 and A-A stackings, correct all mode labels, show spectra after decompression, discuss the resonance alternative, and discuss why the critical pressures differ, including the different temperatures.

11. The "trimorphism" and "new phase" claims are overstated. The P-3m1 component appears after cutting, bending or exfoliating as-grown R-3 crystals (p. 3); the high-resolution reciprocal-space maps of an as-grown crystal (Figs. 7 and 8) confirm R-3 and only "cannot exclude" P-3m1 (p. 13); and the P-3m1 state persists after unloading (Table S1). This is the behaviour of a mechanically induced, metastable, stacking-disordered state, not a thermodynamically stable third polymorph (Conclusions, "We have observed trimorphism in CrBr3"; abstract, "becomes stable"; p. 3). The authors should rephrase these statements or provide evidence of thermodynamic stability, such as DFT enthalpies, reversibility, or P-3m1 in an uncut as-grown crystal (for example via the h − k ≠ 3n rods of item 3).
