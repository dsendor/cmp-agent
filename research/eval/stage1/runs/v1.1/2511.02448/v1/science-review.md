A. Must address

1. The dispersion formula used to extract J^orb is the next-nearest-neighbour form, but the PNO result is interpreted as nearest-neighbour superexchange, and the fitted J^orb does not reproduce the bandwidths marked in Fig. 5. Eq. (1), ε_k = 2J^orb cos kx cos ky, is the momentum dependence of a diagonal (NNN) hop, since cos kx cos ky = ½[cos(kx+ky) + cos(kx−ky)]; Ref. 43 used it for that reason. An NN hop of a single orbital excitation on a square lattice gives ε_k ∝ 2J(cos kx + cos ky), which predicts a different pattern along the measured paths. In the NNN form, X = (0.5,0) and (0.25,0.25) differ by 2J and the [H,H] dispersion is cos²-like. In the NN form, X and (0.25,0.25) are degenerate, the [H,H] bandwidth exceeds the [H,0] one, and the [H,0] bandwidth is half the full Γ–M bandwidth. Yet the paper fits PNO with Eq. (1), obtains J^orb = +14 ± 4 meV, and sets it against the calculated J_NN^orb = 14.5 meV (Table III) as evidence that NN superexchange dominates (pp. 6–7, abstract). The numbers are also internally inconsistent. With J^orb = 14 meV, Eq. (1) predicts a drop of about 56 meV from Γ to (0.475,0) and about 20 meV (2J(1 − 0.29)) from Γ to (0.34,0.34), whereas Fig. 5(a) marks 90 and 62 meV. For CCO, J^orb = −11 meV gives about 44 and 22 meV, against the 56 and 34 meV marked in Fig. 5(c). Either the dashed-line values do not mean what the caption says, or Eq. (1) does not describe the data; in either case the "NN versus NNN" interpretation, the paper's most novel claim, is not supported by the fit actually performed. The authors should state which dispersion relation the charge-transfer model of Ref. 43 gives for NN and for NNN orbital superexchange; fit the PNO and CCO dxy dispersions with the NN form, the NNN form and a combined J_NN + J_NNN form, giving residuals and any constant offset and reporting which form the data distinguish (for example through the relative energies at X and (0.25,0.25)); overlay the fitted curves on both branches; explain how the 62/90/34/56 meV markers relate to them; and restate the comparison with Table III using the matching functional form. If the PNO data are in fact better described by the cos kx cos ky form with positive sign, the NN interpretation in the abstract needs revisiting.

2. The PNO dxy dispersion, a shift of a few tens of meV, is not shown to be robust against fitting and geometry systematics, and could come from changing weights of overlapping components rather than a true shift. In PNO the dxy feature is broad (FWHM ≈ 0.5 eV in Fig. 2c). It overlaps the 0.7 eV "RE" peak on one side, the broad dxz/yz peak on the other, and the self-doping charge continuum underneath. The claimed dispersion (50–90 meV) is comparable to the 40 meV resolution and a small fraction of the peak width. At fixed 2θ = 149.5°, scanning H moves the geometry continuously from grazing incidence (negative H) to grazing emission (positive H), with the switch at Γ, where PNO has its dispersion maximum. Fig. 4 shows, and the text stresses (p. 6), that the polarization character and cross-section of the 1.29 eV dxy peak in PNO are "very different" between positive and negative H, and the σ-polarized dxy intensity at H = 0.4 exceeds the single-ion prediction. A smooth, geometry-driven change in the relative weights of these overlapping components, or in self-absorption, can shift a fitted centroid by tens of meV and mimic a dispersion. The CCO dd peaks are sharp, so this is mainly a PNO concern, but the claim of *opposite* dispersions in the two materials depends on it. The authors should state in the main text which fit parameters (widths, positions of the other components, background terms) were fixed or free across momenta; show the PNO dxy energy from several fitting schemes (free versus fixed widths of dxy, dxz/yz and the 0.7 eV peak; with and without the 0.7 eV peak and the continuum; dxz/yz position free versus fixed); compare ±H points at equal |Q| in grazing-incidence and grazing-emission geometries, and with σ as well as π incident polarization; show a model-free estimator (first moment over a fixed window, or position of the maximum) alongside the multi-peak fit; give the reduced χ² for a dispersionless model; and, if feasible, simulate with the single-ion model how far the apparent centroid moves from cross-section changes alone.

3. The finite interlayer coupling J⊥ and the "three-dimensional antiferromagnetic order" in PNO are not established by the data shown, and the paper contradicts itself on whether PNO is ordered. The abstract and p. 4 state that the similar out-of-plane couplings indicate "that both materials support a three-dimensional antiferromagnetic order", and Fig. 1a draws an ordered AFM lattice for both compounds. Page 6, however, rests the orbiton interpretation on the opposite premise: "the nickelate, unlike the cuprate, does not present evidence of long-range AFM order", so the magnetic string effect "is expected to be absent or much weaker". The Introduction (Refs. 13, 19, 20, 38) likewise notes that bulk infinite-layer nickelates show no long-range order and show spin-glass behaviour; an LSW fit to paramagnon dispersions measures spin correlations, not order. The only evidence for J⊥ = 5.9 ± 1.1 meV is the finite magnon energy near Γ (60–80 meV, Fig. 3a), extracted from the L dependence of the gap ("when approaching the Γ point at non-integer L values, the single-magnon energy remains large"). But the Methods state that L "was not fixed" at fixed 2θ and that this "is not an issue since the in-plane Js are still dominating", which contradicts using that same L variation to determine J⊥. The L values at each point are not given (by one estimate L ≈ 0.43 at Γ for PNO), and no L scan at fixed in-plane momentum is shown. Near Γ the PNO magnon sits within one or two resolution widths of a very strong specular/elastic line (Fig. 1e), coincides with the ~60–80 meV phonon included in the fit, has damping (~100 meV, Fig. 3b) exceeding its fitted energy, and carries the largest error bars in Fig. 3a. A finite zone-centre energy could equally come from exchange or single-ion anisotropy (Ref. 59, cited by the paper), from a damped paramagnon whose peak is pushed up by damping, from the 40 meV resolution combined with elastic subtraction, from the fit misattributing phonon or elastic tails, or from short-range correlations without order; these make different predictions for the L dependence. The authors should state clearly whether they claim long-range order in PNO and reconcile p. 6 with the abstract and Fig. 1a; list the L value of each point in Fig. 3a (and at representative points in the Fig. 1 or Fig. 3 caption) and confirm the SpinW fit used them rather than a fixed L; show raw spectra and fit decompositions at the near-Γ momenta for PNO; test whether a model with J⊥ = 0 plus an anisotropy gap, or a 2D model with free phonon parameters, fits equally well, giving the sensitivity of χ²; and ideally measure the magnon energy versus L at fixed small in-plane Q (by varying 2θ or the photon energy within the resonance). Otherwise they should reduce the claim to "consistent with a finite interlayer coupling" and drop "three-dimensional antiferromagnetic order" for PNO. The related statement on p. 5 that comparable J⊥ values "give a proof of the fully occurred topotactic reduction of the PNO samples and the absence of interstitial oxygen atoms" is not supported by a magnon fit, which is not a direct probe of residual apical oxygen; it should be removed or backed by XRD or XAS evidence.

4. The microscopic estimate in Tables II–III has tuned inputs, so its agreement with experiment is weak validation. For PNO all hoppings were scaled ad hoc to 80% of the CCO values; Δ = 8 eV was "chosen sufficiently larger than U also providing reasonable results", which reads as selection by outcome; U = 3.2 eV (at the low end of common Ni estimates) and J_H = 0.65 eV come from different references. The match J_NN = 14.5 meV against 14 ± 4 meV, and the two-orders-of-magnitude suppression of J_NNN (about −0.1 meV), may depend strongly on Δ, U and t_pd. For CCO the model gives J_NN = 22 meV, which is not observed; the argument that the magnon string effect suppresses it is external to the model, so the comparison in Table III is quantitative only for the PNO NN term, and the text should say so. The authors should show how J_NN^orb and J_NNN^orb vary over plausible ranges of Δ (for example 4–9 eV), U and the hopping scale; show that the sign and magnitude of J_NN − J_NNN, and the near-vanishing of J_NNN in PNO, are robust; explain physically why J_NNN collapses in PNO; and identify the abstract's "markedly different Δ" as a model input rather than a quantity determined here.

5. The damping comparison with Bi2201 does not isolate self-doping as the cause of the mild magnon damping, and the conclusion drawn from it is overstated. Fig. 3b compares PNO damping (~100 meV, this work, 40 meV resolution, the authors' fitting) with Bi2201 values from Ref. 49: a single-layer cuprate with apical oxygen, measured in a different experiment with different resolution and possibly a different fitting protocol, at known hole doping p. The self-doped hole density of PNO is not estimated, so "much milder impact" cannot be stated per carrier. The PNO broadening could also come from extrinsic sources in a ~5 nm reduced film: structural disorder, stacking faults, incomplete or inhomogeneous reduction, residual apical oxygen, strain, or capping-layer and interface effects. The paper also notes that the single-peak fit overestimates damping near (0.5,0) in CCO. Near Γ the PNO magnon energy (~80 meV) is below its damping (~100 meV), so "sharp" (pp. 2–3) is too strong along part of the path. The statement that self-doping gives IL nickelates "a non-disruptive way to achieve superconductivity which is totally absent in copper oxides" (p. 4) goes well beyond this comparison and overlooks electron-doped cuprates and the persistent paramagnons in hole-doped cuprates. The authors should state whether the Bi2201 data were refitted with the same model and resolution; estimate the self-doped hole density (for example from O K-edge or Ni L-edge XAS, or Hall data on these films); give a dimensionless damping measure (Γ/E); discuss non-doping sources of damping; compare against doped infinite-layer nickelates measured and fitted the same way (such as their own Nd1−xSrxNiO2 data, Ref. 46, or Sr-doped PNO or a differently reduced PNO film), and if possible doped infinite-layer cuprate data; and otherwise temper the conclusion, including the "totally absent" statement.

RIXS was done at 20 K, while the resistive onset is slightly below 11 K and zero resistance is reached only below 4 K. The wording in Section II and the comparison with muon data (Ref. 39) should be changed accordingly, or a spectrum below Tc added if technically possible.

7. The five-parameter LSW fit (J1–J4, J⊥) to dispersions along two cuts may not be unique, and the exchange constants and their comparison with the literature depend on it. For PNO, J2 = −8.3 meV (ferromagnetic), J3 = 4.2 meV and J4 = 8.0 meV (J4 > J3) are fitted alongside J1 and J⊥ to roughly 40 points, with uncertainties as small as ±0.3 meV on J1 (45.8 ± 0.3 meV), which looks small given that J2, J3, J4 and ring-exchange terms are known to trade off against each other and against J1. The definition of J4 appears only in the Fig. 3a inset, and in Ref. 48-type models the definitions mix ring exchange and longer-range terms, so "J1 about half of CCO" depends on convention. The PNO dispersion has its [H,H] maximum at (0.2,0.2) rather than at the zone boundary (p. 5), and Fig. 3a does not make clear whether the fit reproduces this. LSW also assumes long-range order, which PNO is said to lack (see concern 3). The authors should give the parameter correlation matrix or confidence regions; show whether simpler models (J1–J2–J3 + J⊥, J1 + ring exchange, J1, J2 + J⊥, or an effective one-band Hubbard parametrization) fit significantly worse; report a model-independent quantity such as E(0.5,0) − E(0.25,0.25) or the bandwidth for both compounds; state whether a quantum renormalization factor Z_c was applied consistently with the literature values quoted (Refs. 12, 24, 46, 47, 57) and compare J1 with those fits in the same convention; comment on the larger CCO magnon width near (0.5,0), which the single-peak fit is said to misrepresent, and whether it biases J1 for CCO; and comment on whether LSW is adequate for a disordered or short-range-correlated state.

9. Epitaxial strain differs between the two films and is not separated from intrinsic chemistry when the exchange constants and dd energies are compared. PNO is on SrTiO3 with an STO cap (a = 3.94 Å), and CCO is on NdGaO3 (a = 3.86 Å). Strain is known to change J in infinite-layer nickelates (Ref. 57). Part of the J1 ratio and of the dxy energy difference, which p. 6 partly attributes to "different ... in-plane lattice constants", may reflect the substrate. A short quantitative estimate, for example J1 ∝ a^(−n) with a typical n, would show how much of the factor of two is intrinsic.
