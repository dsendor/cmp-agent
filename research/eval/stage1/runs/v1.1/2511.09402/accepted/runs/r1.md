Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper uses spectroscopic-imaging STM on Ti-doped CsV3Sb5, where long-range CDW order is suppressed at x = 0.15. It reports that the low-energy QPI patterns from the Sb pz alpha pocket and the V d delta pockets break both rotational and all vertical mirror symmetries. The authors read this as a "chiral-nematic Fermi liquid": a d-wave Pomeranchuk distortion whose director is rotated away from the mirror planes. They argue that this is the parent state of the chiral CDW and of the superconducting phases across the AV3Sb5 phase diagram. If the result holds, it would identify a symmetry-broken electronic background, not tied to translation symmetry breaking, behind the much-debated rotation- and mirror-breaking phenomenology of kagome metals. That would be of broad interest.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- With the CDW absent (x = 0.15, Fig. 1d,e), the Fermi-surface symmetry can be probed without the 2a0 and 4a0 modulations that complicate QPI in pristine CsV3Sb5. Choosing this regime is well motivated.
- The analysis is careful and direction-resolved: side-by-side QPI cuts aligned on the Bragg peaks (Fig. 2b), line profiles (Fig. 2c,d), and energy-resolved dispersions along three equivalent directions (Fig. 3). The anisotropy is shown in several observables (q1, q2, q3 lengths; triangle spacings d12, d13, d23; tilt of the q3 segment), not one.
- The same handedness-type distortion appears across Ti doping (x = 0, 0.04, 0.05, 0.15, 0.27; Fig. 4g), on the Cs-terminated surface, and in Bogoliubov QPI below Tc. An opposite-chirality region is reported in the same sample (Fig. 2g). This reproducibility argues against a one-off artefact.
- The phenomenological model, a cos(2θ + φ) distortion of DFT contours, is simple, and its simulated QPI (Fig. 2f) reproduces the qualitative features of the data.
- The mass renormalisations are internally consistent with the quoted velocities. I checked the ratios m*/mDFT = (1.33, 1.19, 1.47), (1.53, 1.95, 2.33) and (1.45, 1.65, 1.88), and they follow from the numbers given. The comparison with ARPES and DFT+DMFT is discussed honestly.
- The raw data are deposited publicly.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. Uniaxial or shear strain, and scan-frame distortion, are not ruled out as the source of the "chiral-nematic" distortion. The distortion the authors fit, kF(θ) = kF0(θ) + η cos(2θ + φ) with φ = −π/6, is a two-fold (nematic) distortion whose director lies between the σv and σv' mirror planes. This is exactly what a uniform in-plane strain would produce if its principal axis were not along a high-symmetry direction. Such strain is common from differential thermal contraction against the sample plate, from cleaving, or from Ti-induced local stress. A strain of a few tenths of a percent would deform the Fermi surface at the level reported: the average distortion is 1.4–2.2% of |Q_Bragg| (Supplementary Table 1, p. 3). The opposite-handedness region "several hundred nanometers away" (Fig. 2g) is equally consistent with a spatially varying strain direction. The Discussion (p. 8) rules out point defects by T-matrix calculations, but it never discusses strain.

   A second point makes this worse. The affine transformation used to "restore a perfectly symmetric lattice" from the Bragg points (Methods, p. 9; applied to Figs. 2b and 4d) removes the lattice signature of a uniform strain, and of piezo or drift distortion, that would show whether such a deformation is present. The size of the claimed effect also matters. With fields of view of order 50–80 nm, one FT pixel is roughly 0.5–1% of |Q_Bragg|. The direction-to-direction differences in q1 at E ≈ 0 in Fig. 3c are about 0.01–0.02 |Q_Bragg|, i.e. only one to a few pixels. That is the regime where residual nonlinear scanner distortion, creep and slow-scan drift matter. Fast-scan versus slow-scan anisotropy in particular has a fixed orientation in the scan frame and can mimic a twofold distortion at a generic angle.

   This is the central alternative explanation for the main claim, so it needs a direct answer. The authors should:
   (a) report the uncorrected Bragg-peak positions and the affine matrix for each dataset, and show that any lattice strain is far too small, or wrongly oriented, to produce the observed QPI anisotropy. A rigid-band estimate of strain-induced Fermi-surface deformation from the DFT would quantify "too small".
   (b) show the same region scanned with the scan frame rotated (for example by 60° or 90°), so the chirality is shown to be tied to the crystal axes and not to the scan axes.
   (c) ideally, image a boundary between opposite-handedness regions within one field of view with the same tip, together with the local Bragg-peak geometry on both sides.
   (d) state for each sample the measured handedness and director angle φ, to show whether φ is pinned to −π/6 (pointing to an intrinsic electronic origin) or varies (pointing to an extrinsic one).

B. Should address

2. The temperature scale T* and the "parent state" claim rest on inference, not on temperature-dependent data. T* ≈ 167 K is obtained by dividing an "average energy splitting" of −14.4 meV of the delta-pocket dispersions (Supplementary Table 1) by kB (p. 8). The paper does not explain why this splitting should set an ordering temperature; for a mean-field order parameter the relation would carry a numerical prefactor. No QPI data are shown above about 4 K. All data sit at 0.4 K, plus the B-QPI check just above Tc (Supplementary Fig. 4). The title and Fig. 4f place the chiral-nematic Fermi liquid above T_CDW in pristine AV3Sb5. Within the paper, however, the "parent" status rests on doping (the state persists where the CDW is absent) and not on temperature. The authors should measure QPI at x = 0.15 at several elevated temperatures (for example 20, 50, 100, 150 K) and show that the anisotropy persists and then vanishes. Failing that, they should rephrase T* and the "parent state" language as a hypothesis, and present Fig. 4f explicitly as a conjectured schematic.

3. Uncertainties on the velocity anisotropy and on the fitted distortion parameters are missing. The velocities v^m_{i,s} in three directions (p. 6; Supplementary Table 2) are quoted to six significant figures without uncertainties. In Fig. 3c the alpha-pocket data for the three directions overlap within error bars over much of the low-energy window. The ordering v^III < v^I < v^II therefore depends on the fit window around the "kink", which is not specified. The parameters η and φ of the distortion model (p. 3, Fig. 2e,f) are not given numerically: it is unclear whether they were fitted, and with what uncertainty, or chosen by eye. The authors should:
   - state the energy window used for each linear fit and give velocity uncertainties;
   - test the ordering against changes of window;
   - fit η and φ globally to the extracted q_i(E) in all three directions, with confidence intervals, so that "breaks all mirror symmetries" becomes a quantitative statement (φ significantly different from 0 and ±π/2).

4. The relation v_F = 2 v_s and the chiral-nematic model only apply approximately to the delta pockets. For the q2 and q3 inter-pocket vectors, the authors note (p. 6) that the scattering states are not related by inversion, and that v_F ≈ 2 v_s holds only "to leading order in the chiral-nematic distortion". The resulting renormalisations for the delta band (up to 2.33) fall outside the 1.29–1.71 DFT+DMFT range and are then interpreted as further evidence for the exotic state. This interpretation leans on the approximation that is least controlled. The authors should either propagate the distortion consistently, by computing q2,3(E) from the fitted distorted contours, or temper the interpretation of the direction-dependent delta-band renormalisation.

5. The basis for the "chiral-nematic superconducting state" is thin in the main text. The claim (Abstract; p. 8) rests on B-QPI at the subgap energy resembling the normal-state chiral pattern (Supplementary Fig. 8). The same pattern would appear if the normal-state Fermi surface were distorted, for any reason, and the gap were conventional. The observation therefore constrains the Fermi surface, not the pairing symmetry. The authors should present this as superconductivity emerging from (inheriting) a distorted Fermi surface, or show a gap-anisotropy measurement that itself breaks the mirrors.

6. Defect alignment and tip anisotropy are addressed only partly. The T-matrix calculations (Supplementary Figs. 18–20) cover representative isolated defects, and the authors note that no aligned defects are seen (Supplementary Fig. 13). They do not show that tip shape is excluded. An asymmetric tip convolves the real-space LDOS and can redistribute FT intensity anisotropically; that affects intensity-based features such as the 2.6:1.2:1.0 CDW peak ratio at x = 0.05 (Fig. 4c). The authors should list which datasets were taken with independently prepared tips, and show that the handedness is independent of the tip.

7. The statistics of handedness and domains are not reported. Fig. 2g shows one opposite-handedness region. The paper does not say how many samples, cleaves, fields of view and tips were measured at each doping, nor the fraction showing each handedness. A short table would let readers judge whether the effect is universal, and whether the domain structure behaves as expected for spontaneous symmetry breaking (roughly equal populations of both handednesses in the absence of a symmetry-breaking field).

8. The DFT reference has limitations that bear on the claim. Fermi surfaces are computed in the virtual-crystal approximation, without spin-orbit coupling, at a single kz = π (Methods, p. 9). The authors properly call the comparison qualitative. However, the claim of mirror breaking rests on deviations from DFT symmetry of only a few percent. The authors should confirm two points. First, that SOC, kz integration and the actual Ti distribution cannot lower the symmetry of the contours: VCA cannot by construction, but local Ti ordering or a surface relaxation could. Second, that the mirror-symmetric reference is therefore a strict constraint and not an approximation.

C. Minor

9. Fig. 4d orders the panels A−H_I, A−H_III, A−H_II, while Fig. 2b and the text use I, II, III. Please make them consistent or state the reason.

10. "Chirality" is used here for planar (2D) handedness of a director that breaks all vertical mirrors, not for 3D structural chirality or time-reversal breaking. The authors say this on p. 8, but it should be stated in the Introduction. Readers in this field will otherwise connect it with the TRSB chiral CDW literature.

11. Methods give a base temperature of 0.4 K and an electron temperature of 650 mK. Please state which value applies to the B-QPI data and to the Tc comparison.

12. The error-bar definition in Fig. 3 combines fit error and "pixel resolution". Please state the pixel size in units of |Q_Bragg| for each map, together with the field of view and the number of pixels.

13. Fig. 2a and 2g were recorded at different set points (500 pA versus 1000 pA) and lock-in amplitudes. Feenstra normalisation reduces set-point effects. Still, a statement that the Fig. 2g handedness is unchanged at matched set points would help.

14. The reference to T* ≈ 130 K from torque magnetometry (ref. 28) and the 167 K estimate here are presented as mutually supporting. Please comment on the difference.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Add a dedicated "artefact and strain controls" supplementary section: raw Bragg geometry, affine matrices, rotated-scan-frame data, and multiple tips. This would make the central claim much more robust.
- Fit the cos(2θ + φ) model quantitatively to all extracted q_i(E), with uncertainties on η and φ, and report φ for every dataset and domain.
- Even a coarse temperature series of QPI at x = 0.15 would turn T* from an estimate into a measurement.
- Consider imaging a handedness domain wall. A sharp boundary in φ with no change in lattice geometry would be the most direct evidence for spontaneous electronic chirality.

5. Verdict

Verdict: major revision
