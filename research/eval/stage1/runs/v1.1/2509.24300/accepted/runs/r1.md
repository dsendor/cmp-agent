Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper reports transport in exfoliated-style Ca2RuO4 nanoscale crystals (samples 1-5, 8) and MBE films (samples 6, 7). Some samples show a range below about 220 K where the resistance follows R ~ ln(1/T) rather than G ~ ln T, and a "vertical flow" in the β-function. The authors read both as a Bose glass of localized Cooper pairs that persists to about 220 K, between a Fermi glass and a superconducting phase. They also report two "quantum critical" sheet resistances, 65.7 kΩ ≈ (5/2)h/e² and 16.5 kΩ ≈ (5/2)h/4e², and a phase diagram organised by a localization-strength parameter dρ/dT. If the claims hold, they would be striking: bosonic localization at temperatures far above any known 2D superconductor–insulator system, in a correlated oxide. As presented, though, the evidence for Cooper pairs at 220 K rests almost entirely on the functional form of R(T) over a narrow temperature window.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The paper covers a range of samples (seven nanofilms and MBE films, plus bulk literature data) whose room-temperature-to-low-temperature behaviour runs systematically from Mott/VRH, through weakly localized, to superconducting (Figs. 1, 2, 4a).
- The β-function analysis (Fig. 5) puts bulk literature data and the new films on one Vollhardt–Wölfle-type curve, which is a compact way to show the crossover from strong to weak localization.
- The observation that thinning lowers the resistivity of Ca2RuO4 by 3-4 orders of magnitude, together with MBE films with a much shorter c-axis (Supplementary Table S1), is interesting in its own right and fits the strain/distortion tuning known from pressure and epitaxy studies.
- The phase-diagram framing (Fig. 4) gives a falsifiable organising parameter that other groups could test.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The Bose-glass identification rests only on telling R ~ ln(1/T) from G ~ ln T over a narrow window, with no test that pairs are present. Where: Fig. 3a,b and the text on p. 2 (sample 4, 101 < T < 220 K; sample 5, 105 < T < 183 K); Fig. 3c (sample 7); the abstract's "Cooper pairs persist up to high temperatures of ~220 K". Why it matters: over a factor of about 2 in temperature (ln T from about 4.6 to 5.4), ln(1/T) in R and ln T in G differ only through second-order curvature. Many fermionic mechanisms also bend these curves: electron–electron interaction corrections, a crossover toward VRH, two parallel conduction channels (for example a surface or interface layer next to the bulk of the flake), a phonon contribution, or the approach to the resistance maximum near 80-100 K. The paper gives no fit statistics, residuals or fit-window sensitivity; the lines in Fig. 3 are judged by eye. It also gives no measurement that distinguishes charge 2e from charge e carriers. What to do:
   - Give quantitative fit comparisons (residuals, χ² or reduced χ², and stability against moving the window edges) for both forms in every sample called a Bose glass.
   - Measure magnetoresistance in the 100-220 K range. Weak localization of electrons gives a characteristic negative MR with a dephasing field set by e; localized pairs should show a different field scale, consistent with 2e, and positive MR from pair breaking. Comparing samples 1-3 (Fermi glass) with 4-5 (Bose glass) under the same field would be a direct test.
   - Show at least one further pair-sensitive probe or signature, such as Little–Parks-type oscillations, Nernst signal, or tunnelling-gap or noise measurements, on a sample in the claimed Bose-glass regime.
   Without these, "Bose glass at 220 K" should be presented as one possible interpretation of a change in the curvature of R(T).

2. The β-function "vertical flow" is not independent of the ln T / ln(1/T) fits, and the dimensionless conductance it uses is not well defined. Where: Eqs. (1)-(4), Figs. 5 and 6a, and the text on p. 5 and the Fig. 5 caption ("an independent criterion ... beyond simple logarithmic fitting").
   - Eqs. (3) and (4) are derived by assuming either σ = σ0 + σ1 ln T or ρ = ρ0 + ρ1 ln(1/T). So the sign of β' only restates which of the two forms describes the data, i.e. the curvature of R(T). It is not a separate criterion.
   - Any R(T) that approaches a maximum, as samples 4 and 5 do near 80-100 K just below the claimed Bose-glass range, makes β' change sign and gives a "vertical" excursion when d ln g/d ln T passes through zero. Superconducting fluctuation conductivity above T_c^onset would do the same. The authors acknowledge such fluctuations elsewhere (Aslamazov–Larkin fit, Supplementary Fig. S3).
   - The scale factor 2/p with p = 1 is fixed by assumption, not measured. The same factor is applied to bulk 3D crystals (Nakatsuji data) and to 2D films.
   - g = (h/e²)σ is dimensionless only if σ is a sheet conductance. Several samples (Fig. 3c, Fig. 2b) are reported as bulk conductivity in S/cm, and the conversion per layer (or per film) used for Fig. 5 is not stated.
   What to do: state exactly how g is computed for each sample, including the thickness and layer count used. Show that the vertical flow is not just a consequence of the nearby resistance maximum or of fluctuation conductivity, for example by subtracting an Aslamazov–Larkin or Maki–Thompson contribution, or by showing that the flow starts well above where fluctuations matter. Also remove the claim that the flow is independent of the logarithmic fits.

3. The "two quantum critical points" and the critical resistances are not supported by critical-scaling analysis, and their numerical values depend on an uncertain normalization. Where: Fig. 6, p. 6 text, abstract and Conclusion (R^{BG-FG} = 65.7 kΩ ≈ (5/2)h/e², R^{SC-BG} = 16.5 kΩ ≈ (5/2)h/4e², the link to 8/π and to the 5/2 Pfaffian state).
   - A quantum critical point normally needs a control parameter tuned through a separatrix: a temperature-independent crossing, plus a collapse of R(δ, T) onto a scaling form in δ·T^(-1/zν). None is shown. The "critical" β_c = -1.6 is read off as the level where β' changes sign. In Fig. 6a, however, the curves for samples 4-7 sit at quite different β values, and samples 1-3 never reach -1.6 in the same temperature range. It is not explained how one β_c and one resistance are extracted from this set.
   - The resistance values are per-layer sheet resistances. They therefore depend on the flake thickness from tilted SEM, the assumed layer spacing, and the assumption that all layers conduct equally in parallel. No uncertainty is given; a 20% error in thickness would move both values well away from the (5/2) ratios.
   - The 16.5 kΩ value comes from the bias-current-tuned curves in Fig. 6b, where the "crossing" point is not clearly defined (the curves do not cross at one point on the plotted scale), and current tuning carries a heating problem (concern 5).
   What to do: either show a genuine scaling collapse with a defined control parameter (thickness, current, field or the dρ/dT parameter), with error bars on the critical resistances that include the thickness and layer-count uncertainty, or recast these as characteristic crossover resistances and drop the numerology linking them to 8/π and the ν = 5/2 Pfaffian state.

4. The phase identity and homogeneity of the measured flakes are not established. This matters because the low resistivity and the superconductivity are both far outside known Ca2RuO4 behaviour. Where: Methods (powder XRD shows about 5% CaRuO3 and about 6% S-Pbca Ca2RuO4; flakes are told apart from CaRuO3 "by the crystal shape" in SEM); Fig. 1a (nanofilm resistivity 3-4 orders of magnitude below bulk); the MBE c-axis of 11.592 Å, much shorter than either bulk phase. Why it matters: metallic CaRuO3 inclusions, Ca3Ru2O7 intergrowths, Ru-rich or oxygen-deficient surface layers, or substrate/interface conduction in the LaAlO3-grown films could each produce lower resistivity, a parallel channel that distorts the ln T analysis, and, in the superconducting samples, filamentary or proximity paths. "Disorder cannot explain this phenomenon" (p. 5) is stated, not shown. The lattice parameters for the nanoscale crystals come from bulk powder, not from the measured devices. What to do: characterize the measured devices themselves after transport, for example with Raman (which separates the S, L and CaRuO3 phases clearly), TEM/STEM with EDX, or micro-XRD. For the MBE films, rule out substrate or interface conduction and secondary phases. Then report the phase and thickness of each device used in Figs. 3-6.

B. Should address

5. Heating and current effects are not controlled. Ca2RuO4 is well known for current-induced states in which Joule heating has been a central controversy (refs. 28, 34, 35 cited by the authors). Where: Fig. 6b (sample 4, currents from 1 nA to 5000 nA at R ~ 10^4–10^5 Ω, i.e. up to about µW in a 10 nm flake at low temperature); Methods, which do not state the current used for the R(T) curves in Figs. 1-3. What to do: state the measurement current and power for every dataset. Show that R(T) in the Bose-glass window does not depend on current at the levels used. For Fig. 6b, estimate the sample temperature rise or compare with pulsed measurements before reading a current-tuned "SIT" as a quantum transition.

6. The superconductivity of samples 4 and 5 is shown only through R(T) dropping toward zero (Fig. 1c). Where: Fig. 1c, Fig. 4a "SC" region, and the description of "high-temperature superconductivity" with T_c^onset ~ 100 K (from ref. 29). Why it matters: the Bose-glass interpretation presupposes that these samples are 2D superconductors. Neither zero resistance on a linear scale with the noise floor, nor critical-field or critical-current behaviour, nor a Meissner response is shown for these devices. What to do: show the low-temperature R(T) on a linear scale with the noise floor, R(T) under magnetic field to show the onset suppressed, and V–I characteristics.

7. The supporting magnetization argument is weak. Where: p. 2. The diamagnetism cited (around 140 K) was measured on powders of nanoscale crystals, not on the devices, and its onset does not coincide with 220 K. The 220 K comparison is to bulk current-driven diamagnetism from a retracted paper (ref. 34). What to do: drop or substantially soften this paragraph, or present magnetization on samples that are shown to be equivalent to the transport devices.

8. The localization parameter dρ/dT is a resistivity difference that inherits the thickness uncertainty, and the claim that it is controlled by octahedral distortion is not measured on the samples. Where: Fig. 1b definition, Fig. 4a,b, p. 4-5 ("Reducing the distortion of RuO6 leads to a decrease in localization"). The axis is labelled |dρ/dT| in Ω cm K⁻¹ but is defined as ρ(120 K) − ρ(280 K) divided by 160 K. No structural data (tilt or rotation angles, or even the c-axis) are given for individual nanoflakes. The authors themselves note that XRD on the MBE films does not determine the octahedral geometry. What to do: give error bars on dρ/dT from the thickness uncertainty, and either measure a structural proxy (for example Raman phonon frequencies sensitive to tilt) on the devices, or present the distortion mechanism as a hypothesis.

9. Device count and reproducibility. The Bose-glass regime ending in superconductivity is seen in two devices (samples 4 and 5), and the bias-current SIT in one (sample 4). There is no information on thermal cycling, repeated cooldowns, or contact configurations, such as swapping current and voltage leads to rule out inhomogeneous current paths in small flakes. What to do: report how many devices were fabricated and how many showed each behaviour, and show reproducibility on at least one device on a second cooldown.

10. Fermi-glass identification for samples 1-3 also rests on the functional form only. Where: Fig. 2b. Weak localization and electron–electron interaction corrections both give σ ~ ln T in 2D. Room-temperature "weak localization" in a correlated oxide would usually be checked with magnetoresistance. The same MR measurement suggested in concern 1 would settle both regimes.

C. Minor

11. Sample 9 appears in Fig. 4a (T^SC-BG and T^BG-FG) but is not described in Fig. 1, Fig. 3, the text or the Methods. Sample 8 (300 nm) appears in Figs. 1 and 5 but not in Fig. 4. Please add the missing data and descriptions, or explain the omission.

12. The lattice parameters of the MBE films are given twice (p. 3 and Methods); the Methods paragraph compares them with the nanoscale "single crystal" values, which were measured on powder. Clarify which sample each lattice parameter refers to.

13. Fig. 3: the BG and FG ranges marked by arrows should match the numerical ranges in the text (for example, 101-220 K for sample 4). State the fit parameters (R0, ρ1, σ0, σ1) for each sample in a table.

14. Fig. 6a: the y-axis label and legend do not make clear whether β is plotted with the 2/p prefactor of Eq. (1). Mark β_c = -1.6 with its uncertainty.

15. Fig. 4b shows Bose-glass and superconducting regions for bulk data as a shaded blob at low temperature, without data points defining it. Clarify what the shading represents.

16. The phrase "the dρ/dT (the weaker the localization)" on p. 5 and similar sentences need editing, and "cooper pairs" should be capitalised consistently. The comparison with the 5/2 Pfaffian state is speculative and could be removed.

17. Methods: give the electrode geometry (Hall bar or linear four-probe), the channel dimensions used to convert R to ρ and to R per layer, and the thickness uncertainty from tilted SEM.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add magnetoresistance at fixed temperatures between 100 and 280 K on a Fermi-glass sample (1-3) and a Bose-glass sample (4 or 5). This is the most direct way to show whether the carriers in the 100-220 K window are paired.
2. Characterize the measured devices after transport with Raman and TEM/EDX, to confirm that they are single-phase Ca2RuO4 and to give a per-device structural proxy for the RuO6 distortion that can replace or support dρ/dT.
3. Put the critical-resistance claims on a quantitative footing, with propagated thickness and layer-count uncertainties and an attempt at a scaling collapse. Otherwise recast them as crossover scales.
4. Give fit residuals and window-sensitivity tests for the ln T and ln(1/T) fits, and explicitly model superconducting fluctuation conductivity above T_c^onset to show it does not account for the vertical flow.

5. Verdict: major revision
