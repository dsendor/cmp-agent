A. Must address

1. No measurement tests whether the carriers between about 100 and 220 K are Cooper pairs. The headline claim, "signatures of Cooper pairs persist up to high temperatures of ~220 K" (abstract, conclusion; p. 2, "Samples 4 and 5…"), is inferred only from R ∝ ln(1/T) in Fig. 3 (sample 4, 101–220 K; sample 5, 105–183 K; sample 7 in Fig. 3c). The paper has no perpendicular-field magnetoresistance, no field dependence of the BG–FG crossover temperature, no Nernst signal, no shot noise or tunnelling gap, no h/2e (Little–Parks) periodicity, nothing that tells charge 2e from charge e. Why it matters: an R = R0 − a ln T form is not unique to localized bosons. It also arises from Altshuler–Aronov electron–electron interaction corrections, Kondo or spin-disorder scattering off local Ru moments in a correlated oxide near magnetic order, granular-metal conduction, crossovers near the structural or orbital transitions Ca2RuO4 is known to have, a crossover toward VRH, a phonon contribution, and two parallel conduction channels (a surface, interface or minority-phase layer beside the bulk of the flake; see concern 3). None of these is discussed or excluded. What to do: measure magnetotransport across the 100–250 K window on samples 4 and 5 (and the MBE films), in perpendicular and parallel field. Fermionic weak localization gives negative magnetoresistance with an HLN form and a dephasing field set by e; field suppression of pairing should give positive magnetoresistance, a field scale consistent with 2e, and a shift or removal of the ln(1/T) regime. Comparing samples 1–3 (Fermi glass) with samples 4–5 (Bose glass) under the same fields would be a direct test, and it would also fix the dephasing exponent p (concern 11). If possible, add a further pair-sensitive probe on a device in the claimed Bose-glass regime (Nernst, noise, tunnelling, or local diamagnetism on the same devices). Failing these, discuss and exclude the alternatives explicitly and scope the claim to "Bose-glass-like transport phenomenology", one possible interpretation of a change in the curvature of R(T).

2. The ln(1/T) and ln T forms are not shown to be distinguishable over the fitted windows, and the β-function "vertical flow" is not independent evidence. Where: Fig. 3a,b; Eqs. (1)–(4); Figs. 5 and 6a; p. 5; the Fig. 5 caption ("an independent criterion ... beyond simple logarithmic fitting"). The windows span only Δln T ≈ 0.6–0.8 (a factor of about 2.2 in T; ln T from about 4.6 to 5.4). In sample 4, R changes from about 125 to 80 kΩ; in sample 5 by about 20%. To leading order the two forms are identical and differ only through second-order curvature, yet the lines in Fig. 3 are judged by eye, with no residuals, χ² or AIC, and no test of sensitivity to the window edges. The visible deviation of G from the ln T line in Fig. 3a sits at the low-T end, next to the resistance maximum near 80–100 K that precedes the superconducting drop, so superconducting fluctuations are an equally natural cause of the curvature: a 2D Aslamazov–Larkin correction, e²/(16ħ)·Tc/(T−Tc), is about 10⁻⁵ S at T ≈ 2Tc, the same order as G_□/layer in Fig. 3a, and the authors apply an AL analysis themselves in Supplementary Fig. S3. The β-function does not add an independent test. Eqs. (3) and (4) are derived by assuming σ = σ0 + σ1 ln T or ρ = ρ0 + ρ1 ln(1/T), and β′_EXP is a second logarithmic derivative of the same R(T), so the sign of β′ only restates the curvature of σ versus ln T. A vertical segment in β versus ln g arises generically wherever g is stationary while d ln g/d ln T changes, that is at any resistance maximum or plateau (near 100 K in Figs. 1c and 3a,b), and superconducting fluctuation conductivity above T_c^onset would produce the same excursion. What to do: report fit residuals and χ² (or AIC) for both forms in every sample called a Bose glass, and show how the crossover temperatures (220 K, 183 K) move when the window edges move. Fit fermionic WL plus AL/Maki–Thompson fluctuation models with Tc fixed from Fig. 1c, or show that the flow starts well above where fluctuations matter. Show that the vertical flow is not a consequence of the nearby resistance maximum, check its robustness to smoothing, and present the β-function as a re-expression of the R(T) curvature, removing the claim that it is independent of the logarithmic fits.

3. The phase identity and homogeneity of the measured devices are not established, although both the low resistivity and the superconductivity lie far outside known Ca2RuO4 behaviour. Where: Methods (powder XRD of the source crystals shows about 5% CaRuO3 and about 6% of a second phase, reported as S-Pbca Ca2RuO4 or Ca3Ru2O7; flakes are told apart from CaRuO3 only "by the crystal shape" in SEM); Fig. 1a (nanofilm resistivity 3–4 orders of magnitude below bulk; samples 4 and 5, the only superconducting ones, have ρ ≈ 10⁻³ Ω cm, closer to metallic ruthenates); the MBE films on LaAlO3 with c = 11.592 Å, much shorter than either bulk phase; lattice parameters for the nanoscale crystals taken from bulk powder, not from the devices. 4. The "two quantum critical points" and their critical resistances, R^{BG-FG} = 65.7 kΩ ≈ (5/2)h/e² and R^{SC-BG} = 16.5 kΩ ≈ (5/2)h/4e², are not supported by a critical-scaling analysis, are not reproducibly defined, and do not match the paper's own data. Where: Fig. 6, p. 6, abstract and Conclusion. (i) A quantum critical point needs a control parameter tuned through a T → 0 separatrix, with a temperature-independent crossing and a collapse of R(δ, T) onto a scaling form in δ·T^(−1/zν). None is shown; a sign change of β′ at 180–220 K is a finite-temperature crossover, and only Fig. 6b, for a single sample, approaches a separatrix. (ii) β_c = −1.6 is read off where β′ changes sign, but in Fig. 6a the curves for samples 4–7 change sign at visibly different β, and samples 1–3 never reach −1.6 in the same range; how one β_c is extracted is not explained. (iii) The conversion from β_c to a resistance is not stated. With g = (h/e²)σ and the Vollhardt–Wölfle form β ≈ −1/g, β_c = −1.6 gives about 41 kΩ, not 65.7 kΩ. (iv) At the BG–FG crossover marked in Fig. 3, R□/layer is about 80 kΩ for sample 4 (ln T ≈ 5.4) and about 32 kΩ for sample 5 (ln T ≈ 5.2); neither is 65.7 kΩ and they differ by a factor of 2.5, whereas a sample-independent critical resistance requires them to coincide. (v) The 16.5 kΩ value comes from the bias-current-tuned curves in Fig. 6b, where the curves do not cross at one point on the plotted scale (the 65.7 and 16.5 kΩ levels are drawn as dashed lines), and current tuning carries a heating problem (concern 7). (vi) Both values depend on the per-layer normalization (concern 5); no uncertainty is given, and a 20% thickness error would move them well away from the 5/2 ratios, which need the normalization known to well below 10% to be distinguished from 8/π. (vii) The links to 8/π (ref. 42) and to the ν = 5/2 Pfaffian state (ref. 43) have no stated mechanism. What to do: state the β_c → R conversion explicitly and show the derivation sample by sample for every sample with the crossover (4, 5, 6, 7, 9), marking each sample's β′ sign change. Either demonstrate a genuine scaling collapse with a defined control parameter (thickness, current, field or dρ/dT) and a common critical value with error bars that include thickness, layer-count and fitting uncertainty, or recast these as characteristic crossover resistances and remove the 8/π and Pfaffian comparisons (or label them clearly as speculative).

6. The weak-localization framework is applied far outside its regime of validity. Bosonic and fermionic weak-localization logarithms are perturbative corrections valid for g ≫ 1, that is R_□ ≪ h/4e² = 6.45 kΩ for pairs. In Fig. 3a, sample 4 has R_□/layer = 60–125 kΩ, about 10–20 times h/4e² and above h/e²; sample 5 (28–40 kΩ) is also above h/e². These values lie in the strongly localized regime for both carrier types, so attributing a ln(1/T) dependence there to "weak localization of bosons" is internally inconsistent. The inelastic length is also not compared with the film thickness, which decides whether the 2D description holds. What to do: estimate L_φ relative to the thickness, explain how a weak-localization description is justified at these g values, or reframe the regime.
