A. Must address

1. No measurement in the paper tests for Cooper pairs at 180–220 K, and the transport signatures offered as evidence are not specific to bosons. The claims in the abstract and conclusion are that "bosons—Cooper pairs—persist up to high temperatures around 220 K", that a Bose glass separates the SC and Mott/Fermi-glass phases, and that vortices are "found" to be dual to localized pairs. They rest only on the sign of β′ (Eqs. 2–4, Fig. 6(a)) and on the "vertical flow" of the β-function (Fig. 5(b), Fig. 6(a)). Both are curvature information from the same ρ(T). The sign of β′ is the sign of the curvature of ln g against ln T, so any ρ(T) that approaches a maximum, here the maximum near 90–110 K before the superconducting drop (Fig. 1(c)), has β′ < 0 on its high-temperature side whatever the mechanism. Likewise, β = −(2/p) d ln g/d ln T goes through zero and changes sign at nearly constant g wherever R(T) has a maximum, so every sample with a superconducting downturn (4, 5) or a resistance peak will show β rising "perpendicular" to the Vollhardt–Wölfle curve. Superconducting fluctuations of an ordinary fermionic normal state, a magnetic or structural anomaly, or a parallel metallic path would all produce the same signatures. Pairing-sensitive measurements are needed on samples 4–7, with a non-superconducting Mott-like flake as a control: magnetoresistance R(T, B) at several temperatures in the claimed FG, BG and SC regimes, up to several tesla in perpendicular and parallel field, comparing positive pair-breaking MR against the weak-localization or weak-antilocalization magnetoconductance of fermions; the field dependence of the upturn and of the temperature at which the ln(1/T) regime starts; I–V characteristics in the BG window; paraconductivity analysis near T_c; and, if possible, diamagnetism or Nernst signal on the same flakes. The authors should also explain what in the β-function would distinguish their scenario from a resistance maximum caused by superconducting fluctuations of a fermionic normal state. Without these, the 220 K pair-formation claim should be restated as "ρ(T) consistent with the ln(1/T) form proposed for weakly localized bosons", not as evidence of preformed pairs.

2. The ρ ~ ln(1/T) versus σ ~ ln T discrimination is made by eye over windows too narrow to separate the two forms, and the weak-localization regime it presupposes is not shown to exist at these temperatures. The "Bose glass" window is 101–220 K for sample 4 (ln T ≈ 4.6–5.4, Fig. 3(a), R□/layer changing by about 50%) and 105–183 K for sample 5 (ln T ≈ 4.65–5.2, Fig. 3(b), R changing from ~32 to ~40 kΩ, about 25%). Each is less than a factor of two in temperature. The "Fermi glass" window is 220–290 K, a factor of only ~1.3 (ln T 5.4–5.67, Figs. 2(b), 3(a,b)). Over such ranges ρ = ρ0 + ρ1 ln(1/T) and σ = σ0 + σ1 ln T agree to first order and differ only in curvature, which is sensitive to the window, to phonon or other background terms and to any parallel channel. Figs. 3(a,b) show visual fits with no residuals, no χ² comparison, no fitted coefficients and no uncertainty on the boundary temperatures (220 K, 183 K, the dashed lines in Fig. 3), which appear chosen by eye. The Das–Doniach picture (Ref. [13]) and the β-function framework are low-temperature quantum-interference theories. At 100–300 K, in an oxide with ρ ~ 10⁻³–10⁻² Ω cm, the phase-coherence length should be at most a few lattice spacings; the paper gives no estimate of the dephasing length, the diffusion constant D or k_F l, and does not discuss whether the RuO2 layers are effectively decoupled, as a per-layer 2D description requires. Several mundane mechanisms give a smooth upturn with slight concave curvature on a log-T axis: the ferromagnetic transition near 180 K that Ref. [20] reports for these flakes, which lies inside the claimed BG windows; spin-disorder scattering across that transition; a gradual or partial L/S-like structural transition; phonon or polaron hopping; electron–electron interaction corrections (which also give σ ~ ln T); and a two-channel model of activated plus metallic parallel conduction. The authors should report the fitted ln T coefficients per layer and compare them with the expected prefactors (~e²/πh per conducting sheet for fermions and the analogous scale for bosons), stating the number of layers assumed for each flake (for samples 1–3 Fig. 2(b) gives σ in S/cm, so this comparison cannot be made as presented); give residuals, reduced χ² or information criteria for both forms in every sample, and show how the inferred crossover temperatures move when the window edges move; fit the competing magnetic, structural and two-channel scenarios; show whether the BG–FG temperatures correlate with the magnetic transition in the same samples; check for hysteresis on cooling and warming, which would point to a structural or magnetic origin; and extend the ln T behaviour over a wider range of temperature or of a tuning parameter.

3. The two "quantum critical sheet resistances", a headline result, are not determined as critical points, their values are internally inconsistent, and the 5/2 identification is unsupported. (i) R_BG–FG = 65.7 kΩ is obtained from β_c = −1.6, a dashed line drawn in Fig. 6(a) between the low-temperature plateaus of samples 1–3 and those of samples 4–7, not a fixed point found by crossing or scaling. The text says it is "estimated from g_c = −1.6" (p. 8), but g is a dimensionless conductance and cannot be negative. The route from β_c to a resistance is not given and cannot be reproduced; on the VW curve β = −1.6 corresponds to ln g ≈ −1.5 to −2, i.e. R□/layer of order 20–30 kΩ, not 65.7 kΩ. The value also depends on p, on the layer thickness used for "per layer" and on the VW curve. (ii) R_SC–BG is read from a family of R(T) curves at bias currents up to 5 µA on one sample (Fig. 6(b)). Bias current is not a quantum tuning parameter; the 65.7 kΩ dashed line is simply the low-T level of the 5000 nA curve and the 16.5 kΩ marker sits at no evident crossing, so there is no temperature-independent separatrix or scaling collapse. Joule heating dominates current-driven phenomena in Ca2RuO4 (Refs. [19, 20, 27, 28]), and no estimate of dissipated power, electron temperature, dV/dI against T or pulsed-bias check is shown. (iii) For the same sample 4, Fig. 3(a) gives a R□/layer maximum of ≈125 kΩ (consistent with ρ ≈ 7×10⁻³ Ω cm at 10 nm in Fig. 1 and ~16 layers of 0.6 nm), whereas Fig. 6(b) at the lowest bias gives ≈40 kΩ (right axis R ≈ 4 kΩ), a factor of ~3 lower and closer to sample 5's values. Either the normalization or the sample differs, and both critical values depend on it. (iv) No uncertainties are given, yet R□/layer depends on SEM-tilt thicknesses, lateral geometry and an assumed number of layers; a 10–30% geometric error moves the values by more than the difference between 5/2 and 2 or 3. 65.7 and 16.8 kΩ differ from (5/2)h/e² = 64.5 kΩ and (5/2)h/4e² = 16.1 kΩ by 2–4%, so the 5/2 identification and the links to 8/π (Ref. [34]) and the Moore–Read Pfaffian (Ref. [35]) are numerology. The authors should explain the β_c-to-R conversion, define R□/layer and propagate geometric errors, resolve the Fig. 3(a)/6(b) discrepancy, and give error budgets. They should either show a genuine quantum-critical analysis (isotherm crossing and finite-size or dynamical scaling with a tuning parameter such as field or gate, with heating ruled out) or present these as phenomenological crossover resistances and drop the 5/2, 8/π and Pfaffian comparisons.

4. The central interpretation, that thinning relieves the RuO6 distortion and so tunes correlation "rather than impurities", has no structural or compositional measurement on the flakes that were measured (pp. 4, 6, 9; Abstract; Conclusion). Flakes are separated from CaRuO3 "by observing the crystal shape" in SEM (Methods). The quoted c = 12.278 Å for "nanoscale single crystals" is not attributed to a measurement on flakes and appears to come from powder XRD (Miniflex) of the parent crystals before sonication; it is also close to the bulk metallic L-phase value. No Raman, TEM/SAED, micro-XRD, EDX or EELS is reported, and no octahedral distortion is measured on any nanoflake. The flake resistivities (~10⁻³–10⁻² Ω cm, Fig. 1(a)), 3–4 orders below bulk, are the observation on which everything else rests, and they are equally consistent with CaRuO3 inclusions, Ru or RuO2 from sonication damage, Sr- or Ru-rich inclusions, a conducting surface or interface layer, oxygen-excess Ca2RuO4+δ, or current paths along grain boundaries. A percolating metallic path would also naturally produce the resistance maxima, shifted "transition" temperatures and the non-zero-resistance "SIT" of samples 6 and 7. The statement that "impurity-induced disorder in thin films cannot explain this phenomenon" (p. 6) is asserted, not tested. The authors should provide structural (Raman of the octahedral and L/S-sensitive modes, TEM/SAED or micro-XRD) and compositional (EDX/EELS for Ca:Ru and oxygen) characterization of the measured devices, ideally the same flakes after transport or else flakes from the same batch at the same thickness, and discuss explicitly how CaRuO3 or Ru inclusions and off-stoichiometry are excluded. Otherwise the distortion-relief and "not disorder" conclusions should be removed or softened.

6. The β-function construction and the "single universal curve" of Fig. 5(a) have little discriminating power. L² = DT^−p with p = 1 is fixed without justification, and β scales as 1/p. g is formed per RuO2 layer for flakes 10–300 nm thick and for bulk crystals, which assumes fully decoupled 2D layers; it is not stated whether g = (ħ/e²)σ uses a per-layer sheet conductance or a 3D conductivity, nor how the bulk data of Ref. [26] were converted. For any hopping law ln g ≈ −(T0/T)^α, β is automatically linear in ln g (for 2D VRH, β_EXP = (2/3p) ln g), so the collapse of the strongly localized data onto β_VW, including bulk crystals at ln g ~ −20, is largely built in, and the slope match depends on p. The authors should state exactly how g is constructed for each sample, show how the collapse and β_c change with p (e.g. p = 1, 2, 3) and with the layer normalization, state which features of Fig. 5 are independent of p, and not present Fig. 5(a) as evidence that weak-localization scaling applies to Ca2RuO4.

8. The data do not support thickness as the tuning parameter named in the Fig. 1 title and the abstract. Sample 4 (10 nm) and sample 5 (30 nm) superconduct, while samples 1–3 (25, 25 and 35 nm) are Mott-like and sample 8 (300 nm) is insulating. The ground state is therefore not set by thickness, and the authors instead order the samples by |dρ/dT|, which is itself the observed transport. The authors should retitle Fig. 1, show the ground state and resistivity against measured thickness for all flakes with the uncertainty of the 70°-tilt SEM method, and address the non-monotonic thickness dependence directly or withdraw the claim that thickness is the control parameter.

9. The MBE films contradict the paper's own structural argument and have untested alternative explanations. Samples 6 and 7 have the shorter c axis (11.592 Å), which the authors read as stronger distortion, which their argument links to a Mott transition near 220 K (p. 6); yet they are placed in the Bose glass region of Fig. 4(a) below 67–81 K without reaching zero resistance. Epitaxial strain on LaAlO3 (Ref. [30]) also alters the octahedra, so the c axis alone is not a measure of distortion. Their ρ ~ ln(1/T) regime (Fig. 3(c), Fig. S1) extends over roughly 4–67 K, more than a decade with a factor of ~3 change in ρ, which is a more discriminating range than the flake data; but at these temperatures Kondo scattering and electron–electron interaction corrections are standard sources of logarithmic behaviour and should be addressed, for example with magnetoresistance and field-dependent slopes. The paper should also report whether the films are phase-pure and how their thickness and strain state were determined.
