Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors use phase-referenced IQ detection with an inductive microstrip antenna to record, shot by shot, the complex precession signal of a parametrically pumped, out-of-plane magnetized YIG film. They report that the k ≈ 0 condensate signal becomes coherent within a single shot after thermalization, while its absolute phase is uniformly distributed over 1,000 repetitions. They read this as direct evidence of spontaneous phase selection (U(1) symmetry breaking) in a magnon BEC. Single-shot access to the condensate phase is a real experimental advance over Brillouin light scattering, which averages over shots. The earlier evidence for spontaneous coherence (for example ref. 36) was indirect, so the question is a good one. How strong the result is depends on whether a uniform phase histogram really proves spontaneous phase selection at condensation, and on how well the coherence measure is calibrated.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- A clean, phase-locked, all-electrical single-shot method that records both amplitude and phase of the condensate. This is a technique others can adopt and that suits later Josephson and supercurrent experiments.
- The single-shot traces (Fig. 2) show a clear qualitative change: a noise-like phase during pumping, then a linear phase ramp once the condensate forms.
- The coherence rises with a clear threshold in pump power (Fig. 4, about 21 dBm). Its time course is consistent with the spectrogram (Fig. 3a; Extended Data Fig. 2), and two independent detection chains (spectrum analyser and IQ mixer) agree.
- The data are openly available (Zenodo), and the analysis window and normalization are stated in the Methods.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The uniform phase histogram does not yet show that the condensate phase is chosen spontaneously at its formation. There is no positive control, and the phase stability of the set-up is asserted, not shown (Fig. 2a insets; main text p. 1433; Extended Data Fig. 1 caption).
   - Why the histogram alone is not enough. The central claim rests on the phase histograms at t = 1.25 µs being uniform. A uniform histogram is what one expects if the phase is chosen spontaneously. It is also what one expects if the phase is set reproducibly early on and then scrambled by small shot-to-shot differences in the later dynamics.
   - Those differences are large enough here. The condensate frequency drifts from about 3.45 GHz to about 3.32 GHz during formation (Fig. 3a), and this drift comes from the density-dependent nonlinear shift. A 1% shot-to-shot spread in magnon density would therefore change the frequency by roughly 1 MHz. Over the roughly 0.3 µs before the 1.25 µs slice, that adds a phase spread of order 2 rad. The same effect follows from jitter in the condensation onset time combined with the roughly 100 MHz intermediate frequency (a 1.6 ns jitter gives about 1 rad). So a uniform histogram at a fixed laboratory time cannot tell spontaneous phase selection apart from a deterministic but jittery evolution.
   - The phase stability is not shown. The Extended Data Fig. 1 caption says the oscilloscope was not locked to the shared 10 MHz reference "due to technical difficulties" and that phase stability "was confirmed in preliminary measurements". Those measurements are not shown.
   - The pump-phase measurement is unexplained. Fig. 2 shows a pump-phase distribution (grey sectors), but the 7.8 GHz pump is removed by the diplexer and the low-pass filters before the IQ mixer. The paper does not say how that distribution was measured.
   - What the authors should do:
     (i) Add a positive control under the same acquisition: a weak phase-locked tone at about 3.3 GHz, or a directly excited (non-parametric) linear mode at the condensate frequency. Show that its phase histogram over 1,000 shots is sharply peaked. Ideally the control signal should be chirped like the condensate signal.
     (ii) Add a seeding experiment: inject a weak coherent signal at the bottom of the spectrum during thermalization and show that the condensate phase then locks to it. This would show that the method can detect a deterministic phase when one is present.
     (iii) Remove the dynamical phase before building the histograms. Either reference the phase to the moment coherence first appears in each shot, or remove ∫ω(t)dt using the instantaneous frequency of each shot. Then show histograms at the onset of coherence (about 0.8–0.9 µs).
     (iv) Quantify uniformity with a test statistic, such as the Rayleigh test or the mean resultant length with confidence intervals, rather than by eye.
     (v) Explain how the pump phase was measured.

B. Should address

2. Calling this a BEC, and calling the random phase BEC-specific, needs more support (Abstract; pp. 1431–1432; Discussion).
   - The paper identifies the coherent k ≈ 0 state as a thermodynamic BEC. The evidence is the spectrogram and a power threshold. The magnon population across k is not shown.
   - The paper itself names kinetic instability as a redistribution channel. Kinetic instability can directly produce coherent bottom magnons without the gas first thermalizing.
   - Condensation in perpendicularly magnetized YIG films is contested (ref. 32).
   - More generally, a random phase from shot to shot is expected for any instability that grows from noise, whether a laser above threshold or a classical self-oscillator. It is necessary for a BEC but not specific to one. The statement that the work confirms "a central prediction of Bose–Einstein condensation theory" therefore goes further than the data.
   - What the authors should do: add evidence of thermalization, such as Brillouin light scattering spectra in the same geometry or results from prior work in the same film, or a kinetic or stochastic Landau–Lifshitz simulation reproducing the onset time and its power dependence. Alternatively, reword the claims as emergence of spontaneous coherence in a noise-seeded, magnon-condensate-like state, and discuss the kinetic-instability alternative explicitly.

3. The coherence value is not calibrated, and its dependence on signal-to-noise ratio and on detection artifacts is not quantified (Methods "Data analysis"; Figs. 3b and 4a; Extended Data Fig. 2c).
   - The metric is the integral of a normalized single-shot autocorrelation over 100 ns. It measures spectral narrowness within one shot. It should therefore fall as the signal-to-noise ratio drops, yet the Fig. 4 caption says coherence "does not depend on the absolute amplitude".
   - Fig. 4a contradicts that statement in part: the peak coherence at 22 dBm is about 0.45, against about 0.77 at 23 and 25 dBm.
   - The noise baseline is about 0.18, and the plateau sits at about 0.75 rather than 1. Neither is explained.
   - Extended Data Fig. 2c shows a constant IQ artifact tone at 3.77 GHz and a "shadow" between 3.2 and 3.3 GHz. The condensate lies at about 3.32 GHz, right next to that shadow. A constant tone is itself coherent and could raise the baseline or bias the coherence value.
   - What the authors should do:
     (i) Calibrate the metric with synthetic or measured signals of known linewidth, chirp and signal-to-noise ratio, passed through the same chain.
     (ii) Show the coherence value with the artifacts filtered out or subtracted.
     (iii) Explain the 0.18 floor and the 0.75 plateau.
     (iv) Report how the result changes with the window length, for example 50, 100 and 200 ns.

4. The claim that the condensate "does not undergo dephasing" and keeps a "clear remanence in coherence" at 3.5 µs is not supported by the analysis used (Fig. 4; pp. 1434–1435).
   - A 100 ns autocorrelation window cannot detect dephasing on longer timescales.
   - At 3.5 µs, Fig. 4a shows a value of about 0.2–0.25 against a noise floor of about 0.18, with overlapping standard-deviation bands at some powers.
   - The value of a coherence measure computed from the signal itself, at a point where the amplitude is below the noise floor, needs justification.
   - What the authors should do: give a single-shot phase-coherence time, for example the first-order correlation g1(τ) over microsecond delays or the linewidth from long windows, and compare it with the amplitude decay time. Test the 3.5 µs remanence statistically against signal-free traces processed identically.

5. The absence of a coherent response at f_p/2 is used to rule out pump-imposed coherence, but the sensitivity at 3.9 GHz is not shown (p. 1432; Fig. 3a; Extended Data Fig. 1).
   - 3.9 GHz lies at the edge of the diplexer's "< 4 GHz" port and of the F2 filter band.
   - The paper itself estimates that the antenna suppresses magnons with 18 µm wavelength by a factor of about 18.
   - What the authors should do: state the detection sensitivity at 3.9 GHz, for example with a calibration tone, and give an upper bound on any coherent f_p/2 component.

6. All data come from one film, one field (281 mT) and one pump frequency, and some operating parameters are missing (Methods).
   - The pulse repetition rate is not given. That rate determines whether the 1,000 shots are independent: residual magnons or heating from earlier pulses could seed or bias later shots.
   - Possible sample heating at 25 dBm is not discussed.
   - The reference plane for the quoted pump power (generator, amplifier output or antenna) is not stated.
   - What the authors should do: give the repetition rate and the power at the antenna, and show that the key result holds at a second field or pump frequency, or in a second sample or antenna, or explain why it was not done.

C. Minor

7. The oscillations in the coherence plateau (Figs. 3b and 4) are attributed, speculatively, to magnons reflected from the sample edge. Estimating the round-trip time from the group velocity and the 2.7 or 9.5 mm lateral dimensions, and comparing it with the oscillation period, would test this cheaply.

8. The Discussion says that "no direct proof of randomness in the established condensate phase has yet been provided". Please state more precisely how this work goes beyond ref. 36 (spontaneous coherence in magnon BEC) and ref. 11.

9. Fig. 2:
   - The time-axis tick labels are rounded to the point of duplication (0.10, 0.10, 0.11, 0.12, 0.12, ...). Use more digits or relative time.
   - The polar histograms have no radial scale and no bin width.
   - State how the three "randomly selected" traces were chosen.

10. Typos and wording:
   - "standard derivation" should read "standard deviation" (Fig. 4 and Extended Data Fig. 2 captions).
   - "Suppementary" and "oscilllator" (p. 1433).
   - In the Methods, the "bar denotes vector normalization" notation should be defined explicitly (normalized over the window or over the full trace?).

11. Methods: give the oscilloscope sampling rate, the trigger source and the trigger jitter. These set how precisely the absolute phase can be measured, which matters given concern 1.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Add a phase-seeding experiment, with a weak coherent tone at the band bottom during thermalization. A locked phase with the seed and a random phase without it would be the most convincing demonstration that the phase is otherwise selected spontaneously.
- Add a simple stochastic model (for example a Gross–Pitaevskii or kinetic simulation with noise seeding) that reproduces the onset, the coherence value and the phase statistics, including how chirp and density fluctuations scramble the phase.
- Report histograms of phase differences between two times within the same shot alongside the absolute-phase histograms. This separates single-shot phase rigidity from randomness across shots.
- Show the power dependence of the condensation onset time and of the phase uniformity near threshold (21–22 dBm), where critical behaviour would be most informative.

5. Verdict

Verdict: major revision
