A. Must address

1. The phase stability of the detection chain is asserted, not shown, and there is no positive control. The central claim (Fig. 2a insets; Sec. 1.2, p. 5–6; Discussion) is that the uniform phase histogram over 1000 shots "must be an intrinsic property of the BEC" because "all externally induced factors are stable within a few degrees of phase shift" and the pump starts "with a constant phase-offset" (p. 5). But the caption of Extended Data Fig. 5 says the oscilloscope "was excluded [from the shared 10 MHz reference] due to technical difficulties, but phase stability of the setup as a whole was confirmed in preliminary measurements." Those measurements are not shown. With a 3.2 GHz LO and the condensate at about 3.33–3.4 GHz, the IF is roughly 100–200 MHz, so the phase read at a fixed oscilloscope sample depends on trigger timing: 1 ns of trigger or timebase jitter between the pulse generator and the free-running oscilloscope shifts the phase by about 0.8 rad, and a few nanoseconds would produce exactly the uniform histograms of Fig. 2a. The authors should show a positive control taken with the same antenna, filters, amplifier, mixer, oscilloscope, triggering and analysis that gives a sharply clustered histogram. Possible signals: (a) a CW or pulsed tone at about 3.3 GHz from a generator (SG1/SG2) on the shared 10 MHz reference, injected into the detection path; (b) the linear, sub-threshold FMR response of the same film at the band bottom, driven by a weak phase-locked pulse at the BEC frequency; (c) the leaked pump or a pump harmonic recorded through the same IQ path. For each, they should quote the circular standard deviation of the phase over 1000 shots at the same delay (t ≈ 1.25 µs). The trigger and sampling scheme, the IF and the trigger jitter of the oscilloscope relative to the pulse generator should be stated in Methods, not only in a figure caption. Without this control the uniform histograms cannot be attributed to the magnon system.

2. A uniform phase at a fixed laboratory time does not establish a random initial phase. The Fig. 2a histograms are taken at fixed times (0.10, 0.90, 1.25 µs), so each value includes the phase accumulated since pump onset, φ(t) = φ0 + 2π∫(f_BEC(t′) − f_LO)dt′. Fig. 3a shows the condensate frequency chirping by about 100 MHz, from about 3.42 GHz during pumping to about 3.33 GHz by 1.2–1.3 µs, which the paper attributes to the density-dependent nonlinear shift; the chirp will therefore vary from shot to shot with the injected magnon number. A shot-to-shot spread of only about 1 MHz over about 1 µs (or about 3 MHz over 0.3 µs) gives a phase spread of 2π, so a condensate whose initial phase is fixed by the pump would also give a uniform histogram at t = 1.25 µs. The "Measurement 1–3" traces in Fig. 2b indeed show different slopes before about 1.25 µs. The authors should separate the initial phase from the accumulated phase: (i) extract each shot's instantaneous frequency, subtract its integrated phase, and histogram the residual phase at the onset of coherence, or histogram the phase at the time each shot's coherence first crosses a set level (about 0.6–0.8 µs), or report the phase difference between closely spaced times within a shot; (ii) report the shot-to-shot spread of the instantaneous frequency, amplitude and condensation onset time, show that the spread of ∫f dt is or is not small compared with 2π, and test whether the phase correlates with these quantities (for example by plotting phase against instantaneous frequency for all 1000 shots); (iii) replace visual inspection of the rose plots with a circular statistic, such as a Rayleigh test or the mean resultant length with its expected value for N = 1000, next to the corresponding control value; (iv) as a further positive control, show that the directly pumped magnons at ωp/2 display the expected two-fold (φ, φ + π) phase locking to the pump in the same setup, and that this structure is absent for the condensate.

3. Neither signature used, a random phase across shots and a high single-shot coherence value, distinguishes a coherent condensate from a strongly overpopulated but incoherent occupation of the lowest mode. The k ≈ 0 mode is populated by four-magnon scattering out of the parametric pairs, so its phase is not fixed by the pump whether or not a condensate forms; the Fig. 2a histograms at 0.10 and 0.90 µs are uniform as well. The coherence value (Sec. 1.3, Sec. 3.2, Fig. 3c) integrates |autocorrelation| over a 100 ns window, which resolves only about 10 MHz, so any mode narrower than a few MHz scores high, including spectrally narrowed thermal-like or amplified-noise (Gaussian, chaotic) occupation, and the YIG band-bottom mode is intrinsically narrow. The title claim, "emergence of phase coherence", and the abstract's "These findings confirm all postulated fundamental properties of quasiparticle condensates" rest on this. The authors already have the data to discriminate: a chaotic single-mode field gives a Rayleigh-distributed |I + iQ| with g⁽²⁾(0) = 2, while a coherent condensate gives a narrow distribution about a finite mean with g⁽²⁾(0) → 1. They should show histograms of A(t) = |I + iQ| at several delays and the derived g⁽²⁾(0) = ⟨A⁴⟩/⟨A²⟩² as a function of time and pump power, corrected for the amplifier noise floor, and state what in their data separates the condensate from narrowband incoherent occupation.

B. Should address

4. The coherence value is not calibrated, its baseline contradicts its definition, and the claims built on its decay are not supported. The text says "0 denotes perfect white noise and 1 denotes a perfectly coherent signal" (p. 6, Fig. 3 caption), but the no-signal baseline in Figs. 3b and 4a is about 0.17–0.18 and the maximum stays at about 0.75–0.8 even at the highest amplitudes. The value mixes SNR, the amplitude envelope and linewidth within the window, and frequency chirp; the paper itself attributes the dip near 1 µs to chirp and the late decrease to SNR, without showing the latter. It is not stated how the signal is normalized (per window or not), over which lag range |autocorrelation| is integrated, or how ω0 of the reference tone is chosen. The metric underpins the threshold, the statement that coherence "does not depend on the absolute amplitude", "the BEC does not undergo dephasing" (p. 7–8) and the "clear remanence in coherence" at 3.5 µs (Fig. 4 caption). In Fig. 4a the value falls steadily from about 0.75 at 1.8 µs to about 0.2–0.25 at 3.5 µs, an excess of about 0.04 over the noise floor with no stated significance; and phase stability over the condensate lifetime is a statement about long-delay temporal coherence that a 100 ns sliding window cannot test. The authors should calibrate the metric with synthetic and injected signals (a pure tone plus the measured detector noise swept in SNR, a chirped tone, Lorentzian lines of varying width, and Gaussian noise filtered to the measured BEC linewidth), subtract or explain the baseline, and use the calibration to convert the value into a linewidth or coherence time and to show whether the late-time decrease is fully explained by SNR. They should state the significance of the 3.5 µs excess. To test "no dephasing" directly, they should compute per shot the phase difference φ(t2) − φ(t1) after removing the mean frequency evolution, for delays up to about 1.5 µs, and report its variance over shots against delay as a phase-diffusion time, or report g1(τ) out to τ > 100 ns or the per-shot spectral width.

5. The identification of the coherent band-bottom state as a BEC formed through thermalization is not argued in this paper. A noise-seeded random phase is necessary for a condensate but not unique to it: kinetic instability (secondary magnons created in pairs, with only their summed phase fixed), four-magnon parametric processes feeding the k = 0 mode, a parametric oscillator above threshold, lasing-like accumulation in one mode, or classical self-oscillation of a nonlinear uniform-precession mode would all give it. The evidence here is limited to spectrograms showing accumulation at the band bottom (Fig. 3a, Extended Data Fig. 6a) and a reference to earlier work [41]; there is no frequency-resolved population distribution, no estimate of the chemical potential reaching the band bottom, and no estimate of the magnon density relative to the critical density at the 21 dBm threshold. The paper cites the comment disputing BEC in perpendicularly magnetized YIG (Ref. [31]) but does not address it. The authors should discuss these alternatives explicitly, engage with Ref. [31], explain why the coherent signal that appears during pumping (0.6–1.0 µs, at about 3.42 GHz) is a condensate and not a kinetic-instability product, and present evidence for thermalization (for example the populated band shape before condensation, or the delay of the band-bottom signal after pump onset and its power dependence). Otherwise they should scope the title, the abstract ("confirm all postulated fundamental properties") and the Discussion ("proof of the formation of a true BEC") to the spontaneous phase of the band-bottom coherent state.

6. The condensation threshold is not separated from the parametric-instability threshold. Fig. 4 places the condensation threshold at 21 dBm, but the parametric threshold of the 7.8 GHz pump in this geometry is not reported. If 20 dBm is below the parametric threshold, the coherence onset may simply track the onset of parametric injection, not a critical density for condensation. The authors should report the parametric threshold, for example from the reflected pump power on the microwave detector in Extended Data Fig. 5 or from the 3.9 GHz parametric-magnon line, show the full spectrum-analyser spectra at 20, 21 and 22 dBm, and give coherence and phase statistics at finer power steps between the two thresholds, plotted against injected magnon density or peak amplitude.

7. The novelty relative to earlier work is not stated precisely, and several statements overreach. Demidov et al., PRL 100, 047205 (2008) [Ref. 35], titled "Observation of spontaneous coherence in Bose–Einstein condensate of magnons", already reported spontaneous coherence of a magnon BEC from its microwave emission, including its independence from the pump; Refs. [10] and [24] treat the onset of phase coherence in condensates, and Refs. [31, 41] discuss out-of-plane condensation. Against this, the manuscript says "it has been impossible to observe its spontaneous emergence" (p. 2), "no direct proof of randomness in the established condensate phase has yet been provided" and "For the first time, we are able to directly measure the spontaneous emergence of coherence" (p. 8), and the abstract says the findings "confirm all postulated fundamental properties of quasiparticle condensates". The authors should compare their result explicitly with Refs. [10] and [35], say what earlier experiments could not establish (for example the phase relative to the pump) and what the single-shot phase-resolved measurement adds, and moderate these claims.

8. IQ artifacts and calibration are not characterized with respect to the phase and coherence analysis. Extended Data Fig. 6c shows a constant band at 3.77 GHz and a "shadow" between 3.2 and 3.3 GHz, adjacent to the 3.33 GHz condensate line and where its image could fall, both attributed to the IQ technique. A constant, phase-stable spur or LO feedthrough in the analysis band adds a fixed-phase component and could raise the baseline coherence value; IQ gain and phase imbalance and DC offsets distort φ(t), produce image components and can bias the angular histogram toward particular angles. The authors should identify these components, describe how IQ imbalance and offsets were measured and corrected, state whether the artifacts are removed before the phase and coherence analysis, and show that the results are unchanged when they are. Because the detection band is filtered below 4 GHz and limited to 500 MHz, they should also state which frequency range enters the 100 ns window analysis at each time, since during pumping the signal near 3.4 GHz may contain contributions from the non-condensed gas.
