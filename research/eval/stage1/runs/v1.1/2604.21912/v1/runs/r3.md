Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper introduces "cryogenic shock exfoliation", in which graphite is exfoliated onto a hot (110 °C) substrate that is then plunged into liquid nitrogen. Combined with a low-pressure PC/PDMS micro-cavity transfer, the method is reported to raise the rhombohedral area fraction of exfoliated flakes from ~12% to ~32%, to produce single-stacking domains beyond 1000–1500 µm², and to reach ~90% transfer yield. A 13-layer, 10×10 µm² device is then characterized by nSOT magnetometry, transverse magnetic focusing (TMF), current-flow imaging and transport, including a claimed Poiseuille-to-porous hydrodynamic crossover. Supply of rhombohedral material is a real bottleneck for a very active field, so a simple, reproducible route to large domains would be widely used. The device physics shown here mostly demonstrates the method rather than standing as an independent discovery.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- A simple, low-cost and plausibly general procedure that tackles a practical bottleneck. The statistics are gathered on equal substrate areas (Fig. 1g,h; Fig. S2), and the exclusion criteria are stated explicitly.
- Transfer-yield statistics come from 24–62 transfers by seven co-authors (Fig. S4b), and the low-pressure transfer is described in reproducible detail.
- Several independent real-space and spectroscopic probes are applied to the same device: sMIM, IR imaging, capacitance phase diagram, nSOT magnetization imaging and nSOT current imaging. The uniform magnetization map over the full 10×10 µm² (Fig. 2c) is a convincing demonstration of uniformity at the micron scale.
- TMF with up to six resolved peaks on all four edges (Fig. 3c) and negative bend resistance in the square (Fig. S6b,c) directly show ballistic transport over the device scale.
- Current-flow images in three distinct regimes (Fig. 4a,c,e) with matched kinetic simulations make an attractive showcase of what large, clean RMG enables.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The headline "disorder mean free path exceeding 200 µm" is not established by the TMF analysis as constructed. The claim appears in the abstract, the introduction, p. 5, the Methods (Eqs. 4–6) and Fig. 3j (labelled i in the caption). ℓ_mfp(T) is obtained from ℓ_mfp = −(πd/2)/ln[A(T)/A_0], where A_0 is the low-temperature limit of the peak area (Methods; Fig. S7b is normalized "to the average of the four lowest temperature data points"). Normalizing to the low-temperature amplitude sets the low-temperature mean free path to infinity by construction. Any finite disorder scattering at base temperature is absorbed into A_0 and cannot appear in ℓ_mfp(T). The Methods concede that the analysis "is not well suited to determine the mean-free path at lowest temperatures" and that "we can only note that ℓ_0 ≥ 200 µm below 30 K". However, that bound itself comes from the same normalized ratio. For d ≈ 5 µm, the trajectory length πd/2 ≈ 8 µm gives exp(−πd/2ℓ) = 0.96 for ℓ = 200 µm and 0.85 for ℓ = 50 µm. Separating these requires an absolute, not relative, determination of W(E_F, T→0), which the analysis does not provide. The relative amplitudes of the higher-order peaks (1R–6R) do not help as a mean-free-path gauge either. For a given contact spacing, the n-th peak travels the same total path length πd/2 and differs only by n−1 specular reflections, so its decay constrains the specularity p, not ℓ. The simulations instead fix p = 0.5 "empirically" from that decay. The TMF data, together with the negative bend resistance in Fig. S6c, convincingly show ℓ ≳ 10 µm (device scale) at low temperature. Nothing in the paper establishes ℓ > 200 µm, which is the number used to place the device "comparable to the best-in-class mono- and bilayer graphene" and to justify "ultrahigh mobility" in the title. The authors should do one of two things. Option one: give an independent absolute estimate, such as a fit of the full TMF spectrum or of the low-T negative bend resistance (Fig. S6b,c) with the Boltzmann model in which ℓ_mr, p and the contact geometry are constrained jointly, with a stated confidence interval. Option two: restate the claim as a lower bound of order the device size and remove ">200 µm" from the abstract and the comparison with refs. 44–46. In either case the fit should state that A_0 implicitly assumes no scattering at base temperature.

B. Should address

2. All device-level quality claims rest on a single 13-layer device. Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. S6 and Fig. S7 all come from the 740 µm² device of Fig. S5a–c. The four other devices in Fig. S5 (including the 1330 µm² one cited in the abstract) are shown only optically and in IR, with no capacitance, transport, TMF or magnetic imaging. The abstract and conclusion claim that the method "enable[s] highly uniform devices" and raises "area, yield and uniformity" into "a qualitatively new regime". At present uniformity and quality are demonstrated once. At minimum, the authors should show a capacitance phase diagram, or R(n,D) and a TMF or bend-resistance trace, for one or two further devices, preferably including the >1000 µm² ones, and report the spread. Separately, nothing shows that the transport quality comes from the exfoliation method itself: a control device from a standard-exfoliated flake processed with the same transfer would show that. If the authors' position is that quality comes from size and cleanliness rather than from the cryogenic step, the text should say so explicitly.

3. The identification of the n_e = 6.5×10¹² cm⁻², D = 0.41 V/nm flow (Fig. 4e) as hydrodynamic depends on an assumed ℓ_ee and an imaging diagnostic that hardly separates the regimes. In all simulations ℓ_ee is fixed at 50 nm, taken from bilayer graphene (ref. 25) rather than measured, and the Methods note it "might be further reduced". Fig. S8i shows that the main diagnostic, y_max(x) at ℓ_mr = 200 µm, is nearly the same for ℓ_ee from 100 nm to 10 µm. Only ℓ_ee ≲ 50 nm and ℓ_ee = 100 µm stand out. The fit in Fig. S8k thus mostly constrains ℓ_mr, not ℓ_ee. Second, the contrast drawn with Fig. 4a, where ballistic jets appear at (10, 0), compares two different (n_e, D) points with different bands: a dispersive, multi-pocket band versus a single surface-polarized flat band with heavier mass. The isotropic-Fermi-surface Boltzmann model cannot capture how Fermi-surface shape, trigonal warping, a smaller k_F or caustic structure suppress the jets. Third, the same 13-layer device shows six TMF peaks at 2 K at (10, 0), so ℓ_ee ≳ 100 µm there, while the claim requires ℓ_ee ≈ 50 nm at 1.6 K at (6.5, 0.41). That difference of more than three orders of magnitude may well be real for a flat band at T ≪ T_F, but it needs a justification or estimate (for example, a Fermi-liquid estimate of ℓ_ee from the band of Fig. 3a at D = 0.41 V/nm). The authors should: (i) show χ² or residual maps over (ℓ_ee, ℓ_mr) for Fig. 4e, using the full images, not only y_max; (ii) show a simulation with ℓ_ee → ∞ and ℓ_mr matched, including the actual Fermi surface at (6.5, 0.41) or at least its k_F; and (iii) temper "deep in the hydrodynamic regime" if ℓ_ee is not bounded from above.

4. The transport evidence for a size-driven Poiseuille-to-porous crossover is consistent with simpler explanations. The evidence rests on two geometries, a 0.5 µm channel and a 10 µm square (Fig. 4g,j). In the narrow channel, the dR/dT < 0 regime around 15–30 K is assigned to the Gurzhi effect. These curves, however, lie in half- or quarter-metal phases, where spin or valley fluctuation scattering, proximity to phase boundaries in (n, D), or ballistic-to-diffusive boundary scattering with temperature-dependent specularity could also produce a resistance minimum. In the square, monotonic R(T) rising from a negative value is what one expects when ballistic bend resistance (Fig. S6c) gives way to phonon-limited scattering, with or without hydrodynamics. The simulated hydrodynamic prediction (Fig. 4l) is R ≈ 0 for small ℓ_G, so the square data mainly show the absence of a Gurzhi minimum, not porous flow as such. Fig. 4i and l also treat ℓ_ee⁻¹ as a temperature proxy with no mapping from T to ℓ_ee, so the comparison is qualitative. The authors should: (i) add the control simulation with no electron-electron scattering (ℓ_ee → ∞) and ℓ_mr(T) taken from Fig. 3j for both geometries, showing that the channel minimum cannot arise without ℓ_ee; (ii) show the channel's R(T) at a point outside the magnetic phases, if one is accessible; and (iii) where possible, add an intermediate channel width (for example 1–3 µm on the same device) to support calling it a "size-driven crossover".

5. The exfoliation statistics are not controlled for confounders, and the proposed mechanism is not tested. Fig. 1g reports mean rhombohedral fractions of 12% for standard and 32% for cryogenic shock exfoliation, and mean flake areas of 1200 versus 3100 µm². The paper does not state how many flakes, exfoliation sessions and bulk crystals enter each data set, nor whether both methods used the same crystals and tape in interleaved sessions. Rhombohedral fraction is known to depend on thickness, and the inclusion window is 3 to ~20 layers. If cryogenic shock produces thicker or larger flakes, part of the increase could come from a different thickness distribution rather than from stacking conversion. The paper says the shock "induces a partial conversion of Bernal to rhombohedral stacking, likely via differential thermal contraction induced stress". An alternative is that the shock preferentially cleaves along pre-existing stacking faults or rhombohedral-rich regions of the bulk crystal. The authors should: (i) give N (flakes, sessions, crystals) and per-crystal or per-session results with uncertainties or a statistical test; (ii) show the fraction resolved by layer number for both methods; and (iii) add process controls that isolate the active step: heating to 110 °C without LN₂ immersion, and LN₂ immersion without prior heating. If the mechanism is not tested, the conversion claim should be presented as a hypothesis.

6. The transfer and fabrication yield statistics are confounded, and the definition of yield is too narrow for the abstract's wording. In Fig. S4b, each bar compares transfers with and without one technique, but the techniques were applied together and adopted over time by seven people of "varying experience levels". Experience, sample selection (thicker flakes, sMIM pre-screening) and the other techniques are therefore entangled with each single-technique comparison. The cumulative 24/26 is the yield when all techniques are combined, not proof that each step contributes. "Yield" is defined as the fraction of transfers without relaxation to Bernal, judged after transfer (Fig. S4 caption). Yet the abstract speaks of "fabrication yields of ~90%" for devices. The authors should say whether stacking was re-checked after nanofabrication (etching, annealing, top-gate transfer, contact deposition) and how many of the 24 became working devices. They should also either state that the individual bars are not independent comparisons or present a breakdown that controls for the others (for example, by time period or by operator).

7. The stacking order and layer number in the final device are inferred rather than verified. Rhombohedral identity is assigned from IR contrast and sMIM (Fig. 1b–f, S1, S5). Within a "rhombohedral" region, these probes may not detect a single buried stacking fault (for example ABCAB|ACBA…) or ABC/ACB twin domains. Saying that the capacitance map (Fig. 2b) shows "no additional features" is a weak argument for uniformity, since a minority-area region contributes a minority signal. The paper also does not say how N = 13 was determined; the AFM in Fig. 2a gives the height of the whole stack. The authors should give the basis for N, and compare the measured C_P(n_e, D) features with the 13-layer tight-binding model of the Methods (for example, the positions of the layer-polarized phase boundaries or the D-field onset of the gap). Raman 2D-peak mapping or Raman of the transferred device would also help.

8. The effective-mass extraction is described with more certainty than the fit supports. Fig. 3j (labelled i in the figure) and Fig. S7b report m* = 0.075 ± 0.05 m_e. As printed, this uncertainty (67%) makes the "excellent agreement" with the calculated 0.078 m_e uninformative; if it is a typo for ±0.005, it should be corrected. The model (Eq. 8) assumes parabolic bands and a temperature-independent Lorentzian broadening δB_0, while Fig. S7a shows that the Lorentzian misfits the lineshape tails substantially. In addition, the fit in Fig. S7b deviates systematically from the data between ~50 and 150 K. The authors should state the fit uncertainty and how it was obtained, show how sensitive m* is to the lineshape choice (for example, using the measured low-T lineshape as K instead of a Lorentzian), and give the band's non-parabolicity at E_F = 160 meV.

C. Minor

9. Fig. 3 panel labels i and j are swapped relative to the caption and the text. In the figure, j is ℓ_mfp(T) and i is the normalized amplitude ratio. The caption and the text ("shown in Fig. 3i … Matthiessen's rule"; "best fit of m* … (Fig. 3j)") have them the other way round.

10. In the Methods, W(E,T) ≈ W(E_F,T) ~ exp(πd/(2ℓ_mfp)) is missing a minus sign in the exponent; Eq. 5 implies exp(−πd/2ℓ). The fitted exponent α = 1.56 ± 0.05 in Matthiessen's rule is reported without physical interpretation (it lies between T, phonon, and T², electron-electron). One sentence on what it means, and on the fact that TMF ℓ_mfp includes electron-electron and phonon scattering rather than only disorder, would help. The latter matters because the simulations use this TMF value as ℓ_mr.

11. There are inconsistent parameters for the charge-neutrality regime. The main text and the Fig. 4d caption use ℓ_mr ≈ 10 µm. The Methods say the best agreement is "ℓ_mr approximately equal to the device size", and Fig. S8k uses ℓ_mr = 20 µm. "Fig. S8h,g" should read "S8g,h". The Methods also note that near neutrality ℓ_mr is limited by electron-hole scattering, which is not a short-range impurity process; the caveat about using it in the two-rate model should be stated in the main text.

12. The title claims "ultrahigh mobility", but no mobility (or Drude estimate from R_s and n_e) is given anywhere. Either quote a number with its basis or use "high quality" or "long mean free path" in line with concern 1.

13. In Fig. 1g the caption uses geometric means for area, while the text quotes "averages rising from 1200 µm² … to 3100 µm²". The averaging used for the text numbers (geometric or arithmetic; area-weighted or per-flake fraction) and the number of points per series should be stated. The supplementary video cited in the Methods for the pickup process should be included or the reference removed.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Replace the relative TMF analysis with a joint Boltzmann fit of the absolute TMF spectrum and the low-temperature bend resistance in the square, giving a bounded estimate of ℓ_mr and p for the 13-layer device.
- Add electrical characterization of at least one more device, ideally one of the >1000 µm² devices, so that the uniformity and quality claims rest on more than one sample.
- Add the two process controls (heat only; LN₂ only) and a thickness-resolved comparison to Fig. 1g. This would turn the method from a recipe into an understood mechanism and make it more transferable to other metastable vdW polytypes.
- For the hydrodynamic section, add the no-electron-electron (ℓ_ee → ∞) control simulations for both geometries and the (ℓ_ee, ℓ_mr) residual maps, so readers can see which conclusions the images and R(T) actually constrain.

5. Verdict

Verdict: major revision
