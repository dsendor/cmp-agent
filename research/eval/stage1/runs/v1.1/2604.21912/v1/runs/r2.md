Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper introduces "cryogenic shock exfoliation", in which graphite is cleaved onto a heated SiO2/Si chip and the tape and chip are then plunged into liquid nitrogen. Combined with a low-pressure, cavity-suspended PC transfer, the authors report a higher fraction of rhombohedral stacking (12% → 32%), much larger single-stacking domains (a tail beyond 1500 µm²) and a ~90% transfer yield. A 13-layer, 10 × 10 µm² dual-gated device is characterized with capacitance, nSOT magnetization imaging, transverse magnetic focusing (TMF), nSOT current imaging and channel/square transport. If the exfoliation statistics hold up, this is a practically important materials advance for a very active field: the scarcity and small size of rhombohedral domains limits essentially every experiment on rhombohedral multilayer graphene. The physics claims (a mean free path above 200 µm and a size-driven Poiseuille-to-porous crossover) are offered as evidence of device quality. They are more interesting, and less firmly established, than the fabrication claims.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- A simple, easily adopted exfoliation protocol with a clear before/after comparison on equal substrate areas (Fig. 1g,h; Fig. S2), and domain sizes well beyond those in the literature (Fig. 1d–f, Fig. S1).
- Transfer-yield statistics broken down by technique and gathered by seven co-authors (Fig. S4b), with optical and IR images of several completed large-area devices (Fig. S5).
- Strong real-space evidence of uniformity in the flagship device: the nSOT magnetization map (Fig. 2c) and a capacitance phase diagram with no extra features (Fig. 2b).
- High-quality ballistic transport: up to six TMF peaks on all four edges (Fig. 3c), negative bend resistance up to ~100 K (Fig. S6c), and nSOT current imaging that clearly separates ballistic, near-neutrality and single-surface flow profiles (Fig. 4a,c,e).
- A useful kinetic-theory framework (Methods, Figs. S8–S9) that links the imaging and transport data.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The headline "disorder mean free path exceeding 200 µm" is not determined by the TMF analysis as performed. In Methods (Eqs. 3–6), ℓmfp(T) = −(πd/2)/ln[A(T)/A0], where A0 is the average of the four lowest-temperature peak areas (Fig. S7b). Normalizing to A0 sets the low-temperature scattering to zero by construction, so this procedure measures only the temperature-dependent part of 1/ℓ. Any temperature-independent disorder scattering 1/ℓ0 is divided out. With d ≈ 5 µm, ℓ = 200 µm corresponds to A/A0 ≈ exp(−π·5/400) ≈ 0.96. The values near 200–400 µm in Fig. 3j are therefore set by a ≲4% deviation from a self-normalized baseline. That is comparable to the scatter in Fig. S7b and to the uncertainty from the sloped baseline and integration window in Fig. 3e–h. The Methods text concedes as much ("not well suited to determine the mean-free path at lowest temperatures … We can only note that ℓ0 ≥ 200 µm"), but neither the abstract nor the main text carries this caveat.

The other evidence does not reach 200 µm either. All focusing peaks follow trajectories of total length ~πd/2 ≈ 8 µm, so six peaks show specular boundaries and ℓ ≳ device size, not ℓ ≫ device size. The negative bend resistance (Fig. S6c) likewise shows ℓ ≳ 10 µm. The authors should do one of the following:
   - give an absolute bound on ℓ0, e.g. from the absolute focusing amplitude compared with ballistic simulations using the stated p = 0.5 specularity, from the relative amplitudes of successive focusing peaks, or from the low-temperature resistivity together with the known n and m*;
   - add error bars on ℓmfp(T) that propagate the uncertainty in A0 and the baseline;
   - or restate the claim as ℓ ≳ 10 µm (device-size limited) at low temperature, with ~2–3 µm at 300 K.

   A related point: ℓmfp from TMF includes small-angle electron–electron scattering, which the paper elsewhere argues is very strong in other regimes. It is therefore not purely a "disorder" mean free path, and the fitted α = 1.56 is left uninterpreted.

2. The "size-driven crossover from Poiseuille to porous flow" rests on comparing only two geometries, and alternative explanations are not excluded. The evidence is (i) a resistance minimum at 15–30 K in the 0.5 µm channel (Fig. 4g) and (ii) its absence in the 10 µm square (Fig. 4j), interpreted with simulations (Fig. 4i,l) in which only ℓee varies and ℓmr = 200 µm stays fixed. Specific issues:
   - Fig. 4g is taken in the single-surface half- and quarter-metal regime (points chosen as such, p. 5). Both are spin/valley-ordered phases whose order parameter, Fermi surface and spin-fluctuation scattering change on the 10–30 K scale. dR/dT < 0 below the minimum could come from the ordering itself, from weak localization or from boundary scattering changing with T, not from the Gurzhi effect. The authors should show:
     - the minimum at several (ne, D) points, including a non-polarized, non-ordered single-surface or multi-band metal;
     - its evolution with in-plane or out-of-plane magnetic field (a Gurzhi minimum should be suppressed once the cyclotron radius is comparable to the width);
     - the ordering temperatures of the chosen states.
   - In the square, R(T) is dominated by ballistic bend resistance (negative at low T, Fig. 4j, Fig. S6c). Its monotonic rise follows directly from ℓmr dropping below the device size, as the TMF data show (Fig. 3j). The absence of a minimum therefore does not independently show porous hydrodynamic flow. Ballistic-to-diffusive physics alone, with no hydrodynamics, may reproduce it. The authors should show that a simulation with ℓee → ∞ and ℓmr(T) taken from Fig. 3j fails to reproduce Fig. 4j, or else temper the claim.
   - The simulations hold ℓmr = 200 µm fixed while ℓee varies, yet Fig. 3j shows ℓmr falling to ~10 µm by 100 K. A temperature-to-(ℓee, ℓmr) mapping that uses the measured ℓmr(T) is needed for even a semi-quantitative comparison. The magnitudes also disagree: the simulated channel resistance drops by roughly an order of magnitude (Fig. 4i), while the measured dip is ~10% on a ~400 Ω/sq background (Fig. 4g).
   - "Size-driven" implies a width dependence, but only one channel width and one square are shown. Several channel widths from the same flake, or nSOT current imaging of the Poiseuille profile in the channel, would directly support the claim.

B. Should address

3. The uniformity probes are mainly sensitive to the surfaces, while the device is 13 layers thick. The penetration-field capacitance (Fig. 2b), nSOT magnetization (Fig. 2c, taken in a surface-polarized half-metal) and the single-surface transport all probe the outer layers and their surface bands. An interior stacking fault (e.g. ABCAB|ACBA…) could leave the surface bands, and so these signatures, largely intact. The argument from capacitance ("no additional features … suggests uniform stacking") is also weak: a Bernal or faulted minority region would add a smooth background, not necessarily new features. Pre-transfer sMIM and post-transfer IR (Fig. S5) are the only probes of the full stack. The authors should show sMIM, IR or Raman maps of the final 13-layer device region after fabrication, and state the detection limit for minority inclusions in Fig. 2c (pixel size, sensitivity to a sub-µm nonmagnetic region).

4. Only one device is characterized electronically. The yield (~90%) is defined by the absence of IR-visible relaxation after transfer (Fig. S4b caption), not by electronic quality. The capacitance, magnetization, TMF, current-imaging and transport data all come from a single 13-layer device. To support "highly uniform devices" as a reproducible outcome, the authors should give at least basic transport (e.g. TMF or negative bend resistance, or the (ne, D) phase diagram) for one or more of the Fig. S5 devices, ideally at a different layer number. Otherwise "highly uniform" should be described as shown for one device.

5. The exfoliation comparison (Fig. 1g,h) needs controls for confounders and for the proposed mechanism.
   - Thickness and size distributions: rhombohedral fraction depends on layer number and correlates with flake size. In Fig. 1g the standard flakes cluster at smaller areas. The authors should give the layer-number distributions for both methods and compare the rhombohedral fraction at matched thickness and flake size.
   - Sample base: state how many bulk crystals and exfoliation sessions each population came from, and whether the two were interleaved over time.
   - Mechanism: the attribution to thermal-contraction shear stress is untested. Controls that separate the steps (heating to 110 °C without the LN2 quench; quenching without prior heating; different tapes or substrates with different expansion coefficients) would show which step matters and would strengthen the "partial conversion of Bernal to rhombohedral" interpretation.
   - Assignment: the IR contrast labels all bright regions as rhombohedral, while the y-axis is "non-Bernal" fraction. The authors should state how often sMIM finds mixed or faulted stacking (not pure ABC) in IR-bright regions.

6. The transfer-yield attribution has confounders. In Fig. S4b, the techniques were adopted over time and by different people, so the 7/24 → 50/62 improvement may partly reflect learning, flake thickness (the ≥10-layer subset already yields 29/33) or flake source (cryogenic shock or standard). The authors should give yields for each technique with the others held fixed where possible (e.g. pressureless transfer split by layer number and by whether sMIM pre-screening was used). They should also state when after transfer the stacking was assessed, and with which probe.

7. The flow-regime assignments from nSOT current imaging are not unique. Fig. S8i shows that ymax(x) is nearly insensitive to ℓee over 10 nm–1 µm at ℓmr = 200 µm, while S8j shows strong sensitivity to ℓmr. ℓee = 50 nm is taken from bilayer graphene, not fitted. The hydrodynamic label for Fig. 4e therefore rests on the bulk-concentrated profile, and Fig. 4e still shows jet-like streaks reminiscent of Fig. 4a. The authors should:
   - show the best ballistic (ℓee ≥ device size) simulation at the Fig. 4e density and Fermi surface, with a quantitative goodness-of-fit (e.g. χ² against both ℓee and ℓmr over a grid) for Fig. 4c and 4e;
   - discuss whether current-induced heating at 10 µA changes the electron temperature, and therefore ℓee, during imaging at 1.6 K.

   Separately, ℓee ≈ 50 nm at 1.6 K in the flat surface band is remarkable for a Fermi liquid and should be discussed alongside the TMF evidence for long ballistic lengths at n = 10¹³ cm⁻², D = 0.

8. The effective-mass extraction rests on assumptions the data partly contradict. The Vpeak/Apeak analysis assumes a Lorentzian intrinsic lineshape with a temperature-independent δB0. Fig. S7a shows the Lorentzian misses the asymmetric lineshape and its tails, and any scattering-induced broadening with T would also lower Vpeak/Apeak. The quoted m* = 0.075 ± 0.05 me (p. 5) has a 67% uncertainty, which cannot support "excellent agreement" with 0.078 me; if the intended value is ±0.005, it should be corrected. The authors should explain how the uncertainty was obtained, and show the fit's sensitivity to δB0 and to a non-Lorentzian K.

C. Minor

9. Fig. 3 caption and panel labels are swapped. The caption assigns (i) to ℓmfp(T) and (j) to Vpeak/Apeak, but the figure labels the ℓmfp panel "j" and the amplitude panel "i". The text cites "Fig. 3i" for ℓmfp and "Fig. 3j" for m*.

10. Methods: W(E,T) ≈ W(EF,T) ∼ exp(πd/(2ℓmfp)) is missing a minus sign. Eq. 5 uses V_area while the text uses A(T).

11. The near-neutrality ℓmr is given as 10 µm (Fig. 4d caption; Fig. S8f), as "approximately equal to the device size" (Methods), and as 20 µm (Fig. S8k caption). Please reconcile these.

12. Abstract and introduction: "fabrication yields of ~90%" should be defined there as transfers without IR-visible relaxation (24/26, Fig. S4b), not as yields of working devices.

13. Exfoliation statistics: some selection choices need justification: why flakes with uniform areas ≥ 400 µm², why the 80 µm² domain cutoff, and why aspect-ratio exclusions are applied identically to both methods. Please also report the number of flakes in each population in Fig. 1g.

14. Typographical: "Fig. S8h,g" ordering; "Boron Nitride" capitalization; "μm²" formatting in the abstract; ref. 1 contains an ISBN entry.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Bound the disorder mean free path in absolute terms, e.g. by comparing absolute TMF amplitudes with the ballistic Boltzmann simulations already in hand, or with a longer-baseline focusing geometry that the large domains now allow. That would turn the quality claim into a quantitative benchmark.
- Use the large rhombohedral area to fabricate channels of several widths (e.g. 0.5, 1, 2, 5 µm) on one flake. This would give a genuine size-dependence of the Gurzhi minimum and make the Poiseuille-to-porous crossover direct.
- Provide basic electronic characterization of a second and third device from Fig. S5, ideally at a different thickness, to show the method's reproducibility at the level of electronic quality.
- Run the step-by-step exfoliation controls (heat only, quench only, different tapes) to support the proposed stress mechanism and to guide other groups adopting the method.

5. Verdict: major revision
