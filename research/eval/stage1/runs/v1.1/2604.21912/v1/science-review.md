A. Must address

1. The headline "disorder mean free path exceeding 200 µm" is not determined by the TMF analysis as performed. The claim appears in the abstract, the introduction, p. 5, the Methods (Eqs. 3–6) and Fig. 3j. ℓmfp(T) is obtained from ℓmfp = −(πd/2)/ln[A(T)/A0], where A0 is the low-temperature limit of the peak area (Fig. S7b is normalized "to the average of the four lowest temperature data points", and A(T) saturates below 15 K). Normalizing to A0 sets the low-temperature mean free path to infinity by construction: any temperature-independent loss, whether impurity scattering, diffuse boundary scattering or contact transmission, is absorbed into A0 and cannot appear in ℓmfp(T). The method therefore bounds only the temperature-dependent part of 1/ℓ. With d ≈ 5 µm and trajectory length πd/2 ≈ 8 µm, ℓ = 200 µm corresponds to A/A0 ≈ 0.96 and ℓ = 50 µm to ≈ 0.85; the values near 200–400 µm in Fig. 3j are set by a ≲4% deviation from a self-normalized baseline, comparable to the scatter in Fig. S7b and to the uncertainty from the sloped baseline and integration window in Fig. 3e–h. The Methods concede the analysis "is not well suited to determine the mean-free path at lowest temperatures … We can only note that ℓ0 ≥ 200 µm", but that bound comes from the same normalized ratio, and neither the abstract nor the main text carries the caveat. The other evidence does not reach 200 µm either: all focusing orders travel the same total path ~πd/2, so six peaks show specular boundaries and ℓ ≳ device size, and the decay of successive orders (1R–6R) constrains mainly the specularity p, which was itself "empirically chosen" as p = 0.5 from that decay, presupposing a long ℓ. The negative bend resistance (≈ −6 Ω at low T, Fig. S6c) likewise shows ℓ ≳ 10 µm. In addition, peak suppression is caused by both momentum-relaxing and momentum-conserving (electron–electron, phonon) scattering, so the extracted quantity is a total quasiparticle mean free path, not a "disorder" one, yet the Methods use it as the range of ℓmr ("2–200 µm from the TMF measurements") in the simulations. This number underpins the comparison with "best-in-class" mono- and bilayer graphene (refs. 44–46) and the "ultrahigh mobility" of the title. The authors should either give an absolute estimate of the residual momentum-relaxing length — for example a joint Boltzmann fit of the absolute focusing amplitudes, the full TMF spectrum and the low-temperature negative bend resistance (compared quantitatively with the simulated map in Fig. S9a), with ℓmr, p and contact geometry constrained together and a stated confidence interval (e.g. showing how well ℓmr = 20, 50, 100, 200 µm are distinguished), or a Hall/field-effect mobility from the large square — or restate the claim as a lower bound of order the device size (ℓ ≳ 10 µm at low T, ~2–3 µm at 300 K) / as a bound on the temperature-dependent scattering only, removing ">200 µm" from the abstract and qualifying the literature comparison. Error bars on ℓmfp(T) propagating the uncertainty in A0 and the baseline should be added, and the text should state that A0 implicitly assumes no scattering at base temperature.

2. The "size-driven crossover from Poiseuille to porous flow" rests on two geometries measured in different configurations and at mismatched conditions, and simpler explanations are not excluded. The evidence is a resistance minimum at 15–30 K in the 0.5 µm channel (Fig. 4g) and its absence in the 10 µm square (Fig. 4j), interpreted with simulations (Fig. 4i,l) that treat ℓee⁻¹ as a temperature proxy with ℓmr = 200 µm fixed. (i) The channel curves are taken in half- and quarter-metal phases at (2.5, 0.85), (3, 0.51) and (4, 0.51); these are spin/valley-ordered phases whose order parameter, Fermi surface and fluctuation scattering change on the 10–30 K scale, so dR/dT < 0 and a minimum could arise from the ordering itself, proximity to phase boundaries in (n, D), weak localization, or boundary scattering with temperature-dependent specularity. (ii) The channel is a local four-terminal measurement, while the square is a bend/nonlocal configuration whose R(T) is negative at low T from ballistic focusing (Fig. S6c); its monotonic rise follows from ℓmr dropping below device size (Fig. 3j), with or without hydrodynamics, and the hydrodynamic prediction (Fig. 4l) is R ≈ 0, so the square shows the absence of a Gurzhi minimum rather than porous flow as such. (iii) The simulations hold ℓmr = 200 µm while Fig. 3j shows ℓmr falling to ~10 µm by 100 K, and the magnitudes disagree: the simulated channel resistance drops by about an order of magnitude (Fig. 4i), the measured dip is ~10% on ~400 Ω/sq (Fig. 4g). (iv) There is an internal tension: if ℓee were already 50 nm at 1.6 K (as assumed for Fig. 4e), the 0.5 µm channel would be deep in the Poiseuille regime at base temperature, and it is unclear why the minimum, which should mark ℓee ≈ W, appears only at 15–30 K. (v) "Size-driven" implies a width dependence, but only one channel width and one square are shown. The authors should: run control simulations with ℓee → ∞ and ℓmr(T) from Fig. 3j for both geometries, showing that the channel minimum cannot arise without electron–electron scattering and that the square data are not reproduced by ballistic-to-diffusive physics alone; use a T → (ℓee, ℓmr) mapping based on the measured ℓmr(T) and state how ℓee(T) is constrained; measure the channel R(T) at several (n, D) points, including the (n, D) of Fig. 4e and a non-polarized, non-flat-band point such as (10¹³ cm⁻², D = 0), and give the ordering temperatures of the chosen states; show the evolution of the minimum with magnetic field (a Gurzhi minimum should be suppressed once the cyclotron radius is comparable to the width); add further channel widths (e.g. 0.5, 1, 2, 5 µm on the same flake) and show the expected scaling of the minimum's depth and position with W; and, if feasible, temperature-dependent nSOT current images across the crossover. Otherwise the claim should be tempered.

B. Should address

3. All electronic evidence for uniformity and quality comes from a single device, while the headline claims concern a fabrication method. Figs. 2, 3, 4, S6 and S7 all come from one 13-layer device (Fig. S5a–c) with 740 µm² of rhombohedral area, and the electrically probed region is the ~100 µm² square plus a 0.5 µm channel. The other devices in Fig. S5, including the 1330 µm² device cited in the abstract ("highly uniform devices exceeding 1300 µm²"), are characterized only by optical and IR images, and IR contrast after transfer does not establish electronic uniformity (stacking faults, strain, bubbles or sub-diffraction Bernal domains could survive). The ~90% yield is defined by the absence of IR-visible relaxation after transfer, not by electronic quality. The authors should give at least basic electronic characterization — a capacitance or R(n, D) phase diagram with a characteristic isospin transition, and TMF or bend resistance — for one or two further devices, preferably at a different layer number and including a >1000 µm² device, ideally with an electrically probed region larger than ~30 µm, and report the spread. They should also report how many completed devices were cooled down and how many showed the expected rhombohedral phase diagram, to separate post-fabrication yield from transfer yield. Otherwise "highly uniform devices" should be described as shown for one device.

5. Stacking order and layer number in the final device are inferred rather than verified, and the uniformity probes are mainly surface-sensitive. The penetration-field capacitance (Fig. 2b), nSOT magnetization (Fig. 2c, in a surface-polarized half-metal) and single-surface transport all probe the outer layers, so an interior stacking fault (e.g. ABCAB|ACBA…) or ABC/ACB twin domains could leave these signatures largely intact. The argument that the capacitance map shows "no additional features" is weak, since a Bernal or faulted minority region would add a smooth minority background rather than new features. Only pre-transfer sMIM and post-transfer IR (Fig. S5) probe the full stack. The paper also does not say how N = 13 was determined; the AFM in Fig. 2a gives the height of the whole stack. The authors should state the basis for N; compare measured C_P(ne, D) features (layer-polarized phase boundaries, D-field onset of the gap) with the 13-layer tight-binding model in the Methods; show sMIM, IR or Raman (2D-peak) maps of the final device region after fabrication; state what a Bernal inclusion would contribute to C_P and the minimum area fraction detectable; and give the detection limit of the nSOT map in Fig. 2c for a non-switching minority region (pixel size, sensitivity to a sub-µm nonmagnetic region), so that "uniform" has a quantitative meaning.

6. The exfoliation comparison (Fig. 1g,h) is not controlled for confounders, and the proposed mechanism is untested. The paper does not state how many flakes, exfoliation sessions/chips and bulk crystals enter each population, nor whether both methods used the same crystals and tape in interleaved sessions. The rhombohedral fraction depends on layer number (inclusion window 3 to ~20 layers) and correlates with flake size; standard flakes cluster at smaller areas in Fig. 1g, and if cryogenic shock yields thicker or larger flakes, part of the 12% → 32% increase could come from a different thickness distribution. Fig. 1g plots the non-Bernal fraction (1 − A_Bernal)/A_total while the text calls it "rhombohedral"; IR contrast and sMIM may not separate pure ABC from mixed sequences (ABCB, twinned stacking faults), common above ~10 layers. The attribution to "partial conversion of Bernal to rhombohedral stacking … via differential thermal contraction induced stress" is not separated from preferential cleavage around pre-existing rhombohedral or fault-rich regions of the bulk crystal. The authors should give N (flakes, sessions, crystals) with per-crystal or per-session results and uncertainties or a statistical test of the difference; show the layer-number distributions and the fraction at matched thickness and flake size; report how often sMIM finds mixed or faulted stacking in IR-bright regions, and confirm the assignment by Raman or IR spectroscopy on a subset of domains; and add process controls isolating the active step (heating to 110 °C without the LN₂ quench, quench without prior heating, different tapes or substrates with different expansion coefficients, or the cryogenic step applied to already-exfoliated Bernal flakes, or a check of the source crystal or tape residue). If untested, the conversion mechanism should be presented as a hypothesis.
