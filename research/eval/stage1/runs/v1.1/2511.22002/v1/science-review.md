A. Must address

1. There is no quantitative nematic order parameter, and the domains rest on hand-picked windows with no disorder null model (Figs. 3, 4). The central claim is that each nanoscale region "locally break[s] rotational six-fold symmetry" (p. 5) and that the regions form "short-range ordered electronic nematic domains" (p. 3). The evidence is three hand-selected windows per image (Fig. 3a–d, roughly 5–8 nm across; two in Fig. 4) and their FTs, which are only a few pixels across around ½Q_Bragg, with the "peak along one direction" in the Fig. 3b,d insets judged by eye against substantial noise. In a heavily disordered alloy (x = 0.33 and 0.68 put a dopant on roughly 7–14% of Sb sites), QPI or LDOS from randomly placed scatterers will always produce small windows where one direction dominates by chance, and three "domain" orientations will appear without any symmetry-breaking order. Nothing in the paper separates the observed pattern from this null case, and selection bias cannot be excluded. The authors should define a local order parameter over the full field of view, e.g. η(r) = [A_A − (A_B + A_C)/2]/ΣA from the three ½Q_Bragg (or Bragg) Fourier amplitudes via a Lawler–Fujita-type demodulation with a stated filter length, and show its spatial map, its histogram (including the fraction of area significantly anisotropic and the fraction in each orientation), and its spatial autocorrelation and correlation length, with the dependence on filter length shown and the filter length treated as a lower bound. They should run the identical analysis on a null model: simulated T-matrix or tight-binding QPI for random, uncorrelated dopants at the measured density on the real lattice, a C6-symmetrized version of the data, or orientation-randomized subwindows. They should show that the measured domain size, order-parameter distribution or correlation length is statistically incompatible with uncorrelated disorder, and that the correlation length clearly exceeds the mean dopant spacing. The same analysis is needed for the Ti Bragg-amplitude maps (Fig. 4e,f), and for each composition, since the abstract says the effect "persists in all samples". The authors should also state how the "three highlighted regions" were chosen and how many regions were examined.

2. The anisotropy is not separated from the local symmetry and strain imprinted by the dopants, especially for Ti (Figs. 3, 4; Discussion). The V site in the kagome plane has only C2v (mmm) site symmetry, so a single Ti substituent already lowers the local symmetry from C6 to C2, with a C2 axis along one of exactly three directions, through both its scattering potential and its strain field. At y = 0.2 (about 7% of V sites) the dopants are roughly 1 nm apart, comparable to the ~2–4 nm "domains" in Fig. 4. Random Ti placement would therefore produce patches of twofold Bragg-amplitude anisotropy in three orientations, with boundaries between them, without any electronic nematic order, which is exactly what Fig. 4 shows. For Sn the Sb1 site is C6-symmetric, but any finite random configuration of scatterers, Sn clusters (the bright patches in Fig. 3c at x = 0.68 look like possible clusters) or local strain from cleaving or dopant-size mismatch gives locally anisotropic QPI; the authors themselves attribute the Sn-sample modulations to "anisotropic electron scattering and interference" (p. 3, 5), and the local anisotropy of a QPI pattern follows the scatterer configuration. The Discussion (p. 7) names tip anisotropy [41] and strain [30] as extrinsic factors only for a future chirality study, but they are equally relevant to the present C2 claim. The authors should image or identify dopant positions in the same fields of view (e.g. from topography at suitable bias; the bright features in Figs. 1e,f and 3c) and test whether the local anisotropy direction and domain walls are predicted by the nearby dopant configuration; for Ti, relate each domain to the C2v orientation of nearby Ti-occupied V sites. They should map local shear and uniaxial strain from the Lawler–Fujita displacement field of the atomic lattice in the same fields of view and correlate it with the order-parameter map; for Ti this is essential, since the nematic signature is itself a Bragg-amplitude anisotropy. They should state explicitly what distinguishes "domains of electronic nematicity" from "local C2 perturbations of individual dopants or dopant clusters".

3. Short-range, disorder-pinned 2×2 CDW correlations are not excluded, so the claim that the anisotropy is "unrelated to the 2 × 2 charge density wave" is not established (abstract; p. 4; Fig. 3). The anisotropic modulations in Fig. 3 sit at about ½Q_Bragg = Q_CDW, and pristine CsV3Sb5 already shows a 2×2 state with a unidirectional component. The most economical alternative is that dopants pin short-range, locally unidirectional 2×2 (or 2×1, 2×2×n) charge correlations, i.e. fluctuating CDW remnants, which would look much like Fig. 3b,d. The three arguments on p. 4 are not decisive: absence of ½Q peaks at x ≈ 0.1 rests on Extended Data Fig. 1, which is not included, and says nothing about re-emergent short-range correlations at x ≥ 0.33; energy-limited visibility is common for pinned or disordered CDW contrast, which is strongly bias dependent and can show contrast inversion; and the "slight dispersion" and change of shape are not quantified, with the supporting Extended Data Fig. 2 also missing. The authors should plot |q|(E) of the broad ½Q peak along each direction, with error bars from fits, over the full range of Fig. 2d, and compare it quantitatively with the DFT joint-DOS prediction and with a non-dispersive CDW, showing whether the dispersion is resolved beyond the peak width (~20% of Q_Bragg). They should test for the contrast reversal across E_F expected for a CDW gap (versus none for QPI), show setpoint independence, check whether the local modulation phase is locked to the lattice in 2×2 fashion (phase map from a Lawler–Fujita-type analysis), and, if possible, cite or provide bulk diffuse X-ray scattering at x = 0.33/0.68 that bounds short-range 2×2 correlations.

4. The "parent state" claim and the phase diagram of Fig. 1b go beyond data taken only at 4.5 K (title, abstract, Fig. 1b, Discussion). All measurements are at about 4.5 K, yet Fig. 1b shades "Electronic nematicity" over the whole composition axis up to ~100 K, and the title and abstract call nematicity the "parent state … under which other exotic low-temperature phenomena subsequently emerge". The paper shows no temperature dependence, onset or transition of the anisotropy; no link to the nematicity reported in pristine CsV3Sb5 near or above T_CDW ~ 80–100 K (Ref. 38); and no link to superconductivity in these crystals (no T_c, no gap data, no measurement below T_c). The pristine sample is measured only inside the CDW phase and never analyzed for the same local anisotropy, so the statement that pristine-compound phenomena "may arise from the electronic nematic state imaged here" (p. 7) is not supported by the paper's own data. The authors should either measure the anisotropy with the order parameter of concern 1 at several temperatures for at least one CDW-free composition (e.g. x = 0.33 at 4.5 K, 20–50 K and above 80–90 K), to show it is a thermodynamic electronic phase with an onset rather than frozen-in disorder, and give bulk or local evidence that the same crystals superconduct; or restrict the title, abstract, Discussion and Fig. 1b to what is measured: local rotational-symmetry-breaking modulations at 4.5 K in CDW-free doped samples, with the parent-state link presented as a hypothesis.

B. Should address

5. The energy and bias dependence of the domain pattern, which can distinguish static nematic order from scatterer-specific interference or structural distortion, is not shown. A static nematic order should give a domain map largely independent of energy, whereas interference around scatterers should change with energy; a purely structural distortion should give a bias-independent Bragg anisotropy, while an electronic one should change in sign or strength with energy. Fig. 3a (x = 0.33) is shown only at −200 mV, Fig. 3c (x = 0.68) only at +50 mV, and the Ti data (Fig. 4) are a single topograph at +100 mV. The authors should compute the order-parameter map of concern 1 at several energies within the same dI/dV map, inside and outside the window where ½Q_Bragg is strong (Fig. 2d), and report the cross-correlation between maps; showing anisotropy at an energy where the ½Q feature is absent would also separate a Fermi-surface (q = 0) nematic from anisotropic ½Q correlations. For Ti, bias-dependent topographs of both polarities and dI/dV maps of the same area are needed to show the Bragg-amplitude anisotropy is electronic and not a setpoint or tip-height effect.

6. The Ti-doped evidence (Fig. 4) is a different, weaker observable, and its artifact controls are incomplete. The anisotropy is in Bragg-peak amplitudes of constant-current topographs over a field of view of only about 6–8 nm, with one domain wall in Fig. 4a and one more in the missing Extended Data Fig. 3. Domains of different orientation under one tip rule out a uniform tip anisotropy, but Bragg-amplitude maps are sensitive to drift, piezo nonlinearity, fast-scan direction, surface reconstruction, setpoint and the Fourier filter width. The authors should state the filter parameters for Fig. 4e,f (the width sets the smallest resolvable domain and the smoothness of the orange boundary) and whether the color scales of the two maps are common, since otherwise the claim that one area "lights up" in one map and the other area in the other cannot be compared. They should show forward and backward scans or a rotated scan frame (or a second tip), show that the boundary in Fig. 4a is the same at different setpoints, and give larger fields of view with domain statistics for all three orientations. Unless the Ti data reach the standard of the Sn data, the statement that the "phenomenology is confirmed in two different types of samples" (p. 6) should be softened, noting that the observables differ (½Q QPI for Sn, Bragg modulation for Ti).

7. The text calls a finite-q unidirectional modulation "q = 0 nematicity" without defining the order parameter. The Discussion (p. 6) describes "short-range q = 0 electronic nematicity", but the Sn-sample evidence is unidirectional modulation at finite q ≈ ½Q_Bragg, which describes a local unidirectional density wave (smectic-like), a short-range 1Q charge order, or anisotropic QPI around anisotropic scatterers, none of which by itself implies a q = 0 nematic order parameter. Only the Ti data (Bragg-peak anisotropy) are directly q = 0. The authors should state which observable is the nematic order parameter in each sample (e.g. the anisotropy of the ½Q modulation amplitudes as a q = 0 quantity), why a finite-q anisotropy implies it, and why the Sn and Ti signatures belong to the same state.

8. The mirror-symmetry statements are unquantified and internally inconsistent (pp. 3, 5, 6). The Introduction says each domain "appears to break all but one in-plane mirror symmetries", Results that it "breaks two in-plane mirror symmetries but appears to generally preserve the in-plane mirror symmetry along the direction of the arrows", and the Discussion that it has "two in-plane mirror lines that are clearly broken, and one that appears to be intact". The kagome Sb surface has six in-plane mirror lines, not three, and a unidirectional cosine modulation along Q_A (a C2 domain derived from C6v) preserves the two mirrors parallel and perpendicular to Q_A, i.e. C2v. The authors should specify which mirrors are meant and test mirror preservation quantitatively within each domain, e.g. by comparing Fourier amplitudes at mirror-related wave vectors or a correlation between the domain image and its mirror image, with uncertainties, including the tip-asymmetry check they mention (Ref. 41). As written it cannot be told whether the local symmetry is C2v, C2 or lower, which matters for the comparison with CsTi3Bi5 ("the same local symmetry"), with Ref. 44, and for the possible chiral state raised in the Discussion.

9. The DFT and autocorrelation comparison is qualitative, rests on unjustified assumptions and cannot address the anisotropy (Fig. 2i–l, Methods). The calculation linearly interpolates Wannier hoppings between CsV3Sb5 and CsV3Sb4Sn (virtual-crystal-like, capturing average filling but not disorder broadening) at x ~ 0.25 while the data are at x = 0.33; shows CECs only at k_z = 0 although the Fermi surface disperses in k_z; compares DFT energies directly with experiment without a stated shift, chemical-potential correction for Sn hole doping, or renormalisation, with agreement at −100 meV only qualitative; and assumes "all scattering processes are possible", giving many features absent from the data. The CECs and autocorrelation are C6-symmetric by construction, so they can at best assign the wave vector and explain the energy window, not the local C2; and any pocket centred at M produces ½Q_Bragg-type scattering by symmetry, so the wave-vector match alone does not discriminate between models. The authors should give the energy alignment, show the k_z dependence or a k_z-integrated JDOS, compare predicted and measured |q|(E) and the energy window (Fig. 2d, ellipse) quantitatively (see concern 3), state the dopant site assumed (Methods: Sn "within the kagome plane") and whether the STM surface Sb layer is the same Sb1 layer, and state explicitly that the calculation is used only to assign the wave vector.
