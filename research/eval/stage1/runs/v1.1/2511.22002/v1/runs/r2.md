Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper uses SI-STM on CsV3Sb5 with the 2×2 CDW suppressed by Sn substitution on the Sb site (x ≈ 0.1, 0.33, 0.68) and Ti substitution on the V site (y = 0.2). It reports nanoscale regions in which modulations are stronger along one lattice direction. From these it concludes that electronic nematicity is "ubiquitous" across the AV3Sb5 phase diagram, is unrelated to the 2×2 CDW, and is "the parent state of kagome superconductors". Whether rotational symmetry breaking in AV3Sb5 is separate from the 2×2 CDW is an important and open question, and tuning composition to remove the CDW is a sensible way to ask it. As written, though, the evidence is qualitative local anisotropy in strongly disordered alloys, measured at a single temperature. The jump from these images to a nematic phase, and then to a "parent state", is much larger than the data support.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- Two chemically distinct routes to suppressing the CDW (Sb-site Sn and V-site Ti) are compared within one study.
- STM topographs, FT linecuts and dI/dV maps clearly show that the sharp 2×2 CDW and 4×1 stripe peaks are absent for x ≥ 0.33 and for y = 0.2 (Figs. 1g–k, 2b,d, 4b). This is useful local confirmation of the bulk phase diagram.
- The broad ½Q_Bragg features in the x = 0.33 sample are linked to pockets near M through DFT constant-energy contours and their autocorrelation (Fig. 2i–l). This is a plausible and useful identification.
- Figs. 3 and 4 show domains of different orientation imaged with the same tip, which partly addresses the concern that the anisotropy comes from the tip.
- The question matters to a broad community, and the data cover a wide composition range.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. **The anisotropy in Figs. 3 and 4 is not tested against isotropic disorder, so it is not shown to be nematic order.** The central claim is that each nanoscale region "locally break[s] rotational six-fold symmetry" (p. 5) and that these regions form "short-range ordered electronic nematic domains" (p. 3). The evidence is three hand-picked windows per image (Fig. 3b,d, roughly 5–7 nm across) and their FTs, which hold only a few pixels around ½Q_Bragg. In a strongly disordered alloy (x = 0.33 puts a dopant on roughly one in fifteen Sb sites), QPI or local density of states from randomly placed scatterers will look anisotropic in any small window just from statistics. Three domain orientations will then appear without any symmetry-breaking order. No quantity in the paper separates the observed pattern from that null case. The authors should:
   - define a local nematic order parameter, for example the normalized difference of the three ½Q_Bragg (or Bragg) Fourier amplitudes computed with a stated window size, and map it over the full fields of view;
   - report its distribution and its spatial autocorrelation length, and show how both depend on the window size;
   - compare against a null model under identical processing: an isotropic simulation with the measured dopant density, a C6-symmetrized version of the data, or orientation-randomized subwindows. Only anisotropy that is significantly larger, or more spatially correlated, than this baseline supports "nematic domains";
   - make the same analysis for the Ti-doped Bragg-amplitude maps (Fig. 4e,f), and give the Fourier-filter width. That width sets the smallest apparent domain size and the smoothness of the boundary drawn in orange.

2. **Local dopant and strain anisotropy is not ruled out as the source of the domains, especially for Ti.** The V site in the kagome plane has only C2v site symmetry. A Ti atom on a V site therefore produces a local perturbation (strain field, scattering potential) with a C2 axis along one of three directions. A random distribution of Ti will produce patches of local twofold anisotropy in three orientations without any electronic nematic order. Fig. 4 shows exactly this pattern: atomic-lattice (Bragg-peak) modulation that is stronger along one direction in 2–4 nm patches. Sn clusters and local strain can do the same in the Sn series. The phrase "electronic nematicity" requires showing that the electronic anisotropy is not simply the local structural and chemical anisotropy imprinted by dopants. The authors should:
   - correlate the domain orientation maps with the positions and local configuration of dopants, which are often visible as defects in topographs (e.g. the bright features in Figs. 1e,f and 3c);
   - show the bias dependence of the Bragg-peak anisotropy in the Ti sample. A purely structural distortion should be roughly independent of bias; an electronic one should change sign or strength with energy. Fig. 4 is taken at a single bias of +100 mV;
   - discuss the measured or estimated local strain;
   - state explicitly what distinguishes "domains of electronic nematicity" from "local C2 perturbations of individual dopants or dopant clusters".

3. **The data do not support the claims about the phase diagram and the "parent state".** All data are at about 4.5 K. Yet Fig. 1b shades an "Electronic nematicity" region extending to about 100 K across the whole composition axis, and the title and abstract call nematicity the "parent state" under which other phenomena "subsequently emerge". The paper does not show:
   - any temperature dependence, onset or transition of the anisotropy;
   - any comparison with the nematicity reported in pristine CsV3Sb5 near T_CDW. Note that the pristine sample is never analyzed for the same local ½Q_Bragg/Bragg anisotropy as a reference;
   - any link to the superconducting state at these compositions (no gap data, no T_c of the measured crystals).

   A "parent state" claim needs at minimum: anisotropy measured at several elevated temperatures for at least one doped composition, to show it is a thermodynamic electronic phase rather than frozen-in disorder; and bulk or local evidence that the same crystals superconduct. Otherwise the title, abstract and Fig. 1b should be restricted to "local rotational-symmetry-breaking modulations at 4.5 K in CDW-free doped samples".

4. **The signal measured is finite-q and unidirectional, but it is labelled q = 0 nematicity.** In the Sn series, the anisotropic feature is a modulation at about ½Q_Bragg along one direction (Fig. 3; the domain labels even read Q^A_CDW, Q^B_CDW, Q^C_CDW). The Discussion calls this "short-range q = 0 electronic nematicity" (p. 6). A short-range unidirectional modulation at ½Q_Bragg is, phenomenologically, also what a short-range 1Q (2×1) charge order or a pinned fluctuating CDW would look like. The arguments on p. 4 against residual short-range CDW are weak:
   - the peaks are "only observed at some energies": in disordered CDWs, setpoint effects and energy-dependent contrast are common;
   - there is "slight dispersion" and a change of shape: this is not quantified, and Extended Data Fig. 2 is not in the submission;
   - the peaks are absent at x ≈ 0.1: this does not preclude re-emergent short-range correlations at higher x.

   The authors should:
   - quantify the dispersion of the ½Q_Bragg peak position with energy, with error bars, and compare it with the DFT autocorrelation over the same energy range;
   - test for the contrast reversal across E_F expected for a CDW gap, versus none for QPI;
   - show setpoint-independence;
   - explain how a finite-q QPI anisotropy implies a q = 0 nematic order parameter. Anisotropic QPI around anisotropic scatterers does not imply it.

B. Should address

5. **The sample statistics are not stated.** The paper does not say how many crystals, cleaves, tips and fields of view were studied for each composition. The text lists Sn compositions "x = 0.15, x = 0.33 and x = 0.68" (p. 3), but the figures show x = 0.1 and the text also says "x ∼ 0.1". The Ti result rests on images of about 6 nm (Fig. 4) and one further boundary in Extended Data Fig. 3. Give the numbers of samples and areas, show that the domain phenomenology repeats across them, and give the domain fraction of each orientation over large areas. The Discussion itself notes that an imbalance between orientations would change the large-area FT symmetry.

6. **The mirror-symmetry claims are not quantified.** The text states that each domain "breaks two in-plane mirror symmetries but appears to generally preserve the in-plane mirror symmetry along the direction of the arrows" (p. 5; also p. 6). Nothing tests this. Since the Discussion raises a possible chiral state at lower temperature, a quantitative mirror-asymmetry measure for each domain is needed. Examples: the intensity ratio of FT peaks related by the putative mirror, or a correlation between the domain image and its mirror image, with uncertainty. Include the tip-asymmetry check the authors themselves mention (ref. 41).

7. **The DFT comparison is not quantitative and does not test the anisotropy.** Several assumptions need justifying:
   - the calculations interpolate hoppings linearly between CsV3Sb5 and CsV3Sb4Sn, a virtual-crystal-like approach, for "x ∼ 0.25", while the data are at x = 0.33;
   - only kz = 0 contours are used;
   - no rigid shift or chemical-potential correction is discussed for the hole doping from Sn;
   - the matching energy (−100 meV) agrees only qualitatively.

   The autocorrelation (Fig. 2j) is sixfold by construction, so it supports the wavevector assignment but says nothing about the claimed anisotropy. The authors should:
   - compare the predicted and measured |q| against energy over the full range in Fig. 2d;
   - state the dopant site assumed (Methods: Sn "within the kagome plane") and whether the STM surface Sb layer is the same Sb1 layer;
   - justify the kz = 0 choice, or show kz-integrated contours.

8. **No undoped reference is analyzed the same way.** The pristine x = 0 sample is used only to show the sharp 2×2 peaks. No local-window anisotropy analysis, identical to the one applied to the doped samples, is shown for it. A lightly doped sample just past x_c (x ≈ 0.1, where the authors say ½Q_Bragg peaks are absent) is a natural control. Is local anisotropy present there at the Bragg or QPI wavevectors, and if not, why not, given that the "nematicity" is said to persist across the whole phase diagram?

9. **Several key arguments rely on Extended Data that are missing.** The case against residual CDW at x ∼ 0.1 (Extended Data Fig. 1), the energy dependence of the peak shape (Extended Data Fig. 2) and the second Ti boundary (Extended Data Fig. 3) are all cited but not included. They are needed to assess concerns 4 and 5.

10. **The actual compositions of the measured crystals are not given.** Sn and Ti contents are nominal growth ratios (Methods). Give the measured compositions (EDS or WDS, or bulk structural refinement) for the crystals measured, and estimate the local dopant density from STM defect counts. Concern 1 needs this to set the disorder level of the null model.

C. Minor

11. **Fig. 1b, phase diagram.** The caption refers to "four blue circles", but the markers are diamonds and only three are clearly visible. The temperature axis is broken (4 K to 80 K). The nematic shading above the SC dome is not based on data (see concern 3).

12. **Notation for the Ti compound is inconsistent.** The text uses CsV3−xTixSb5 (p. 5), Methods uses "(x=0.2)", and Fig. 4 uses CsV3−ySb5Tiy. Use one formula and one symbol.

13. **The FT linecut direction in Fig. 1k is ambiguous.** It is taken "along the Q_stripe direction (if present)". Specify the direction used for x ≥ 0.1 and whether linecuts along all three directions were averaged.

14. **The "~20% of Q_Bragg" width of the broad peaks (p. 4) should be given as a fitted FWHM with uncertainty.** The corresponding real-space correlation length should be stated and compared with the domain size in Fig. 3.

15. **Fig. 4 needs scale information.** Give the Fourier-filter parameters for panels e,f and state whether the color scales are common to both maps. Without this, the claim that one area "lights up" in one map and the other area in the other map cannot be compared.

16. **The relation to ref. 44 should be discussed.** Ref. 44 is a concurrent study of Ti-doped CsV3Sb5 reporting a chiral-nematic state. Since the present paper reports mirror-preserving nematic domains, the differences or agreements between the two should be addressed explicitly.

17. **Minor wording.** "a crucial question that remains" (p. 3); "lights up in for" (p. 6); "seemingly oriented along a single crystalline directions" (p. 5).

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Build the paper around a quantitative local nematic order parameter. Map it over large fields of view for every composition, including x = 0 and x ≈ 0.1, and benchmark it against an isotropic-disorder null model processed identically.
2. Add variable-temperature SI-STM on at least one CDW-free composition (e.g. x = 0.33 at 4.5 K, about 30 K and about 80 K). This would show whether the local anisotropy is an electronic phase with an onset or simply frozen-in disorder. It is the most direct way to support the "parent state" language.
3. Register the domain orientation against dopant and defect positions. For Ti, relate each domain to the C2v orientation of nearby Ti-occupied V sites, and show the bias dependence of the Bragg-peak anisotropy.
4. Tone down the title, abstract and Fig. 1b to match what is measured: local, low-temperature rotational-symmetry-breaking modulations in CDW-free doped CsV3Sb5. Present the link to a universal parent state as a hypothesis.

5. Verdict

Verdict: major revision
