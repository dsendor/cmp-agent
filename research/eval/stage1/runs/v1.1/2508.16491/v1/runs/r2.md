Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper proposes that the high-temperature electrical resistivity of a metal carries a fingerprint of a flat band (FB) near E_F: phonon-mediated interband scattering into the FB makes the scattering rate follow the DOS, giving a sublinear ρ(T) when E_F lies below the FB edge and a superlinear ρ(T) when it lies above. A two-parabolic-band Boltzmann model shows these trends. A modified Bloch–Grüneisen (BG) formula, Eq. (1), using the DFT DOS is then fitted to ρ(T) of five intermetallics (Ni3In, CaRh2, Ni3Ge from the literature; Pt3Sn, Pd3Pb and CoSn measured here up to 862 K). The idea is in the spirit of Mott's s–d model, and a cheap transport-based diagnostic for flat bands would be useful. As written, however, the paper does not show that the signature is specific to flat bands, or that it can actually be used to locate one.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- A simple, physically clear idea: the scattering phase space follows D(E), so a sharp DOS edge near E_F leaves a characteristic curvature in ρ(T) at T ~ Δ/3. The scaling plot in Fig. 3(e) makes this clear, including the deviation from BG behaviour at T/Δ ≈ 1/3.
- New high-temperature data (1.5–862 K) on CoSn single crystals and polycrystalline Pt3Sn and Pd3Pb, plus specific heat for Pd3Pb.
- The trends line up with independent indicators: the Sommerfeld coefficients in Fig. 4(c) and the DFT FB positions in Fig. 4(b). The CoSn deviation near 400 K ≈ Δ_DFT/3 with Δ ≈ 0.1 eV is a concrete, falsifiable consistency check.
- The proposal to use the thermopower sign as a complementary probe is useful.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The curvature of ρ(T) is not shown to be specific to flat bands, and other common explanations are not excluded.
   - The central claim (abstract; conclusion, p. 4–5) is that sublinear or superlinear ρ(T) at high T reveals where the FB sits relative to E_F. Both curvatures are generic in intermetallics.
   - Sublinear ρ(T) at resistivity levels of 100–160 μΩ cm (Ni3In, CaRh2 in Fig. 4(a)) is the textbook regime of resistivity saturation near the Mott–Ioffe–Regel limit. It is routinely described by the parallel-resistor formula without any DOS structure.
   - Superlinear ρ(T) can come from thermal expansion, phonon softening or anharmonicity, a temperature-dependent Debye temperature, or a temperature-dependent chemical potential.
   - The authors themselves note (p. 3, refs. 45–47) that similar ρ(T) features in Heusler compounds and skutterudites arise from other energy-dependent scattering.
   - The paper has no null control: no compound with a smooth DOS near E_F (for example a structural analogue without an FB, or a compound with comparable ρ magnitude) that shows BG-like linear ρ(T) when analysed the same way.
   - There is also no fit with a constant DOS plus saturation (parallel resistor) to set against the Eq. (1) fits. Without this, the agreement in Fig. 4(a) does not discriminate between mechanisms.
   - Requested:
     - Fit each dataset with (i) the standard BG law, (ii) BG plus a parallel-resistor saturation term and (iii) Eq. (1), using the same number of free parameters, and report the goodness of fit for each.
     - Include at least one reference metal without an FB near E_F, ideally isostructural (for example a Cu3Au-type compound for the Ni3Ge family), measured under the same conditions.
     - Discuss when the FB interpretation can be distinguished from saturation, using ρ magnitudes relative to the Ioffe–Regel limit.

2. The "single-parameter fit" and the claim that FB positions can be inferred are not documented or shown quantitatively.
   - The text says C is "the only free parameter" (p. 3). Yet Eq. (1) also contains Θ_D and the exponent n (3 or 5), and presumably a residual resistivity ρ0.
   - The main text gives none of these per compound: which n was used, whether Θ_D was fixed from specific heat or fitted, and how ρ0 was handled. The supplementary material (ref. 30) is not part of the submission.
   - The only quantitative test of the "inference" is the CoSn estimate T ≈ Δ_DFT/3 ≈ 400 K in Fig. 4(e). That onset rests on a hand-drawn dashed linear extrapolation over 200–800 K, with no stated criterion or uncertainty.
   - The fit formula said to give the FB position and effective mass without any DOS information (Eq. 6 in the SM) is never applied in the main text. No extracted E_F − E_edge or m_FB values with error bars are reported for any compound.
   - The abstract and conclusion present this as "a simple, broadly applicable method for detecting flat bands". To support that, the authors should:
     - tabulate, for every compound, the fixed and free parameters with their uncertainties;
     - apply the DOS-free fit formula to each dataset and report the extracted FB edge position and m_FB with uncertainties;
     - compare these with independent determinations (DFT, and for CoSn the published ARPES FB position, refs. 38–39);
     - show how sensitive the extracted Δ is to the fit window and to the choice of n and Θ_D.

B. Should address

3. Hidden assumptions in Eq. (1).
   - Eq. (1) takes σ(E) ∝ 1/D_DFT(E). This assumes that the velocity term v²(E) and the density of current-carrying states are energy-independent, and that the FB states themselves carry no current.
   - It also uses the DOS at E rather than at E ± ħω. Phonon energies up to k_BΘ_D (tens of meV) are comparable to the FB widths and Δ values considered (20–100 meV), so inelastic smearing should matter.
   - The total DOS used includes all orbitals, not only the FB states that act as scattering sinks.
   - Requested: state these approximations explicitly; estimate their effect, for example by using the DFT transport function or band velocities for at least one compound; and say whether the conclusions about the sign of the curvature survive.

4. The temperature dependence of the chemical potential and of the lattice is not included.
   - With a sharp DOS peak within ~0.1 eV of E_F and temperatures up to 862 K (k_BT ≈ 74 meV), μ(T) can shift substantially, and in the two-band model this shift by itself changes the curvature of ρ(T).
   - Thermal expansion over this range also shifts bands.
   - Requested: say whether μ(T) is computed self-consistently in the model (Figs. 2–3) and in Eq. (1), and show the effect if it is not.

5. The flat-band model is crude for the materials studied.
   - The model uses two isotropic parabolic bands with m_FB = 10 or 100 m0.
   - Kagome and pyrochlore FBs are dispersionless over only part of the Brillouin zone, touch dispersive bands at quadratic points, and have strong orbital and momentum-dependent matrix elements. Interband electron–phonon coupling depends on orbital overlap, not only on phase space.
   - The paper should discuss why a phase-space-only, constant-coupling model is expected to hold here, and should present the qualitative agreement as qualitative.

6. The classification relies on DFT, which is known to misplace FBs.
   - The FB positions used for classification come from GGA-PBE.
   - For CaRh2, which gives the worst fit, the DOS comes from a coarse Materials Project grid (p. 3).
   - For Pt3Sn, which appears in Fig. 4(a), the main text never states where the FB lies or which regime it is in. It is also absent from Fig. 4(c).
   - Ni3In's FB position relative to E_F is sensitive to correlations, as its strange-metal behaviour shows.
   - Requested:
     - give the DFT details (code, k-mesh, spin–orbit coupling, which is important for Pt and Pb compounds, and lattice parameters);
     - show the DOS for all six compounds;
     - test how robust the fits are to rigidly shifting the DOS by ±20–50 meV. This would also show how precisely transport can pin the FB position.

7. Reliability of the high-temperature measurements is not documented.
   - The main text has no methods for the 300–862 K measurements: atmosphere, contact geometry, sample-thickness uncertainty, heating and cooling hysteresis, and whether the samples were checked after heating.
   - Sn-, Pb- and In-containing intermetallics can lose volatile elements or oxidize at these temperatures.
   - The superlinear upturn in CoSn above ~400 K, the key quantitative datum, would also follow from a slow irreversible change.
   - Requested: show heating and cooling curves and the residual resistivity before and after the high-temperature run; state how many samples were measured per compound and whether the curvature is reproducible.

8. Anisotropy and sample form are mixed.
   - The data combine CoSn along c, Ni3In in the ab plane, and polycrystalline Pt3Sn and Pd3Pb, yet Eq. (1) uses only the isotropic DOS.
   - For anisotropic kagome compounds, the c-axis and in-plane transport functions can differ strongly.
   - Requested: comment on whether the CoSn in-plane resistivity shows the same high-temperature behaviour, and on grain-boundary contributions in the polycrystals.

9. The low-temperature argument from the exponent n is weak.
   - The CoSn fit giving n = 4.99(2) (Fig. 4(d)) is used to support E_F lying away from the FB.
   - The signal, ρ − ρ0 < 0.2 μΩ cm over 1.5–55 K, is tiny, so n is strongly correlated with ρ0 and with the fit window. The quoted error is presumably statistical only.
   - Interband Wilson-type n = 3 behaviour can also be suppressed at low T by the minimum phonon wavevector needed to bridge the two Fermi surfaces.
   - Requested: report how n depends on the fit range and ρ0, and avoid treating n = 5 as evidence on the FB position.

10. Ni3In is used as a positive case even though its ρ(T) is dominated by non-phonon physics.
    - Ni3In shows n = 1 strange-metal behaviour down to the lowest temperatures (p. 4).
    - The agreement of Eq. (1) at high T could therefore be coincidental: an electron–electron or quantum-critical contribution can also be sublinear or saturating.
    - Requested: state the fit range used for Ni3In and show the residuals.

C. Minor

11. Pd3Pb's Sommerfeld coefficient of γ ≈ 0.03 mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻² (Fig. 4(c), p. 4) is unusually small even for a semimetal. Please check the units and normalization (per mole of formula unit or of atoms) and compare with the DFT bare γ for all compounds.

12. Fig. 3 caption: "(a) and (c) above E_F" refers to the FB edge position. Saying "FB edge above E_F (E_F − E_edge < 0)" in the caption, as the legends do, would avoid confusion with "E_F above the edge" in the text (p. 2).

13. Fig. 4(e): state how the dashed linear reference was determined (fit range) and mark Δ_DFT/3 together with its uncertainty.

14. Fig. 4(a): ρ0 differs between compounds and between single crystals and polycrystals. State whether ρ0 is subtracted or fitted, and give the residual resistivity ratios.

15. Text issues: "we do not included electron-electron scattering" (p. 2); Fig. 3 is discussed before Fig. 2 is fully introduced; ref. 9 lacks a volume number; the SM link is a placeholder ("xxx").

16. The thermopower proposal (p. 5) would be much stronger with at least one measured S(T) for CoSn or Pd3Pb, or with model S(T) curves computed from the same σ(E).

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a blind test. Use the DOS-free fit formula on a compound whose FB position is known independently from ARPES (CoSn), then predict the FB position for a compound not used to build the method, and compare.
2. Use tuning. Measure ρ(T) on a doped series that moves E_F through the FB edge, for example a Ni3(In,Sn) or Co(Sn,In) series, and show the predicted change from sublinear to superlinear curvature. This would be far more convincing than comparing different compounds.
3. Combine ρ(T) with S(T) and Hall data on the same samples, so the extracted Δ is constrained by several transport coefficients computed from one σ(E).
4. Bring the key SM content into the main text: the model equations, the fit formula and a parameter table.

5. Verdict: major revision
