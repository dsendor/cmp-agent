Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript proposes that the shape of the electrical resistivity ρ(T) at elevated temperatures (hundreds of kelvin) can be used to locate a flat band (FB) relative to E_F. In a two-parabolic-band Boltzmann model where the electron-phonon scattering rate follows the DOS, a heavy band just above or below E_F gives sublinear or superlinear ρ(T). The authors then fit a DOS-weighted Bloch-Grüneisen expression (Eq. 1) to ρ(T) data on five intermetallics (Ni3In, CaRh2, Ni3Ge, Pt3Sn, Pd3Pb, plus new data on CoSn). A cheap transport-based diagnostic for FBs would interest the kagome/pyrochlore community. However, the physics at its core, that interband scattering into a high-DOS band makes ρ(T) nonlinear, is Mott's s–d scattering picture (Ref. 29). The paper's novelty therefore rests on showing that the method locates a *flat* band specifically and does so quantitatively. The current version does not yet show this.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The basic message is clear and physically sound, and the model calculations (Figs. 2, 3) set out the expected trends transparently, including the T/Δ scaling in Fig. 3(e).
- New high-temperature data (up to ~860 K) on Pt3Sn, Pd3Pb and CoSn add to the literature, and the low-T CoSn exponent n = 4.99(2) (Fig. 4(d)) is a clean consistency check on phonon-dominated scattering.
- The qualitative link between sublinear ρ(T), large γ (Ni3In, CaRh2) and superlinear ρ(T), small γ (CoSn, Pd3Pb) in Fig. 4(c) is suggestive.
- The method is easy to apply and could guide which compounds deserve spectroscopic follow-up.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. **Specificity: the signature is not shown to be unique to flat bands.** The paper claims a "broadly applicable method for detecting flat bands" and that the behaviour is "universal". Sublinear ρ(T) at high T is ubiquitous in transition metals and compounds with a d-band edge near E_F. Pd, Pt and A15 compounds are the classic cases, and they are usually explained by Mott s–d scattering (the same physics as here), by Ioffe–Regel saturation/parallel-resistor behaviour, or by a temperature-dependent chemical potential. Superlinear high-T ρ(T) is likewise common, for example from thermal expansion and phonon softening (Θ_D(T)) or anharmonicity. The model itself (two parabolic bands with m_FB = 10 m0 or 100 m0) describes any heavy band. Nothing in it uses the defining features of a kagome or pyrochlore FB: dispersionless over the zone, band touching, and orbital or sublattice character that may suppress certain scattering matrix elements. As written, the analysis detects "a steep DOS feature near E_F", not a flat band. The authors should:
   - (i) show a null/reference comparison: a compound with a conventional d-band edge but no FB, ideally isostructural to one of the samples, or elemental Pd/Pt. Either the method distinguishes it from the FB compounds, or the claim must be reframed as detecting DOS edges in general.
   - (ii) quantify, or at least bound, the competing mechanisms (saturation with ρ_sat, thermal expansion, μ(T) shift) for the measured compounds. For example, fit with a parallel-resistor model for comparison and state why the FB interpretation is preferred.
   - (iii) temper "universal" and "detecting flat bands" accordingly.

2. **The "tracking"/inference claim is not demonstrated quantitatively.** The title and abstract say one "can infer the position of FBs near E_F" from ρ(T). However, the fits in Fig. 4(a) take the DFT DOS as fixed input and adjust only the prefactor C. They therefore test whether the DFT DOS is compatible with the data. They do not infer the FB position from transport. The inverse procedure (the fit formula, Eq. 6 in the SM, that yields the FB position and normalized mass without DFT) is mentioned but never applied in the main text. No extracted E_F − E_edge or m_FB/m0, with uncertainties, is reported for any compound. The only quantitative inference is CoSn (Fig. 4(e)): a deviation "at around T ≈ 400 K ≈ Δ_DFT/3" read off against a hand-placed linear dashed line. This has three problems:
   - the onset criterion is not defined, and no uncertainty is given;
   - the factor 1/3 comes from Fig. 3(e) for one specific choice, m_FB = 10 m0 and Δ = 100 meV, and its dependence on m_FB and on edge sharpness is not shown;
   - the result is compared only with the DFT value the authors already had.

   To support the headline claim the authors should:
   - (i) apply the DOS-free fit (SM Eq. 6) to every compound and tabulate E_F − E_edge and m_FB/m0 with error bars;
   - (ii) for the DFT-based fits, scan a rigid shift of E_F within D_DFT(E) and show fit quality versus shift. A clear minimum near zero shift with a stated width would show that ρ(T) actually constrains the FB position;
   - (iii) compare the inferred positions with independent spectroscopic values where available, e.g. ARPES on CoSn (Refs. 38, 39) and on Ni3In (Ref. 23), not only with the DFT that the fits use.

B. Should address

3. **Free and fixed parameters in Eq. 1 are not stated.** The text says "this prefactor is the only free parameter". Eq. 1 also contains Θ_D and n, and the data in Fig. 4(a) clearly need a residual resistivity ρ0, which is absent from Eq. 1. For each compound the authors should state:
   - the Θ_D used (fixed from specific heat, or fitted?);
   - the n used (5 or 3, and why);
   - how ρ0 was treated;
   - the temperature range of each fit;
   - a goodness-of-fit measure.

   If Θ_D is adjusted, the claimed one-parameter agreement is overstated, since Θ_D strongly controls the curvature between 50 and 300 K. Comparing the fitted Θ_D with calorimetric values would be a useful independent check.

4. **Temperature dependence of the chemical potential is not discussed.** With a large, asymmetric DOS within ~50–100 meV of E_F, μ shifts appreciably at 500–1000 K. Such a shift alters ρ(T) by the same mechanism the paper invokes and could even move E_F across the edge. The main text does not say whether μ(T) is computed self-consistently from particle conservation in the model (Figs. 2, 3) or in Eq. 1. If it is held fixed, the authors should show how much including μ(T) changes Fig. 3 and the fits in Fig. 4(a).

5. **Factorized, quasi-elastic form of Eq. 1.** Eq. 1 multiplies a BG phonon integral by a Fermi-window average of 1/D_DFT(E). In doing so it:
   - ignores the energy transfer ħω in absorption and emission, which is not negligible when Δ (20–100 meV) is comparable to phonon energies;
   - ignores the band-resolved velocities and the DOS of the initial (dispersive) states in the transport function;
   - uses the total DOS, including states from all bands, rather than the final-state DOS reachable by phonons.

   The authors should justify this approximation against the full two-band model of the SM. For example, they could apply Eq. 1 to the model DOS of Fig. 2 and compare the result with the exact model ρ(T) of Fig. 3. The statement that the results are "consistent with" first-principles RTA calculations (Refs. 33, 43) should be made quantitative by overlaying those curves.

6. **Ni3In: low-T strange-metal behaviour versus the fit.** The text notes that ρ_ab of Ni3In is linear in T down to the lowest temperatures (n = 1, Refs. 23, 31). It also notes that electron-electron scattering is excluded from the model. Yet Fig. 4(a) shows a fit to Ni3In over the whole range. The authors should state the temperature window used for Ni3In. They should also show that the high-T sublinearity is not simply the crossover of a non-phonon T-linear term (or saturation) rather than FB interband scattering, given that Ni3In is the paper's main sublinear example.

7. **Anisotropy and sample form.** The model is isotropic. However, the data mix several sample forms:
   - CoSn single crystals measured along c;
   - Ni3In measured in the ab plane;
   - polycrystalline Pt3Sn and Pd3Pb.

   Kagome FBs are strongly two-dimensional, so interband scattering rates and velocities should differ between ab and c. The authors should discuss whether the CoSn c-axis result is expected to reflect the in-plane FB. Ideally they should show ρ_ab(T) of CoSn as well.

8. **Robustness of the high-temperature measurements.** Data up to 862 K on Pt3Sn, Pd3Pb and CoSn are new, but the main text gives no information on:
   - the high-T apparatus and atmosphere;
   - possible oxidation, Sn/Pb loss or annealing;
   - warming/cooling hysteresis or thermal cycling;
   - geometric-factor uncertainty;
   - the number of samples measured per compound.

   Because the fit prefactor C scales with absolute ρ, and the claimed effects are curvature changes at the highest temperatures, the authors should show reproducibility on heating and cooling and on at least a second sample.

9. **Dependence on DOS resolution and DFT settings.** The authors themselves attribute the worst fit (CaRh2) to the coarse Materials Project DOS and argue that a finer grid would give "more pronounced sublinear behavior". This shows that the fit outcome depends on how the DOS is broadened. The following settings, and the sensitivity of the fits to them, should be stated:
   - energy grid and smearing;
   - k-mesh;
   - spin-orbit coupling;
   - magnetism (CoSn, Ni3In);
   - any correlation correction (DFT+U and the like, known to shift FBs in these systems).

   For CaRh2, recomputing the DOS on a fine grid would turn an excuse into a test.

C. Minor

10. **Pd3Pb γ.** The value γ ≈ 0.03 mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻² for Pd3Pb is extraordinarily small for a metal containing Pd, about two orders of magnitude below typical intermetallic values. The units, per formula unit or per atom, and the fit range should be checked, and the raw C/T vs T² (Fig. S2) should be described in the main text.

11. **Choice of m_FB values.** Figs. 2–3 use m_FB = 10 m0 and 100 m0, but no value is connected to the real compounds. Stating which regime each material is expected to be in would help.

12. **Missing thermopower test.** The discussion proposes the sign of S as a discriminator but shows no thermopower data. Even a single S(T) curve for CoSn or Ni3In would greatly strengthen the paper (see Suggestions).

13. **Figure and caption details:**
    - Fig. 4(d): r² = 0.991 is reported on a signal of only ~0.2 μΩ cm. State ρ0 and the RRR of the CoSn crystal.
    - Fig. 4(e): explain how the dashed linear reference was chosen.
    - Fig. 3(e) inset: the colour-to-temperature mapping is hard to read.
    - Fig. 2 caption: "E_F − E_edge = −50 and +50 meV" should be tied explicitly to the curves of Fig. 3.

14. **Wording:**
    - "Note that we do not included" should be "do not include".
    - "fits perfectly to what would be expected" should be softened.
    - The relationship to prior work by overlapping authors (Refs. 45–48), which already used energy-dependent scattering to explain ρ(T), should be stated so that the new contribution is clear.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a reference compound without an FB but with a d-band edge (e.g., an isostructural analogue or elemental Pd), analysed in exactly the same way. This is the most direct way to show what is specific to FBs.
2. Turn the method into a quantitative inverse procedure. Apply SM Eq. 6 to all compounds and report fit-quality maps in (E_F − E_edge, m_FB). Compare the results with ARPES-determined FB energies.
3. Measure the Seebeck coefficient S(T) on the same CoSn crystal and a Pd3Pb or Pt3Sn sample. This tests the predicted sign and temperature dependence with the same model parameters.
4. A doping or pressure series in one system (e.g., In/Sn substitution in CoSn, moving E_F toward the FB) would show the predicted crossover from super- to sublinear ρ(T) in a single material family. That would be compelling evidence for "tracking".

5. Verdict: major revision
