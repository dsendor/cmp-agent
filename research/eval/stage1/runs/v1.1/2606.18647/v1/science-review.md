A. Must address

1. The Hall signatures do not single out trions, or a Lorentz force on trions, over neutral excitons plus free carriers. The main text (p. 6) states that the negative Rxy in the hole layer "unambiguously demonstrates the presence of a trion Hall effect and trion drags", and the abstract and Fig. 1c say the Hall effect arises from a Lorentz force on trions. The authors' own Methods model reproduces the signals without trions. The negative-trion result is Rxy ≈ −(B/e)[(1+f)/n_e + f²(1−σX0/σe0)/n_T] with f = σT0/(σT0+σX0); its leading term −(B/e)(1+f)/n_e is a free-electron Hall term, and setting σT = 0 (f = 0: excitons plus free electrons, all holes bound) gives Rxy = −B/(n_e e). Directly from Eq. (1) with the Mo layer open-circuited (J_e = 0), the W-layer resistivity tensor is ρ_h = σX⁻¹ + σe⁻¹ (E_h = E_e + σX⁻¹J_h, E_e = σe⁻¹J_h), so perfect exciton drag alone transfers the electron-layer Hall response into the hole layer. Likewise, with the other layer open-circuited, exciton drag alone gives a nonzero Hall drag with the sign of the excess free carriers in the driven layer (driven excitons drag the bound carriers, the free carriers counterflow and produce a Hall field), so the opposite signs of Rxy(drag) on the two sides of neutrality are also expected without trions. On the positive side the paper itself gives Rxy ≈ B/(n_h e), set only by free holes, and attributes the fall of n_H to "a loss of free holes to the formation of trions and excitons", which exciton formation alone would produce. Neither expression contains the trion cyclotron frequency ω_c,T. As presented, the sign of Rxy and the existence of Rxy(drag) identify the sign of the excess free carriers once minority carriers are bound; the trion assignment rests on the optics (Fig. 2c, device 3) and earlier work, not on the Hall data. The authors should: (a) make the Hall-density comparison quantitative using the gate-calibrated densities already in Fig. 3d (Vb = 0.88 V cut): in the X+X+ region compare n_H with p − n (holes bound only in excitons) versus p − 2n or the trion-plus-exciton expectation (with the trion fraction from RC if possible), and on the X+X− side compare n_e with n − p versus n − 2p. Fig. 3d has p ≈ 1.5–1.8 × 10¹² cm⁻² and n ≈ 1.1–1.5 × 10¹² cm⁻² across the positive window, so the scenarios predict clearly different n_H; currently n_H falls to zero at p ≈ n, as pure exciton binding predicts. On the negative side, at Vg ≈ 0.03 V the measured |n_H| is about 1 × 10¹² cm⁻² while the gate-defined imbalance n − p appears considerably smaller; explaining this mismatch quantitatively would be a discriminating test. (b) Derive Rxy(drag) from Eqs. (1)–(5), which the Methods do not give although it is the most direct trion probe, and compute Rxx, Rxx(drag), Rxy and Rxy(drag) for both the exciton-plus-free-carrier and trion models; compare with Fig. 3b and Fig. 4d. (c) Fit Fig. 3d and Fig. 4b,d with both models, state which measured quantities depend on ω_c,T τ_T, and show that the data constrain it; other candidate discriminators include the magnitude and Vg dependence of Rxy(drag)/Rxx(drag) and the relation between Rxy and Rxy(drag). (d) Explain why the Hall drag is confined to the triangles if excitons plus free carriers would give a similar signal, for example by showing that the drag ratio Rxx(drag)/Rxx is much lower outside them. If the Hall data cannot discriminate, the title claim should be narrowed to a Hall response of a bound-state (exciton/trion) fluid in the doped excitonic insulator, consistent with optically identified trions, and "unambiguously" removed.

2. The Hall data are not shown to be antisymmetric in B, and admixture of the very large longitudinal signals is not excluded. In the trion regions Rxx is 10⁴–10⁶ Ω (Fig. 1e; Extended Data Fig. 7c), higher on the X+X− side (p. 4), and Rxx(drag) reaches about 10⁶ Ω (Fig. 2a), while the reported Hall signals are about 1–15 kΩ (Figs. 3, 4; Extended Data Fig. 6): for example −3 to −5 kΩ for the negative Rxy in Fig. 4b and about −3 kΩ in Fig. 4d. A contact misalignment or inhomogeneity of a fraction of a percent to a few percent (Extended Data Fig. 1 shows irregular, etched geometries) would give a spurious "Hall" signal of the same size. The Methods describe no symmetrization of Rxx or antisymmetrization of Rxy and Rxy(drag), and Fig. 3c covers only B ≥ 0; the near-zero Rxy at low B there is partly reassuring but does not cover the 4 T regime where Rxx is largest. Several features look like possible admixture: the most negative Rxy values sit directly against the grey "completely insulating" mask where Rxx diverges (Fig. 3a; Fig. 4a below about 3 K); the mask boundary carries alternating red/blue speckle near Vb ≈ 0.86 V (Fig. 3a,b; Extended Data Fig. 6); and at 10 K both Rxy(drag) line cuts in Fig. 4d settle at a common finite negative value of about −0.2 to −0.3 kΩ, not expected for a pure Hall-drag signal. At 7.33 Hz, with source impedances near the 100 MΩ preamplifier input impedance and cable capacitance, phase shifts and loading are also possible. The sign change across Vg = 0 argues against pure Rxx admixture but does not fix the magnitudes, on which the quantitative n_H in Fig. 3d directly depends. The authors should show Rxy and Rxy(drag) measured at ±B and antisymmetrized (at least the Vb = 0.88 V cut in Fig. 3a and the temperature sweeps in Fig. 4b,d), show the symmetric part next to Rxx and Rxx(drag), report the quadrature (out-of-phase) component and two-terminal contact resistances in the trion regions, state the quantitative criterion (Rxx threshold, lock-in phase, noise) behind the grey masks in Figs. 1–4 and Extended Data Fig. 6, and check Onsager reciprocity of the drag by swapping drive and sense layers (Rxy(drag)(B) versus R_yx(drag)(−B)) as far as the contacts allow. The electron-Hall claim in particular should be shown to survive antisymmetrization.

3. The Hall results come from a single device. All Rxy and Rxy(drag) data (Figs. 3, 4; Extended Data Figs. 6, 8) are from device 2; the Rxx maps (Fig. 1, Extended Data Fig. 7) are from device 1; the optics and Rxx(drag) at 6 T (Fig. 2, Extended Data Fig. 3) are from device 3, which is not shown in Extended Data Fig. 1; and the device used for Extended Data Figs. 4 and 5 is not stated. The negative Rxy in the hole layer and the sign-changing Hall drag therefore appear in only one device. The authors should show Rxy (ideally also Rxy(drag)) for at least one further device, in particular the electron-Hall sign reversal (device 1 has Hall-bar contacts 1–5), say how many devices were measured in total, and state which device each figure comes from; otherwise state plainly that the result comes from a single device.

4. The conductivity-tensor model in the Methods is incompletely specified and not used quantitatively. Eqs. (1)–(5) are written down but the solution is only quoted; σe0 appears in the final Rxy expression but is never defined; the trion and electron tensors in Eq. (3) use the same sign of ω_c for positive and negative charges, leaving the sign convention unclear; the main text calls f "the ratio of exciton to trion conductivity" while the Methods define f = σT0/(σT0+σX0); the Rxy expression diverges as n_T → 0, obscuring its relation to the exciton-only limit; and it is not stated whether the exciton conductivity is taken to have no Hall component for dipolar excitons in a field. The free-carrier drag σD is dropped as "negligible at low temperatures", yet the same model is used to interpret the 10 K data (Fig. 3d, Fig. 4) where free carriers dominate, and at 4 T the free carriers are Landau-quantized (Extended Data Fig. 8), where Hall drag between layers can be substantial. The authors should define all quantities and sign conventions, give the full inversion of Eqs. (4)–(5) including Rxy(drag) (at least in a supplement), state which terms survive at the measured ω_c τ and the regime of validity, justify neglecting σD at 4 T (for example from the small Rxy(drag) outside the trion regions at the same field), and use the measured Rxx, Rxx(drag), drag ratio (Extended Data Fig. 8) and Rxy(drag) to constrain f and test the model against Figs. 3d and 4b,d (see concern 1).

5. Residual signals at 10 K are called "weak" or negligible but are not. In Fig. 3d the 10 K n_H shows an "anomaly" near Vg ≈ 0–0.005 V reaching about 2.9 × 10¹² cm⁻², roughly double the gate-defined total hole density, so Rxy is well below the free-hole value and some electron-like or drag contribution remains; near Vg ≈ −0.09 V the 10 K n_H also slightly exceeds the total hole density. In Fig. 4d the n-type Rxy(drag) stays at about −0.2 to −0.3 kΩ up to 10 K, while the text says it is "negligible at T ≳ 8K". The statements that trions are fully ionized above about 8 K and that the 10 K curve "follows the expected dependence" should be qualified, and the residuals explained (free-carrier drag, exciton drag persisting above the trion ionization scale, which bears on concern 1, or an offset to be removed by field reversal, see concern 2).

6. Interlayer leakage, AC coupling, linear response and heating are not documented. Drag and single-layer measurements are taken at Vb ≈ 0.84–0.92 V across a 1.5–2 nm hBN barrier, with a 1 mV, 7.33 Hz excitation into channels of 10⁵–10⁷ Ω, read through 100 MΩ preamplifiers. Where the sample resistance dominates, most of the 1 mV drops across the channel, an energy comparable to the 0.7–1.2 meV binding energy and well above k_BT at 1.5 K. In high-impedance regions, small leakage or capacitive pickup at 7.33 Hz can mimic drag. The authors should show the DC interlayer leakage current versus Vb in the relevant windows, report the residual AC coupling after potentiometer balancing and the lock-in phase criteria, show that Rxy, Rxy(drag) and the negative Hall response are linear in (independent of) excitation amplitude in the trion regions, and show that the effective electron temperature is 1.5 K. This matters because the strongest Hall signals lie at the edges of the masked regions.

7. The field dependence does not test Lorentz-force scaling. A Hall effect of charged trions should give Rxy(drag) odd in B and, at low field, roughly linear in B at fixed trion density. Fig. 3c shows Rxy only along one Vb, where the trion region itself appears and disappears with B, so field dependence is mixed with phase-boundary motion. Since a narrow trion region already exists along p = 2n at B = 0 (Fig. 1d), the authors should measure Rxy and Rxy(drag) versus B, including negative B, at low field along the p = 2n line or at a fixed point that stays inside a trion region, to separate the Hall response of a fixed trion fluid from the B-induced change in phase composition.

9. The trion binding-energy estimates rest on approximate, loosely connected conversions. The Methods give E_T,b ≈ 1.2 meV from a 25 mV threshold shift at 6 T, using only the WSe2 hole quantum capacitance C_Q with g_s g_v = 2. The threshold concerns a single valley (the K valley of the W layer), so g_v = 1 seems more appropriate and would roughly double E_T,b to about 2.4 meV; the bilayer-MoSe2 electron quantum capacitance, the gate capacitances and asymmetric hBN thicknesses (which enter the lever arm from Vb to the chemical potential) are left out; ε_r = 3.5 and d = 2 nm are used while the Methods give d = 1.5–2 nm, a roughly 30% spread; and the value is taken at 6 T, where the region is about to vanish. Separately, "≈ 8 K (or 0.7 meV)" equates a crossover temperature with the binding energy, whereas for Saha-type ionization equilibrium the dissociation entropy typically puts the binding energy several times above k_BT*. The two values and the valley-Zeeman scale at 6 T (whose g-factor is not given) are nonetheless called "in good agreement". The authors should give uncertainties for each estimate, justify the degeneracy and lever-arm choices, state which number they consider reliable, give the g-factors and Zeeman energy behind the statement that trions are destabilized above about 6 T, fit the T dependence (for example Rxy(drag)(T) in Fig. 4d, or the trion fraction) with an ionization model, and extract the threshold shift as a function of B from Extended Data Fig. 4 to test whether E_T,b(B) decreases with the Zeeman energy until the region closes near 6 T.

10. The field dependence of the trion region is not explained. At B = 0 trions occupy only a narrow line at p = 2n (Fig. 1d); at 2–6 T this broadens into wide triangles before vanishing above about 6 T (Figs. 1e, 3c; Extended Data Figs. 4, 6). The text explains the disappearance (valley Zeeman energy exceeding the binding energy) but not why a field first enlarges the stability region of spin-singlet trions, which the Zeeman energy should disfavor. Since this field window is where the "trion Hall effect" is seen, the authors should give a quantitative electrostatic or thermodynamic argument for the shape and B-evolution of the triangles, using the valley-split bands of Fig. 2d and the binding energy, and show the B-dependence of the threshold shift in Extended Data Fig. 4.

11. Optical and transport conditions differ. The optics (device 3, Attodry, 1.6 K, broadband white light up to 50 nW/µm²) and the transport (devices 1 and 2, TeslatronPT, 1.5 K) are combined into one phase diagram and one ionization scale (about 8 K from transport, about 10 K from Extended Data Fig. 5). Illumination of highly insulating regions with resistive contacts could cause heating, photodoping or photo-induced shifts of the gate response that move the thresholds defining the trion regions and the 25 mV shift. The authors should estimate optical heating at the stated intensity, check that the thresholds in Fig. 2c do not depend on illumination power, and either show RC on the transport device or show that boundary positions and the ionization temperature match across devices.
