A. Must address

1. The central assignment, that the vacancy-induced in-gap pair (E_e above and E_h below E_F) consists of excitonic pair-breaking states, is not distinguished from ordinary defect levels in a narrow-gap semiconductor or hybridization-gap semimetal that may not be excitonic. A Te vacancy in any insulator with an ~85 meV gap is expected to bind localized donor- or acceptor-like in-gap levels. Every piece of evidence offered for pair breaking also follows from that picture. The energies depend on defect configuration and environment (site-dependent 2δ, Fig. 2d). The peaks shift under tip perturbation (Fig. 2e). They share one spatial envelope, because both may come from the same localized orbital (Fig. 3g,h). The continuum near the defect is suppressed (Fig. 3d), which local band shifts or map normalization could cause (see concern 7). Dimers show bonding/antibonding splitting, as any coupled bound states do (Fig. S10; cf. the authors' own analogy to Mn in GaAs, ref. 36). The states depend on local doping (Fig. 4). A single-particle level can also give two STS features, one on each side of E_F: resonant tunnelling at one polarity and a tip-induced charging/ionization step or negative differential conductance at the other, or two charge-transition levels or orbitals of the same vacancy. The paper does not rule these out. The argument on p. 3 that "coupling-tunable energies ... highlight their origin from the e–h pair-breaking states" applies equally to a conventional defect level. The authors also note (pp. 5–6) that the states show no e–h symmetry, no clear orbital structure and no magnetic/nonmagnetic selectivity, which removes the fingerprints that make YSR identification convincing in superconductors. The Conclusion's statement that the results "in turn confirm that the probed insulating gap at impurity-free region arises from the excitonic pairing" (p. 6) is circular: the subgap states are assigned to pair breaking because the gap is assumed to be excitonic, and are then used to settle a debated question about the gap's origin. The authors should add a dedicated discussion, with data, of why a conventional defect-level picture fails. They should also provide at least one observable that separates the two scenarios, stating what an ordinary defect level would predict for it. Candidates: (i) DFT supercell calculations of each V_Te configuration without excitonic order, showing whether single-particle in-gap levels can or cannot reproduce two peaks at the observed energies and the ordering V_Te2 < V_Te4 < V_Te1 < V_Te3; (ii) temperature tracking of the subgap states well beyond 20 K, towards the excitonic/structural transition, where pair-breaking states should merge into the gap edge as Δ(T)→0 while a defect level would stay at roughly fixed energy relative to the band edges; (iii) a quantitative defect-by-defect correlation, across many defects and regions, between the local gap Δ (or gap edge) and E_e, E_h, E_e + E_h or their asymmetry, as the pair-breaking picture predicts; (iv) a model-specific signature tested across the hundreds of vacancies in Fig. S3, such as the predicted correlation between E_e/E_h weight asymmetry and peak position, or different spatial patterns of E_e and E_h set by the electron and hole band characters; (v) comparable vacancy measurements on a reference compound or region with a similar band structure but no excitonic order (for example Ta2Ni3Te5, ref. 25, or a Se analogue). Without such a test, the title, abstract and conclusion should be narrowed to "subgap states consistent with pair breaking", and the claim that the results "confirm" the excitonic origin of the gap should be removed.

2. Tip-induced band bending (TIBB) and charging are not separated from the claimed "tip electric field" tuning, and the lower-energy subgap state may be a tip-induced charging effect. In Fig. 2e (and Figs. S4, S5) the set-point current is raised from 0.2 to 8 nA at fixed V_b = 0.1 V. This changes the tip–sample distance, tip field, current density and possibly local heating all at once. In Fig. 4e the current reaches 90 nA at 0.1 V (junction resistance ~1 MΩ), and the lower-energy state is "triggered" only above ~25 nA. In a material with an ~85 meV gap, low carrier density and weak screening at 1.6 K, setpoint-dependent defect energies are the textbook signature of TIBB. A charge state switches once the level crosses E_F under the tip, which gives threshold-like appearance of new peaks and charging rings in dI/dV maps. To fit the shift on tip approach, the authors' model needs |J1,2| to *decrease* as the tip approaches, which they call "unexpected" (p. 4) and explain with an ad hoc opposite-sign tip potential. Near-contact tunnelling also raises the possibility of local heating and of tip–sample interaction modifying the surface. The authors should: separate field from distance by varying the set-point bias at fixed current and measuring at constant height with different V_b; show whether the continuum gap edges move with I_t (TIBB would move them) alongside the subgap peaks; map dI/dV(r, V) around a defect over a range of tip heights to look for charging rings; report whether the high-current changes are fully reversible, so that permanent modification of the defect or tip is excluded; and estimate the band bending from the gap, carrier density, dielectric constant and work functions, as is standard in STS of semiconductors. If the gap edges move by a comparable amount, the "continuously tunable impurity scattering" in the abstract reduces to tip gating. The "secondary pair-breaking" assignment of the lower-energy state, a headline claim of the abstract, must then also be reframed.

B. Should address

3. The model parameters do not match the measured gap, are re-chosen for each figure, and are inconsistent with each other, so the theory–experiment comparison is only qualitative. The measured gap is ~85 meV (p. 2; ~100 meV from ARPES), yet Fig. 2f uses Δ0 = 35 meV with μ = 12.25 meV; Fig. 4d uses Δ0 = 45 meV with J1 = −15, J2 = −40 meV for one region and Δ0 = 18 meV with J1 = +20, J2 = −10 meV for the other, so the sign of J1 flips; Fig. 4f uses Δ0 = 30 meV, J1 = −6, J2 = −1.8 meV and a tanh profile with x_c = 80 b0. The model is a two-band singlet EI with none of the multi-band, spin–orbit-coupled, quasi-1D band structure of Ta2Pd3Te5, which has been discussed as a topological/Dirac system (refs. 6, 22). With Δ0, μ, J1 and J2 free in each panel, the model can reproduce two asymmetric in-gap peaks almost regardless of their origin. The authors should: state which parameters are fitted and which are fixed from ARPES/DFT band parameters (Supplementary Sec. II); explain why Δ0 is less than half the measured gap (single-particle gap vs order parameter, band offsets); fit at least one representative defect spectrum (E_e, E_h, intensity ratio, linewidth) with one stated parameter set, with the gap fixed to the measured value, and show that a single set describes Fig. 2c–f for at least one defect type; report the fitted J1,2 for each vacancy type and link them to the 2δ ordering in Fig. 2d; show the model's prediction for a non-excitonic reference (Δ0 = 0, band gap) so readers can see which features require excitonic order; state which features the model predicts that were not used to fix the parameters; and explain physically why the tip should reduce rather than enhance the scattering.

4. The e–h imbalance assignment in Fig. 4, on which the "secondary pair-breaking" claim rests, is unclear, possibly inconsistent and not quantified. Type-I and type-II regions are distinguished only by the energy of a "characteristic normal-state peak" (arrows in Fig. 4b, near +130 meV). That peak is not identified with any band feature, and the size of its shift is not stated. From Fig. 1d the gap runs from about −35 mV to +50 mV, which puts its midpoint above E_F and would suggest hole excess, yet p. 5 states that the gap centre lies below E_F (electron excess). The Fig. 4a caption says the dashed curve outlines regions of "relatively stronger e–h balance", whereas the text and abstract call type-II regions more ("strongly") imbalanced. Local electrostatic doping would also shift a conventional defect level relative to E_F, so the lower-energy states need not reflect a locally suppressed order parameter. The model in Fig. 4f assumes a reduced gap with Δ(x) = Δ0 tanh(x/x_c) and x_c = 80 b0 ≈ 30 nm, a long length scale for defect-induced suppression that is not connected to any measured gap map. The authors should define the gap centre explicitly, identify the reference peak, and give a consistent mapping from spectral features to imbalance, with the caption and text using "balance/imbalance" consistently. They should also show maps of the gap magnitude, gap-centre energy and normal-state peak energy over the area of Fig. 4a, show that the gap is actually reduced in type-II regions, and correlate these defect by defect with the presence and energy of the lower-energy subgap states.

5. The temperature dependence is interpreted in a way that is hard to reconcile with the energy scales. In Fig. 2g the subgap states between 1.6 and 20 K (k_BT ≈ 1.7 meV) are said to decay faster than a Fermi-function convolution of the 1.6 K data predicts, and this is attributed partly to "thermal suppression of excitonic gap" and "quantum pairing poison". The excitonic gap is tens of meV and is reported to persist to hundreds of kelvin (refs. 6, 21; ~100 meV at 20 K from ARPES), so it should be essentially unchanged over this range. A decay on a ~1 meV scale points to a small energy scale instead. Candidates are charging energies, tunnelling rates into a weakly coupled localized level (ref. 32), phonon and lifetime broadening, temperature-dependent tip DOS, lock-in modulation broadening and thermal drift of the setpoint. The authors should: show the gap-edge energies at each temperature; give peak energy, height, linewidth and spectral weight of E_e and E_h against T with error bars; include the lock-in modulation amplitude and the actual tip DOS in the convolution; discuss which mechanism sets the temperature scale; and either extend the measurement to temperatures where Δ is appreciably reduced (100 K or above, if feasible), showing the subgap energies following Δ(T), or tone down the claim. The "far lower surviving temperature" of the lower-energy states (Fig. S12) needs the same treatment and should be quantified in the main text.

6. The spatial "locking" of E_e and E_h is not shown to be specific to an EI. The collapse of the E_e and E_h linecuts onto one curve (Fig. 3g,h) is taken to show that the two states are pair-breaking partners. However, two features from a single localized level (for example a resonance plus its charging/ionization feature, or one orbital probed at two charge levels) would also share one envelope. In superconductors, the distinguishing feature of YSR states is usually the different spatial patterns of the u and v components, so the excitonic model may make a sharper prediction here. The authors should show the model's calculated E_e and E_h spatial profiles (Fig. S8) alongside the data, say whether the model predicts identical or distinct profiles along b and c, discuss whether the different orbital characters of the electron and hole bands of Ta2Pd3Te5 would predict different E_e and E_h patterns, and compare at the atomic scale rather than as broad envelopes.

7. The "spectral-weight transfer" seen in the dI/dV maps may be a setpoint-normalization artefact. The contrast reversal between in-gap maps and the 60 meV continuum map (Fig. 3d–f) is offered as evidence of spectral-weight transfer across the gap, likened to superconductors. Constant-current dI/dV maps are normalized by the integrated LDOS up to the setpoint bias, so a defect with large in-gap LDOS will suppress dI/dV at other energies through normalization alone. The authors should give the map setpoints and show the 60 meV suppression either at constant height or with a setpoint above 60 meV. Otherwise this observation should not be used as evidence for pair breaking.

8. The vacancy identification and the 2δ statistics need more support. Vacancies are assigned to Te1–Te4 sites from decorated-tip images alone (Fig. 2b, Fig. S2). No simulated STM images or formation-energy arguments exclude adatoms, antisites or subsurface defects, and the absence of Te5 vacancies is not explained. The histograms in Fig. 2d overlap strongly; V_Te4 (76 meV) and V_Te2 (69 meV) differ by only 7 meV in 2δ_m. The number of defects per type, the fitted Gaussian widths and the setpoint used are not given, even though 2δ depends on setpoint (Fig. 2e). The type-I/type-II inhomogeneity of Fig. 4, which alone shifts subgap energies by tens of meV, could broaden or bias the histograms. The authors should report N per type with fitted widths and uncertainties on 2δ_m; use a fixed setpoint for all histogram spectra or correct for it; state how the type-I/type-II inhomogeneity was accounted for; test whether the ordering V_Te2 < V_Te4 < V_Te1 < V_Te3 is statistically significant and reproduced across samples and tips; and, since 2δ is claimed to track coupling strength, show whether 2δ correlates with the local gap edge or the local normal-state peak energy (Fig. 4b) for each defect.

11. The magnetic character of the vacancy is not tested. A Te vacancy may carry a local moment, in which case the in-gap states could have Kondo/YSR-like magnetic character. The discussion of impurity sensitivity (pp. 5–6), which argues that nonmagnetic impurities break excitonic pairs regardless of the spin structure of pairing, relies on the vacancy being nonmagnetic. The authors should show spectra of a vacancy in an applied field (Zeeman splitting or shift, or its absence) or justify the nonmagnetic assumption. This would also bear on the spin-singlet versus spin-triplet question the authors raise; if a field-capable system was used, even one field series would help.
