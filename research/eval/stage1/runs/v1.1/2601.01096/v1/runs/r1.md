Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports STM/STS of Te vacancies on the cleaved (100) surface of Ta2Pd3Te5, a candidate excitonic insulator (EI). Each vacancy produces a pair of in-gap resonances (Ee above and Eh below E_F). The authors interpret these as excitonic pair-breaking states, the EI counterpart of Yu-Shiba-Rusinov/impurity states in superconductors, and they report a second, lower-energy pair in more electron-doped regions. If the assignment holds, this would be the first atomic-scale observation of the impurity bound states predicted by Zittartz (1967), and a local probe of excitonic order. The topic is timely; a related study is acknowledged (ref. 41). The difficulty is that in-gap defect states are generic in any insulator, so the novelty depends on showing that these states have properties specific to an excitonic condensate. The present version does not yet show this convincingly.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- High-quality atomic-resolution data. The functionalized (Ta2Pd3Te5-cluster) tip resolves the five Te chains, so individual vacancies can be assigned to specific chains (Figs. 1c, 2a,b).
- A statistical survey of many vacancies with chain-resolved histograms of 2δ (Fig. 2d). This goes beyond single-defect case studies.
- A wide set of perturbations on the same defects: tunnelling current (Fig. 2e, S4, S5), temperature (Fig. 2g), spatial mapping (Fig. 3), dimers (S10, S11) and local inhomogeneity (Fig. 4).
- A two-band mean-field model with intra- and inter-band impurity scattering (J1, J2) that qualitatively reproduces the asymmetric Ee/Eh intensities and their evolution.
- The question, how local depairing relates to global excitonic coherence, is of broad interest to the EI and correlated-matter communities.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The central assignment, that the subgap pair is excitonic pair breaking, is not distinguished from ordinary defect states in a gapped semiconductor or semimetal.
   - What the paper shows: a Te vacancy in any insulator with an ~85 meV gap is expected to create localized in-gap levels (donor- or acceptor-like, possibly several from different orbitals). Their energies depend on the chain the vacancy sits on and on the local electrostatic environment. The observations offered as evidence for pair breaking would all follow from this generic picture:
     - one resonance on each side of E_F;
     - energies that depend on defect configuration (Fig. 2d);
     - shifts under tip perturbation (Fig. 2e);
     - a shared spatial envelope (Figs. 3g,h);
     - bonding/antibonding splitting in dimers (Fig. S10, by the authors' own analogy to Mn in GaAs, ref. 36).
     On p. 3 the authors argue that "coupling-tunable energies ... highlight their origin from the e–h pair-breaking states". That argument applies equally to a conventional defect level.
   - Two features are missing. A single-particle defect level gives one resonance; it can show two in STS through tip-induced charging/ionization, through negative differential conductance, or through two orbitals. The paper does not rule out these mechanisms for the Ee/Eh doublet. Nor does it show a signature unique to an EI bound state. Examples of such signatures:
     - spatial oscillations of Ee and Eh with different phases set by the electron and hole band characters;
     - a quantitative relation between Ee + Eh, or their asymmetry, and the local gap Δ;
     - the evolution of the states across the excitonic transition.
   - The ingredient that is specific to an EI, that the impurity couples electron and hole bands through the condensate, needs a test that a conventional in-gap level would fail. Comparable vacancy measurements on a reference compound with a similar band structure but no excitonic order would also help; Ta2Ni3Te5 (ref. 25) or the Se analogue, if available, are candidates.
   - What to do:
     - Add a dedicated discussion, with data, explaining why a conventional defect-level picture fails.
     - Quantitatively test a prediction that differs between the two pictures. Candidates are the locking of Ee and Eh energies to the local gap edge across many defects and regions, and Ee–Eh relations predicted by the model.
     - Soften the Conclusion's statement that the results "confirm that the probed insulating gap ... arises from the excitonic pairing" unless such evidence is added. The origin of the gap in Ta2Pd3Te5 is still debated, and the impurity data are being used to settle it in a circular way.

2. Tip-induced band bending and charging are not separated from the claimed "tip electric field" tuning, and the lower-energy state may be a tip-induced charging effect.
   - What was measured: in Fig. 2e (and S4, S5) the set-point current is raised from 0.2 to 8 nA at fixed Vb = 0.1 V. This changes tip–sample distance, tip field, current density and possibly local heating all at once. In Fig. 4e the current reaches 90 nA at 0.1 V, i.e. a junction resistance of about 1 MΩ, and the lower-energy subgap state is "triggered" above about 25 nA.
   - The alternative: in a material with an ~85 meV gap and low screening, these are textbook signatures of tip-induced band bending and charging of a localized defect level. A charge state switches once the level crosses E_F under the tip, which gives threshold-like appearance of new peaks and charging rings in dI/dV maps.
   - The current interpretation: the authors rationalize the observed decrease of |J1,2| on tip approach with an ad hoc opposite-sign tip potential (p. 4).
   - What to do:
     - Separate the effect of field from that of distance by varying the set-point bias at fixed current, and by measuring at constant height with different Vb.
     - Map dI/dV(r, V) around a defect over a range of tip heights to look for charging rings.
     - Report whether the high-current changes are fully reversible, so that permanent modification of the defect or tip is excluded.
     - Estimate the band bending at these set points (gap, carrier density, dielectric constant), as is standard in STS of semiconductors.
   - Why it matters: without these steps the "continuous tunability by tip electric field" (abstract) and the "secondary pair breaking" (Fig. 4e) cannot be attributed to the excitonic order parameter.

B. Should address

3. The model parameters are inconsistent with the measured gap and change from figure to figure, so the theory–experiment comparison is only qualitative.
   - The parameters used:
     - Measured gap: 85 meV (p. 2).
     - Fig. 2f: Δ0 = 35 meV and μ = 12.25 meV.
     - Fig. 4d: Δ0 = 45 meV with J1 = −15, J2 = −40 meV for one region, and Δ0 = 18 meV with J1 = +20, J2 = −10 meV for the other. The sign of J1 flips.
     - Fig. 4f: Δ0 = 30 meV, J1 = −6, J2 = −1.8 meV, x_c = 80 b0.
   - The authors should explain how Δ0, μ and J1,2 were chosen: fitted, or fixed from the measured gap and from ARPES/DFT band parameters (Supplementary Sec. II). They should also explain why Δ0 is less than half the measured gap.
   - They should also show at least one quantitative comparison, simulated against measured Ee, Eh, intensity ratio and linewidth, with the gap fixed to the measured value. As it stands, the model has enough freedom to reproduce two asymmetric in-gap peaks almost regardless of their origin.

4. The temperature dependence is interpreted in a way that is hard to reconcile with the energy scales.
   - What the paper says (p. 4, Fig. 2g): the subgap states decay between 1.6 and 20 K faster than Fermi-function convolution predicts, and this is attributed partly to "the thermal suppression of excitonic gap".
   - The problem: the excitonic gap in Ta2Pd3Te5 is reported to persist to temperatures of order hundreds of kelvin (refs. 6, 21). Between 1.6 and 20 K the gap should be essentially unchanged; Fig. 1 quotes ~100 meV at 20 K from ARPES. Fig. S12 also reports that the lower-energy states vanish at an even lower temperature.
   - Why it matters: a decay on a ~1 meV scale points to a small energy scale. Candidates are charging energies, tunnelling rates into a weakly coupled localized level (ref. 32), and phonon broadening. It does not point to a condensate-related scale.
   - What to do:
     - Show the gap-edge energies at each temperature in Fig. 2g.
     - Give the linewidth and spectral weight of Ee and Eh against T, with the instrumental (lock-in modulation) broadening stated.
     - Discuss which mechanism sets this temperature scale.

5. The statistics and assignment of the vacancy types are not given fully in the main text.
   - Fig. 2d gives the most probable 2δ for each type. However, the histograms overlap strongly; V_Te4 and V_Te2 in particular differ by only 7 meV in 2δm. The total number of defects per type, the number of crystals, cleaves and tips, and the widths of the Gaussian fits are not stated in the main text.
   - What to do:
     - Report N for each type and the fitted widths with uncertainties.
     - State whether the ordering V_Te2 < V_Te4 < V_Te1 < V_Te3 is reproduced across samples and tips.
     - Report how the type-I/type-II inhomogeneity (Fig. 4) was accounted for in the histograms; it alone shifts subgap energies by tens of meV and could broaden or bias them.
     - Since the claim is that 2δ tracks the coupling strength, show whether 2δ correlates with the local gap edge or the local normal-state peak energy (Fig. 4b) for each defect.

6. The tip conditions for spectroscopy are not specified.
   - Topographs are taken with both a "normal" and a Ta2Pd3Te5-cluster-decorated tip (Figs. 1c, 2a,b). A tip terminated by a fragment of a gapped material can add its own DOS features to dI/dV.
   - The authors should state which tip was used for each spectrum in Figs. 1–4. They should also show that the subgap peaks and the gap lineshape are reproduced with metallic tips checked on a reference surface (for example, after Au/Cu calibration).

7. The spatial "locking" of Ee and Eh is not shown to be specific to an EI.
   - What the paper shows: the Ee and Eh linecuts collapse onto one curve (Figs. 3g,h), and this is taken to show that the two states are "locked" as pair-breaking partners.
   - Why this is not specific: two features from a single localized level (for example, a resonance plus its charging/ionization feature) would also share one envelope. In superconductors, the distinguishing feature of Shiba states is usually the different spatial pattern of the electron and hole components.
   - What to do: compare the measured maps quantitatively with the model's Ee/Eh spatial distributions (Fig. S8). Discuss whether the different orbital characters of the electron and hole bands in Ta2Pd3Te5 would predict different Ee and Eh patterns. Show this at the atomic scale rather than as broad envelopes.

8. The type-I/type-II assignment of e–h imbalance rests on one spectral feature and needs support.
   - What the paper does: the doping level is inferred from the energy of a "characteristic normal-state peak" (Fig. 4b, black and red arrows near +130 meV), and the lower-energy subgap states are ascribed to a locally suppressed order parameter.
   - The alternative: local electrostatic doping would also shift a conventional defect level relative to E_F.
   - What to do:
     - Show maps of the gap magnitude and of the gap-centre energy over the area in Fig. 4a, and their correlation with the subgap energies at many defects.
     - Show that the gap is actually reduced in type-II regions, as the model in Fig. 4f assumes.

9. The dimer analysis rests on a few examples.
   - The interacting dimer (1.3 nm, Fig. S10) and the non-interacting dimer (~2 nm, Fig. S11) appear to be single cases.
   - The authors should state how many dimers of each separation and orientation were measured, and whether the splitting scales with separation and direction (along b versus c), as the anisotropic decay lengths in Fig. 3 (0.6 vs 2.5 nm) would predict.

10. A magnetic-field check is missing.
   - A Te vacancy may carry a local moment, in which case the in-gap states could have a Kondo/YSR-like magnetic character. The discussion of "impurity sensitivity" (pp. 5–6) relies on the vacancy being non-magnetic.
   - The authors should either show spectra of a vacancy in an applied field (Zeeman splitting or its absence), or justify the non-magnetic assumption.

C. Minor

11. The caption of Fig. 4a says the dashed curve outlines "regions exhibiting relatively stronger e–h balance". The text defines type-II as more imbalanced, and the abstract refers to "strongly electron–hole imbalanced" regions. Please make clear which region is outlined and use "balance/imbalance" consistently.

12. The gap estimate on p. 2 (Δ = 85 meV from a −35 meV onset and a ~50 meV peak) mixes an onset with a peak position and is called "under-estimated". Please define the gap criterion, and give the gap-centre offset from E_F, since this offset underpins the e–h imbalance argument.

13. Fig. 2e and Fig. 4e: say how the spectra were normalized ("normalized impurity spectra") and give the lock-in modulation amplitude, which sets the resolution of the linewidths discussed.

14. Fig. 3: the decay widths "0.6 nm vs. 2.5 nm" should be defined (FWHM, exponential length?) and given with uncertainties. The quoted widths should also be checked against the axis ranges in Figs. 3g,h.

15. p. 5: "crystal field splitting ... typically ~100 times smaller than the energy of excitonic gap" needs a reference or an estimate. A ~1 meV crystal-field splitting is unusual for a Te/Ta/Pd compound.

16. Ref. 41 (Kwon et al.) addresses the same physics. A short comparison of the findings, whether consistent or different, would help readers.

17. Language: "predicated" should be "predicted" (p. 5), and "formulism" should be "formalism" (p. 6). A few other grammatical slips need editing.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add one decisive discriminator between EI pair breaking and conventional defect levels. Options are quantitative scaling of Ee and Eh with the locally measured gap across many defects and regions, a reference compound without excitonic order, or a model prediction fixed by independent band parameters and then tested against data.
2. Run a systematic tip-height and set-point-bias study, with charging-ring mapping, to quantify tip-induced band bending, and present it alongside Fig. 2e and Fig. 4e.
3. Fix Δ0 in the model to the measured gap, and use band parameters from ARPES/DFT. Then report the fitted J1,2 for each vacancy type, linking them to the 2δ ordering in Fig. 2d.
4. Extend the temperature dependence to higher temperatures, ideally including measurements of the gap itself. This would connect the impurity states to the excitonic order parameter rather than to low-energy broadening.

5. Verdict

Verdict: major revision
