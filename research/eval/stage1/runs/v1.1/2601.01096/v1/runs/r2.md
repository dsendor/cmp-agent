Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports STM/STS of surface Te vacancies in the candidate excitonic insulator Ta2Pd3Te5. Each vacancy produces a pair of in-gap peaks (E_e above and E_h below the Fermi level). Their separation 2δ depends on the vacancy site, the peaks shift with tunnelling current, their spatial profiles are "locked", and in strongly electron–hole-imbalanced regions a second, lower-energy pair appears. Read with a two-band mean-field model, the authors interpret these as impurity-induced excitonic pair-breaking states, the analogue of Yu–Shiba–Rusinov states. Local spectroscopy of pair breaking in an excitonic condensate would be new and of broad interest, since impurity states have not been resolved in excitonic insulators before. However, the central assignment currently rests on features that conventional defect levels in a narrow-gap semiconductor would also show, and that needs to be addressed.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- High-quality atomic-resolution data with site-resolved assignment of vacancies (V_Te1–V_Te4). Statistics of 2δ over many defects are shown (Fig. 2d, Fig. S3).
- A rich set of observations on one system: tip-current tuning, temperature dependence, spatial mapping with the spectral-weight reversal between in-gap and continuum maps (Fig. 3d–f), dimer hybridisation (Fig. S10/S11), and dependence on the local electron–hole imbalance (Fig. 4).
- A minimal model (intra-band J1, inter-band J2 scattering in a two-band excitonic insulator) qualitatively reproduces the asymmetric peak pair and its evolution. This gives a concrete framework that others can test.
- The topic is timely: Ta2Pd3Te5 is a leading excitonic-insulator candidate, and a related study has appeared independently (ref. 41).

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. No test separates excitonic pair breaking from ordinary defect levels in a (possibly non-excitonic) small-gap semiconductor. A Te vacancy in any narrow-gap insulator generally creates localized in-gap levels. In STS these routinely appear as a pair of features: an empty-state and a filled-state resonance, or two charge-transition levels of the same defect orbital. They shift with tip–sample distance through tip-induced band bending, sit on the defect at a fixed spatial profile, hybridise in dimers, and depend on the local doping. Each piece of evidence offered for pair breaking also fits this conventional picture:
   - site-dependent 2δ (Fig. 2d);
   - shift with I_t (Fig. 2e);
   - identical E_e/E_h line profiles (Fig. 3g,h);
   - bonding/antibonding splitting in dimers (Fig. S10);
   - dependence on local doping (Fig. 4).

   The Conclusion goes further and states that the results "confirm that the probed insulating gap at impurity-free region arises from the excitonic pairing". That argument is circular: the subgap states are assigned to pair breaking because the gap is assumed to be excitonic. The authors should provide at least one discriminating observation. Options:
   - (i) Follow the subgap states with temperature towards the excitonic/structural transition of Ta2Pd3Te5, or at least well beyond 20 K. Pair-breaking states should merge into the gap edge and vanish as Δ(T)→0. A defect level would instead stay roughly at fixed energy relative to the band edges.
   - (ii) Use a quantitative signature specific to the excitonic model, such as the predicted correlation between E_e/E_h weight asymmetry and peak position, tested across the ~hundreds of vacancies in Fig. S3 rather than on one defect.
   - (iii) Make a reference comparison with an isostructural compound or region where the gap is believed to be non-excitonic.
   - (iv) Include DFT supercell calculations of the Te-vacancy levels without excitonic order, showing they cannot produce two peaks at the observed energies.

   Without one of these, the title and abstract claims should be scaled back to "subgap states consistent with pair breaking".

2. The I_t dependence is not separated from tip-induced band bending or charging, yet it is central to the argument (Fig. 2e, Fig. 4e). As I_t rises from 0.2 to 8 nA (and up to 90 nA in Fig. 4e) at fixed V_b = 0.1 V, the tip approaches the surface. In a gapped, low-carrier-density material, the band bending and the electric field at the defect then change strongly. This shifts defect levels and can switch their charge state; the "triggering" of the lower-energy state above ≳25 nA (Fig. 4e) is a typical ionisation-ring/charging signature. The authors invoke a tip potential that reduces |J1,2| and call it "unexpected" (p. 4). However, they do not measure the field independently or rule out the electrostatic mechanism. They should:
   - vary the set-point bias at fixed current, and vice versa, to separate field effects from current effects;
   - estimate the tip-induced band bending, for example from the shift of the continuum gap edges with I_t, which should be shown alongside the subgap peaks;
   - check for ring-like features in dI/dV maps around defects at high set-point;
   - show that the gap edges stay fixed while the subgap peaks move.

   If the gap edges move by a comparable amount, the "continuously tunable impurity scattering" in the abstract reduces to tip gating.

B. Should address

3. The model parameters do not match the measured gap, and they are re-chosen for each figure. The measured gap is quoted as Δ ≈ 85 meV (p. 2), yet Fig. 2f uses Δ0 = 35 meV, Fig. 4d uses Δ0 = 45 and 18 meV, and Fig. 4f uses Δ0 = 30 meV with a tanh profile (x_c = 80 b0). J1 and J2 are also freely adjusted in each panel. With so many free parameters, the agreement shown is qualitative only. The authors should:
   - explain how Δ0 relates to the measured 85 meV gap (single-particle gap vs order parameter, band offsets);
   - fit one representative defect spectrum with a stated set of parameters;
   - state which features the model predicts that were not used to fix the parameters.

   They should also explain physically why the tip should reduce rather than enhance the scattering, instead of only noting that this is unexpected.

4. The temperature-dependence argument is weak. In Fig. 2g, spectra are compared only up to 20 K, against a Fermi-function convolution of the 1.6 K data. The claim that the states "decay faster" than thermal broadening, and the link to "quantum pairing poison", are not quantified. In particular:
   - The comparison should include the lock-in modulation and the actual tip DOS.
   - It should show peak height, width and energy against T with error bars.
   - It should state whether the gap edge itself changes between 1.6 and 20 K. A ~100 K-scale gap should be essentially unchanged there, which makes it hard to attribute the faster decay to "thermal suppression of excitonic gap".

   Fig. S12, cited for the lower surviving temperature of the lower-energy states, needs the same treatment.

5. The direction of electron–hole imbalance in Fig. 4 is unclear and possibly inconsistent. From Fig. 1d, the gap runs from about −35 mV to about +50 mV, which puts its midpoint above E_F and would suggest hole excess. Yet p. 5 states that the gap centre lies below E_F (electron excess). The type-I/type-II assignment rests on the energy of a "characteristic normal-state peak" (arrows, Fig. 4b) that is not identified with any band feature. Also, the Fig. 4a caption says the dashed curve outlines regions of "relatively stronger e–h balance", while the text calls type-II regions more imbalanced. The authors should define the gap centre explicitly, identify the reference peak, and give a consistent mapping from spectral features to imbalance. Since the "secondary pair-breaking" claim (abstract) rests on this mapping, it matters.

6. Vacancy identification and statistics need support. The defects are assigned as surface Te vacancies on Te1–Te4 purely from decorated-tip imaging (Fig. 2b, Fig. S2). There is no simulated STM image or formation-energy argument to exclude adatoms, antisites or subsurface defects. For Fig. 2d, the authors should give:
   - the number of defects of each type;
   - how many samples, cleaves and tips they came from;
   - whether the same 2δ ordering holds for each tip.

   The overlap between the V_Te4 and V_Te2 distributions is large, so the claim of "statistically distinct" ranges should be supported by a test or by the fitted widths.

7. The spatial "locking" of E_e and E_h (Fig. 3g,h) is presented as specific to excitonic pair breaking, but it is not specific. A single defect orbital probed at its two charge levels, or particle and hole components with similar envelope, would give the same profile. In superconductors the u and v components of YSR states generally have different spatial profiles, so the excitonic model may make a sharper prediction here. The authors should show the calculated E_e and E_h profiles from their model (Fig. S8) alongside the data and say whether the model predicts identical or distinct profiles along b and c.

8. Single-defect demonstrations. The I_t series (Fig. 2e, Fig. S4/S5), the temperature series (Fig. 2g) and the maps (Fig. 3) are shown mainly for V_Te2. The paper should state how many defects of each type show the same trends, and should show the temperature dependence for at least one other vacancy type.

C. Minor

9. The gap value: Δ = 85 meV is called "under-estimated" relative to ARPES (~100 meV at 20 K), but the hole-side onset at −35 meV is set by eye. State the criterion (for example, a noise threshold on dI/dV) and the uncertainty.
10. Fig. 2e and Fig. 4e: give the V_b set-point and the I_t value for each trace, and convert I_t to a relative tip-height change Δz so readers can judge how far the tip moved.
11. Fig. 3g: the x-axis spans only ±0.4 nm, while the text quotes a 0.6 nm width; Fig. 3h spans ±1.7 nm for a quoted 2.5 nm. Define how the widths are extracted (FWHM of fitted profile?).
12. The discussion of orbital character (p. 5, "crystal field splitting… ~100 times smaller than the energy of excitonic gap") needs a reference or an estimate. As written it is not plausible for a Te p / Pd d system.
13. Supplementary Sec. II should state whether the model includes spin–orbit coupling and whether the order parameter is solved self-consistently around the impurity. Fig. 4f suggests not; if so, say so.
14. Relation to ref. 41 (Kwon et al.): briefly state the points of agreement or disagreement.
15. Language: "predicated" → "predicted" (p. 5); "splitted" → "split"; "tends to emerge" → "tend to emerge"; "formulism" → "formalism".

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Add a decisive control that separates pair breaking from conventional defect levels. Temperature dependence through the gap-closing temperature is the most direct; DFT vacancy levels without excitonic order are a complementary option.
- Report the gap edges together with the subgap peaks in every I_t and T series, and add a bias-set-point series to rule out tip-induced band bending.
- Make the theory quantitative: tie Δ0 to the measured gap, fit one spectrum, and test a parameter-free prediction (asymmetry vs energy, or E_e/E_h spatial profiles) across the defect ensemble.
- Tone down the Conclusion's statement that the results "confirm" the excitonic origin of the gap, unless the added controls support it.

5. Verdict

Verdict: major revision
