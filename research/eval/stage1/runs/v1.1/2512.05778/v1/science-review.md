A. Must address

1. **No laser-free control shows that the Raman memory is thermal, and the quench rate after a power step is undefined.** All Raman cycling (Figs. 1, 3) uses the laser as heater: the power steps from 10 mW to P(T_T) and back, described in Methods as "almost instantaneous heating and subsequent freezing". This raises three issues. (i) Manganites, LPCMO in particular, respond to light non-thermally; the authors themselves cite photo-induced phase changes (Deng2024, Lin2018), and photo-induced melting of charge order is well documented. (ii) The cooling rate after the power step is unknown but presumably far faster than the 5 K/min used for the M and ρ cycles, and in the dynamic phase-separation (DPS) regime of LPCMO the phase fractions depend strongly on cooling rate (kinetic arrest, glassy relaxation), so the Raman "quench" and the PPMS cycles are different thermal histories. (iii) Figs. S4 and S5 themselves show systematic differences between laser and cold-finger heating: mode areas in Fig. S5d–f peak at about 7.5 (temperature) against about 4.5 (power); the rotational FWHM differs above T_C; frequencies differ above T_COO; and the S-JT position under power heating departs from the cold-finger curve between T_C and T_COO, exactly the window of interest. Either the laser-heated spot is not equivalent to a uniformly heated sample, or the laser does more than heat. The direct test is cheap and decisive, since the standard-heating data of Fig. 2 already use this mode: repeat the Fig. 3 protocol with the cold-finger heater cycling between 38 K and the same T_T values at fixed low laser power (10 mW), ideally also at the T_B, T_T grid and ramp rate of Fig. 4, and record Raman at T_B. If Fig. 3d,e is reproduced, the effect is thermal; if not, it is laser-specific and the interpretation and the "laser-assisted phase engineering" framing must change. The authors should also report the cooling rate or time constant after a power step.

2. **The laser-heating temperature scale is calibrated with the observable under study, with no independent thermometer, and its uncertainty is understated.** Every T_T in Figs. 1 and 3, and hence the placement of the memory window relative to T_C, T_MI and T_COO (the central claim), comes from Table S1, built by "visual inspection and comparison of spectra" between cold-finger and laser-power series in coarse 25 K steps. The comparison used the low-frequency (Bose-factor) region and the widths and intensities of the ~250, ~480 and ~614 cm⁻¹ structures, i.e. the very JT intensities and widths that the paper then shows to be history dependent; the reference curve (Fig. 2) is itself one particular thermal history in a material with large thermal hysteresis, so a spectrum that "looks like" 100 K may be a different phase mixture at another temperature. Agreement between the Fig. 3 windows and the transition temperatures is therefore partly built in. In addition: linearity is assumed and the intercept fixed to zero (footnote, p. 12) although the free fit gives −3(5) K; a 21.8 K self-heating offset at 10 mW is added by extrapolation; the quoted ±4–8 K uncertainties reflect only linear-fit statistics, not the visual matching or background differences the authors acknowledge (p. 12); and the radial and depth gradients inside a 50 µm Gaussian spot at up to 150 mW are ignored, even though the optical penetration depth is far smaller than the spot, the cold-finger contact goes through silver paint, and the thermal conductivity of LPCMO changes across T_C. The spectra extend to negative Raman shift (Fig. S2), so the authors should use the Stokes/anti-Stokes intensity ratio of the 250 or 480 cm⁻¹ mode as an independent local thermometer at each target power, give realistic uncertainties on T_T (or state that the window edges are uncertain at the ~10–20 K level), and discuss how a temperature distribution within the probed volume affects the identification of the window T_C < T_T < T_COO.

3. **The claimed laser-induced, non-volatile resistive switching is not measured.** The Discussion and Conclusions state that the study "demonstrates the potential of laser-induced thermal cycling to induce non-volatile structure/resistive-switching", "introduces a novel method for controlling phase separation and resistive properties through localized laser heating", and that "for the first time" resistive switching is "associated with the local structure". But resistivity was never measured during or after a laser cycle: it was measured with two probes on a whole pellet (a different piece and surface from the Raman sample) cycled in a PPMS at 5 K/min, with T_B = 30 K rather than 38 K and a different T_T grid. The Raman spot (50 µm, near-surface) and the transport path (~0.7 mm between probes, bulk percolation) probe different volumes. "Non-volatile" is also untested: there is no retention measurement of ρ, M or Raman intensity at T_B over time, although LPCMO in the DPS regime is known for strong time relaxation at low temperature and only a 5-minute wait (Raman) or a 5 K/min ramp (M, ρ) is used. The authors should either measure resistance across a laser-cycled region (for example a thin film, narrow bar or device geometry whose cross-section is dominated by the illuminated volume), together with M(t), ρ(t) and Raman at T_B over hours after selected cycles (for example T_T = 100, 150 and 210 K), or remove the laser-switching and "non-volatile" claims from the abstract, Discussion and Conclusions and describe the Raman (optical) and transport (thermal) results as parallel observations under different protocols.

4. **Reproducibility of the Raman memory is not shown.** Fig. 3 appears to be a single cumulative sequence on one spot. Methods states that "different spots and pieces of the sample were measured with the same protocol", but none of those data are shown, and the error bars in Fig. 3 are fit errors only. Several features carry the interpretation and rest on one or two points: the AS-JT FWHM jump from ~48 to ~87 cm⁻¹ (Fig. 3b), the AS-JT ΔI/I_i spike to ~1.1 at T_T ≈ 100 K, and the negative values / sign change of ΔI/I_i between ~120 and ~200 K (Fig. 3d). Without repeats these could be spot-to-spot or time-dependent fluctuations. The authors should show the full sequence on at least two further spots or pieces with the spread (for example as overlaid curves in Fig. 3c–e), repeat at least one T_T in the T_C–T_MI window to show the cycle-to-cycle response is reproducible, and repeat the whole sequence a second time on the same spot to show the memory is a repeatable property of the material. The same applies to Fig. 4: state whether the result is reproduced on a second pellet or in a second run after cycling to room temperature.

B. Should address

5. **The cumulative protocol mixes dependence on T_T with accumulated history and aging.** T_T only increases (Fig. 1b, Table S1), so each cycle starts from the state left by the previous, lower T_T, and the I_0-referenced change (Fig. 3e) convolves the current T_T with all earlier excursions. The time spent at T_B also grows through the sequence, and the DPS regime relaxes with time. To support a picture in which the state encodes the maximum temperature reached, the authors should show non-cumulative single cycles from a freshly as-cooled state (warmed above T_COO or to room temperature, cooled to T_B) to representative T_T in each window and compare them with the cumulative result; repeated cycles to the same T_T to test whether the change saturates or keeps accumulating; and a time series at T_B without cycling over the duration of the full sequence.

6. **Laser-induced surface modification is not excluded.** Powers up to 150 mW on a 50 µm spot (roughly several kW/cm²) are applied repeatedly to the same spot, and manganites are known to lose oxygen and decompose under such illumination. The strongest Raman line of the decomposition product Mn3O4 lies near 655–660 cm⁻¹, close to the "spurious peak" (~660–670 cm⁻¹) marked in red in Fig. 2a and prominent near 200 K in Fig. 2b, which the authors attribute "possibly" to oxygen vacancies without evidence; it also lies close to the S-JT band. Fig. S6f (150 mW) shows a strongly changed background. The reset above T_COO (Figs. 1h, S6i) argues against gross damage but not against partial modification. The authors should explain how this peak was handled in the fits, track its intensity at T_B after every cycle, show optical images of the spot before and after the sequence, and show that a fresh cryostat cooldown from 300 K after the full sequence restores the as-cooled spectrum of Fig. 1c, including the ~660 cm⁻¹ feature.

7. **The extracted intensities depend on the background model, and the normalization uses a mode that also changes.** All spectra were fitted with Lorentzians on a "fixed predefined 7th degree polynomial" background, with fit quality judged visually (Fig. S6). The JT modes are broad (FWHM 50–120 cm⁻¹, Figs. 2c, 3b) and overlap, so their areas are very sensitive to the background, and the electronic/polaronic continuum visibly changes between cycles (Fig. 1e,f differ above ~700 cm⁻¹), so the JT intensities could partly track the continuum. Intensities are normalized to the ~250 cm⁻¹ rotational mode, yet its area changes by a factor of ~4 across the same temperature range (Fig. S5d), its width changes strongly, its frequency shifts at T_C (Fig. S4a), and whether it stays constant at T_B across cycles is not shown. The authors should state how the error bars in Figs. 2c,d and 3b–e were obtained, show un-normalized JT and rotational-mode areas and the background level at T_B versus T_T, and test how robust ΔI/I₀ is to the background (lower-order polynomial, fit restricted to 200–750 cm⁻¹, or a physically motivated electronic-continuum shape).

8. **JT intensity is assumed, not shown, to measure the AFMI-COO phase fraction, and Fig. 5 has no quantitative input.** The Discussion and Fig. 5 read the JT intensity at T_B as the amount of charge/orbitally ordered phase. JT-mode intensity also reflects correlated-polaron short-range order, electronic screening and reduced probed volume in metallic FMM regions (so a change in near-surface FMM connectivity could change the intensity without changing the COO fraction), and resonance conditions at 532 nm that change with the electronic state. In Fig. 2d the JT intensity peaks near T_MI (~150 K), where correlated polarons are strongest, not where the COO fraction is largest. Fig. 5 is described as "semi-quantitative" or "constructed" but no quantity enters it. The authors should justify the mapping, report mode frequencies at T_B versus T_T, and estimate COO/FMM fractions (for example from ΔM at T_B using M at small DC field compared with the saturated moment, or from ρ), plotting them on one T_T axis with the resistivity to test the percolation picture; otherwise present Fig. 5 and the "phase fraction" language as hypothesis and state explicitly that Fig. 5 is a schematic.

9. **The Raman and bulk results come from mismatched protocols, so their "clear agreement" is only qualitative.** Raman: T_B = 38 K, near-instantaneous laser quenches, T_T on the calibrated grid 59–317 K, local 50 µm probe. M and ρ: T_B = 30 K, 5 K/min ramps, T_T = 50, 60, …, 250 K, whole pellet. Cooling rate strongly changes the FMM fraction in LPCMO, and in Fig. S8 the transition temperatures differ by ~30 K between cooling and warming. The detailed shapes also differ: Raman ΔI/I_i peaks at ~100 K and is negative between ~150 and 200 K, whereas M shows sharp features near 90 K and 200–210 K, with a large positive jump at T_T ≥ 210 K (Fig. 4c,d) that has no Raman counterpart; ρ gains about two-fold between T_C and T_COO while M changes by a factor of ~0.5. The authors should overlay Raman ΔI/I_0 and M, ρ ΔF/F_0 on a common T_T axis with the matched windows marked, discuss where they disagree, state whether the same pellet was used for both, and ideally match the protocols (which the cold-finger Raman control of item 1 would provide).

10. **The magnetic and transport measurements do not directly measure phase fractions.** "Magnetization" was measured in ACMS mode with a 10 Oe, 11 Hz AC field and no DC field (Methods), yet Fig. 4a reports it in µB/Mn (~10⁻² µB/Mn). The authors should state whether this is the AC moment, the real part of χ_ac or something else, how the PPMS remanent field was handled, and relabel it as AC susceptibility if appropriate; this quantity is governed by domain dynamics, demagnetization and remanent field, which could show cycle dependence unrelated to phase fractions. Resistivity is two-probe with silver paint and gold wires on a polycrystalline pellet at 0.1 µA; in the insulating regime (up to ~10⁵ Ω mm) contact and grain-boundary resistance can dominate and can themselves be history dependent. Before attributing the threefold change in ρ (Fig. 4d) to bulk phase reconfiguration, the authors should provide four-probe resistivity for at least one cycle (or at least the sample geometry and a contact-resistance check) and DC M(T) at a moderate field.

11. **The novelty needs positioning against established thermal-history memory in manganites, and the overclaiming wording should be moderated.** History-dependent states, return-point memory, minor hysteresis loops, cooling-rate dependence, glassy relaxation and kinetically arrested FMM/COO coexistence in LPCMO and related compounds are established, both in cited works (Ghivelder2005, Sacanell2004/2018, Sharma/Sarma2004, Kundhikanjana2015) and in uncited work on kinetic arrest and cooling and heating in unequal fields (CHUF protocols). A warming excursion into T_C < T_T < T_COO converting FMM to COO, which then stays arrested on fast recooling, appears to account for the observations. The M and ρ results of Fig. 4 should be compared explicitly with these works, and the paper should state precisely what the structural (JT-mode) probe adds, for example whether the AS- and S-JT modes respond differently, as Fig. 3b suggests. Phrases such as "previously unidentified regime of structural irreversibility", "a new form of nonequilibrium phase dynamics", "for the first time, it is demonstrated…", and the neuromorphic/quantum-device framing in the abstract and conclusions should be justified or toned down to match a single-sample, bulk, cryogenic study.
