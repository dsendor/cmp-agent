A. Must address

1. Strain, deposition damage, chemical reaction or Au interdiffusion are not ruled out as causes of the Au-specific suppression and broadening, so the attribution to screening and charge transfer is not established. The abstract and Discussion (pp. 13–15, Fig. 5c) attribute the lowered, broadened transition on Au to electrostatic screening and charge transfer that create a Tc gradient perpendicular to the layers. They reach this by elimination: the effect is absent on insulating Al2O3, and the sharp 4L/Al2O3 transition "serves as a control to exclude a degradation effect" (p. 15), leaving metallicity as the only difference. But the two interfaces differ in more than conductivity. (i) Formation: on Au, the metal is thermally evaporated directly onto the freshly cleaved, rippled, Se-terminated Ta2NiSe5 surface (Methods, p. 17). Hot Au adatoms can intermix, form Au–Se bonds, create Se vacancies or roughen the interface, and the text itself says Au "conforms better to the surface ripples and binds more strongly" (p. 4). On Al2O3, the first layer is ALD Al2O3 grown with TMA/H2O at 100–150 °C, and Au is evaporated only afterwards, so no metal ever touches that flake. The Al2O3 sample therefore controls only for oxidation, not for metal-deposition damage. (ii) Strain: Tc in Ta2NiSe5 is very sensitive to uniaxial strain and pressure (refs. 7, 14–17, including the authors' own Ref. 16). A conformal amorphous Au film bonded to the ripples, with a thermal expansion different from Al2O3, will impose different and possibly depth- or laterally-inhomogeneous thermal-mismatch strain on cooling from 400 K. That alone would lower and broaden the transition within the ~1 µm laser spot. (iii) Optics: the optical environments differ (see concern 3). The thickness dependence (broadening in 6L and 4L, none in 18L), which is the evidence for a vertical effect, would also follow from a strained or damaged layer near the interface. The authors should do one or more of: (a) compare high-temperature orthorhombic (e.g. 295 K and 340–400 K) frequencies and linewidths of the Ag(o1)–Ag(o8) modes, and the Ag(m4)/Ag(m5) frequencies at 100 K, across all five flakes and against bulk, to bound strain and disorder (strain and doping shift the Ag(o) modes differently); (b) estimate the thermal-mismatch strain and compare it with the known dTc/dε; (c) measure a flake with an ultrathin insulating spacer (about 1–2 nm Al2O3 or hBN) between Ta2NiSe5 and Au made by the same evaporation/cold-welding route, which keeps the deposition, strain and optical environment largely unchanged but blocks charge transfer (a spacer of variable thickness would also measure the interface decay length directly); (d) measure a flake placed on a pre-formed metal surface without evaporation onto Ta2NiSe5, such as the Au(111)/mica ribbons of Fig. 1b or a dry-stamped flake on Au; (e) check for Au–Se reaction by XPS or cross-sectional TEM, if feasible. Without such evidence, the effect should be described as Au-specific, with screening and charge transfer, the "Tc gradient perpendicular to the layers" in the abstract, and everything built on that attribution in the Discussion presented as one hypothesis alongside strain and interface damage.

B. Should address

2. Each condition rests on a single flake. Five flakes are measured, one per thickness/substrate combination (p. 7), with no repeat flake, no spot-to-spot variation within a flake, and no remeasurement after thermal cycling. The central comparison (4L/Al2O3 sharp at ~280–287 K against 4L/Au at 220±40 K, ending near 180 K) and the Fig. 5 phase diagram therefore rest on individual flakes, and flake-to-flake variation (local thickness errors, partial adhesion, wrinkles, local damage) could produce differences attributed to the substrate. The 6L/Au flake partly replicates the Au result, but the ~40 K reduction for 4L/Al2O3 relative to the bulk-like 7L/Al2O3 has no replicate, and the 309 K reported for 8L on SiO2 (ref. 45) suggests sizable sample-to-sample spread. Since the authors say flakes can be "regularly" isolated (p. 4), they should add at least one more 4L (or 3–6L) flake on each substrate, even if only the Ag(m5) onset is measured over a reduced temperature set; state how many flakes were screened or measured and whether any were excluded; and state whether any flake was remeasured after heating to ~400 K and whether Tc or the line shapes changed. A comment on whether ALD at 100–150 °C with H2O could alter the Al2O3 interface would also help.

3. The depth weighting of the Raman signal near a metal is not considered, yet the layer-resolved interpretation depends on it. At a good metal surface the tangential optical field nearly vanishes, so with in-plane polarizations at 632.8 nm the Ta2NiSe5 layer touching Au contributes least to the ac signal. The overall intensity drop (×25 and ×50 scaling for 6L and 4L on Au, against ×3 for 18L; Figs. 3b, e and 4) is consistent with this. The Al2O3 flakes sit on 50–100 nm Al2O3 over Au, an interference stack with a different weighting. The claims that interfacial layers are "detectable in the overall signal" (p. 13), that the effect is confined to "one or two layers" (pp. 13–14), and the ~150 K local suppression assigned to them (p. 13, Fig. 5b inset) all depend on how much the bottom layers are weighted. It is also hard to reconcile a 1–2 layer effect with the nearly identical ±40 K broadening of 6L and 4L on Au (only a ~30 K shift), since the effect should be diluted in 6L. The authors should compute the layer-resolved Raman efficiency by a transfer-matrix calculation for 4L and 6L in both stacks (Ta2NiSe5/Au/Cr and Ta2NiSe5/Al2O3/Au), fold it into the Supplementary Note 4 toy model, and show whether a strongly down-weighted bottom layer can still produce the observed broadening. If not, the ~150 K local suppression would need revising (likely upward) or the suppression must extend further into the flake.

4. Lateral inhomogeneity within the laser spot has not been ruled out. The gradual onset on Au could arise from in-plane variation of Tc, caused by roughness of the amorphous evaporated Au, wrinkles, bubbles, varying contact quality or inhomogeneous strain, rather than from a vertical gradient. The main text relies on the Supplementary Note 4 toy model for the vertical picture. Raman maps across the 4L/Au flake (and ideally 6L/Au) at one or two temperatures inside the broadened window, for example of the Ag(m5) frequency and intensity or the Ag(m5)/background ratio at 220 K and at 100 K, would test this. If the response is uniform at the spot scale, the vertical picture becomes much more compelling.

5. The Tc^avg and width extraction for the Au flakes is heuristic, and the 4L/Au structural and electronic values are not independent. The ±40 K width comes from assuming that the second, lower kink of Ag(m4) sits a fixed 60 K below Tc, as in the sharp flakes, and reading the excess separation (~100 K for 6L and 4L/Au) as broadening (pp. 9–10). The origin of the second kink is not discussed, and its separation from Tc could itself depend on strain, doping or a separate low-temperature crossover. The peak positions come from local maxima of a smoothed, interpolated second derivative with 10–15 K Gaussian smoothing in temperature (Fig. 3k–o, Methods p. 19), comparable to the 10 K data spacing near Tc, which itself broadens features. The 4L/Au electronic Tc^avg (220±46 K) is obtained by shifting the 6L −1/q curve by ~30 K and borrowing the 6L width (p. 12), so the agreement with the structural 220 K is partly built in, and the abstract's "finishes roughly 150 K below the bulk Tc" rests on two layers of inference. The authors should explain the origin of the second kink; show raw peak positions from direct fits (e.g. two-Lorentzian fits of the 112–143 cm⁻¹ region) alongside the derivative-based ones and state the effect of smoothing on the extracted widths; fit the temperature dependence of an order-parameter proxy (the Ag(m4)/Ag(m5) integrated intensity, which scales with the order parameter squared, or the Ag(o3)*/Ag(m4) shift in Fig. 3p–t) and −1/q to a model with an explicit distribution of local Tc, reporting centre and width with uncertainties; and state plainly that the 4L/Au electronic Tc is an estimate from the 6L data and that the lower bound near 180 K is model-dependent.

6. The non-detection of the quasi-elastic peak in 4L/Au may be a sensitivity limit, and the inference that Au weakens electron–hole rather than electron–lattice interactions is not secure. The Discussion (pp. 15–16, abstract) says Au "suppress[es] its magnitude beyond detection", which "affirms" the excitonic nature of the quasi-elastic peak, and, because the maximum of −1/q drops by only 16% (0.38 against 0.45), concludes that Au weakens electron–hole rather than electron–lattice interactions. But the peak could be undetectable regardless of any change in excitonic fluctuations: the 4L/Au signal is ~50 times weaker (Figs. 3e, 4e, t); for the Au flakes only, a Rayleigh leakage peak at 15 cm⁻¹, 5 cm⁻¹ from the 10 cm⁻¹ evaluation point, was removed and replaced by interpolation (Methods, p. 19); the bare-substrate ac controls in Fig. 2g start at ~30 cm⁻¹, so there is no substrate control at 10 cm⁻¹; and a ±40 K spread of Tc across layers would by itself smear a peak only ~30–40 K wide in the sharp flakes (Fig. 4p–r) into a plateau. Comparing −1/q, a ratio insensitive to overall intensity, with an absolute quasi-elastic intensity is not like for like, and metallic screening and doping also renormalize the phonon, the hybridization gap, the electron–phonon coupling and the Fano continuum, so a modest change in −1/q does not show that electron–lattice coupling is unaffected. The authors should show the raw low-frequency spectra for 6L and 4L/Au before Rayleigh removal and Bose correction, with the noise level and a detection limit relative to the 6L/18L peak heights; add a temperature-dependent ac control on bare Au down to 10 cm⁻¹; simulate the quasi-elastic response expected from the layer-resolved Tc model to test whether broadening alone explains the non-detection; and temper the electron–hole versus electron–lattice conclusion.

7. The quantitative excitonic/lattice partition of Tc (~150 K excitonic, ~180 K lattice, p. 16) overreaches. It assumes that Au fully removes the excitonic contribution in the interfacial layers while leaving lattice and electron–lattice contributions intact, and it measures the suppression from the bulk 326 K. The 4L/Al2O3 flake already sits at ~280–287 K, so the Au-specific suppression at equal thickness is closer to 100 K. The maximal local suppression of the interfacial layer is not measured but inferred from the lower edge of a broadened transition, which need not mean the excitonic part has been fully quenched. The authors should measure the Au effect against 4L/Al2O3, present the ~150 K as an upper bound within a stated model or remove the numerical partition, and keep the abstract's discussion of excitonic physics at the level of a hypothesis consistent with the data. A quantitative comparison, for example the expected Tc suppression from image-charge screening of the exciton binding energy as a function of distance from Au, would strengthen it.

8. Laser heating and the temperature calibration are not documented, and heating could differ between substrates. About 1 mW of focused 632.8 nm light is used on flakes a few nm thick. The Methods (p. 18) state that the temperature was "checked" with the Stokes/anti-Stokes ratio but show no values. Thermal anchoring differs: Ta2NiSe5 directly on 20–30 nm Au/Cr is well heat-sunk, whereas Ta2NiSe5 on 50–100 nm ALD Al2O3 is more isolated. A few tens of kelvin of differential heating would lower the apparent Tc and could mimic part of the ~40 K reduction for 4L/Al2O3 and of the Au–Al2O3 difference. In addition, the cryostat with a sapphire window and calibration factor is described only for measurements below 300 K, while the data extend to 340–400 K and every Tc lies near the 280–330 K crossover, where a change of setup could introduce discontinuities. The authors should report Stokes/anti-Stokes temperatures against nominal temperature for each flake, at least near Tc; show a laser-power dependence (e.g. 0.25, 0.5 and 1 mW) of the transition marker, such as the Ag(m5) onset, for 4L/Al2O3 and 4L/Au; and explain how temperatures above 300 K were reached and calibrated and whether the same setup was used across 300 K.

9. Thickness and substrate effects are confounded, and the ~40 K reduction of 4L on Al2O3 is unexplained. The 4L/Al2O3 Tc (280–287 K) is well below 7L/Al2O3 (320–330 K). The authors call this "moderately perturbed" with "minimal influence from the substrate" (p. 3) but give no mechanism (intrinsic dimensional effect, ALD-related surface modification or strain). The thickness series is not matched across substrates (7L and 4L on Al2O3; 18L, 6L and 4L on Au), so with only one matched thickness the "intrinsic" Tc at 6L without Au is unknown and it is unclear which part of the 4L/Au suppression is substrate-specific. The authors should add a thickness series on Al2O3 (e.g. 3L, 5L, 6L, ideally 2L on either substrate), or a 4L flake on a third insulator such as hBN; a thicker intermediate flake on Au (e.g. 8–10L) would test the predicted crossover from interface-dominated to bulk-like behaviour. At minimum they should discuss the 4L/Al2O3 reduction alongside the 8L/SiO2 result (ref. 45).

10. The charge-transfer scenario has no magnitude estimate or direct evidence. The paper uses a Ta2NiSe5 work function of ≈5.4 eV but does not give the assumed work function of the amorphous evaporated Au on a Cr underlayer, which may differ from Au(111) and is likely near 5.0–5.1 eV, so the expected transfer may be modest. There is no estimate of the induced carrier density and its decay length, nor a comparison with the Tc versus density measured by gating in atomically thin flakes (Ref. 50) or by K dosing (Refs. 25–28). The authors should give an order-of-magnitude estimate of the transferred density over a 1–2 layer depth (from work functions and density of states) and the Tc shift it implies from those references, to show whether charge transfer or image-charge screening can plausibly give ~100 K. Any direct probe, such as doping-induced shifts of the Ag modes, Kelvin probe or XPS on a Au-supported flake, would strengthen the claim.

11. The Fano fits are applied to what the authors propose is a mixed-phase signal. If the 4L/Au signal is a superposition of orthorhombic and monoclinic layers, a single Fano function (Eq. 1) fitted to the sum gives effective parameters whose meaning is unclear. This affects the interpretation of the broadened −1/q in Fig. 4n, o and the "16% reduction" argument. The authors should fit a weighted two-component model consistent with the toy model, or note this limitation explicitly.
