Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript introduces an evaporation-assisted, Au cold-welding exfoliation and transfer route that yields few-layer Ta2NiSe5 flakes of 10–100 µm on Au and on Al2O3, and uses polarized Raman spectroscopy (monoclinic Ag(m4)/Ag(m5) onset, B2g(o1) Fano asymmetry, quasi-elastic low-energy weight) to track the 326 K transition as a function of thickness and substrate. The headline observation is that 4L and 6L flakes on Au show a transition lowered to Tc^avg ≈ 220–250 K and strongly broadened, whereas 4L on Al2O3 stays sharp at ≈280 K. The authors interpret this as an interfacial Tc gradient from charge transfer and screening by Au, and from the contrast between the suppressed quasi-elastic peak and the barely reduced Fano asymmetry they argue that Au mainly weakens excitonic (electron-hole) interactions, giving at least 100 K of excitonic contribution to Tc. Substrate control of a candidate excitonic insulator is timely, and the fabrication method may be useful well beyond this material. The physical conclusions, however, rest on one flake per condition and on an attribution that the present controls do not single out.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The fabrication protocol is new and practical. It gives large, few-layer flakes of a material that otherwise exfoliates into narrow ribbons (Fig. 1B vs. Fig. 1D,E), and the cold-welding step is dry and could be done in inert gas or vacuum.
- Polarized Raman is shown to resolve the orthorhombic-to-monoclinic transition down to 4L. Bare-substrate spectra (Fig. 2F,G) show that the ac channel is free of substrate signal, which is a sensible check.
- The data are presented transparently: raw spectra, interpolated maps, second-derivative maps and extracted peak positions (Fig. 3), plus three independent transition markers (Figs. 3 and 4) that agree for the sharp-transition samples.
- The 18L/Au and 7L/Al2O3 flakes reproduce bulk behaviour (Tc ≈ 320 K, sharp Fano and quasi-elastic peak), which gives internal benchmarks for the method.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The Au-versus-Al2O3 difference is attributed to charge transfer and screening, but the two substrates differ in many other ways, and none of these is controlled (Discussion, pp. 14–15). The Au interface is made by thermally evaporating Au straight onto the freshly cleaved Ta2NiSe5 surface. That process can cause chemical bonding or intermixing at the interface (strong Au–chalcogen binding is what makes the cold-weld exfoliation work), thermal load, and interfacial strain. The Al2O3 interface is made by ALD at 100–150 °C with TMA/H2O precursors. Amorphous Au and ALD alumina also differ in roughness, thermal-expansion mismatch and clamping. Ta2NiSe5's Tc is known to be very sensitive to uniaxial pressure and strain (refs. 7, 14–17), so a strain- or disorder-induced Tc gradient near the Au interface would give the same broadened, lowered transition with no excitonic screening at all. The conclusion that "charge transfer and electrostatic screening ... are the likely culprit" (p. 15) is reached only by elimination between two substrates. The authors should:
   - compare phonon frequencies and linewidths above Tc (orthorhombic phase, e.g. at 340–400 K) for 4L/Au, 4L/Al2O3 and 18L/Au, to bound strain;
   - add a control that separates the conductor from the chemistry, ideally a thin insulating spacer (for example 1–2 nm of Al2O3 or hBN between Ta2NiSe5 and Au), which keeps the processing similar but suppresses charge transfer while preserving some image-charge screening, or alternatively a different metal or a conducting substrate with a different work function;
   - give some direct evidence of charge transfer (work-function difference, a Raman mode shift known to track doping, or XPS/ARPES on an equivalent interface).

   Without this, the paper supports a narrower claim: an interface effect specific to evaporated Au.

2. Each condition rests on a single flake (p. 7: "five samples: 7L and 4L flakes on Al2O3, and 18L, 6L, and 4L flakes on Au"). The central result, the ≥100 K suppression and broadening in 4L/Au, comes from one 4L/Au flake. The 6L/Au flake points the same way, but it is also a single sample. Flake-to-flake variation from folds, bubbles, partial thickness steps, local delamination of the cold weld, or oxidation during the long temperature series could easily give a broadened, lowered transition. The authors should show at least one more 4L (or thinner) flake on Au, and preferably another 4L on Al2O3, measured the same way, plus a spatial map (or several spots) on the 4L/Au flake showing whether Tc^avg and the broadening are uniform across it. Repeating a cooldown would also show whether the result survives thermal cycling.

3. The step from "quasi-elastic peak suppressed, Fano asymmetry not strongly reduced" to "Au primarily weakens electron-hole (excitonic) interactions" and "establishes an excitonic component" (pp. 15–16, abstract) is not justified by the analysis shown. The two observables are treated inconsistently:
   - The 17% drop in the −1/q maximum (0.46 → 0.38) is dismissed as "a simple temperature broadening of the peak" (p. 15).
   - The loss of the quasi-elastic peak in 4L/Au (Fig. 4T) is read as destruction of critical excitonic fluctuations. Yet a sharp, critical-point peak in χ''ac(10 cm⁻¹) is exactly the feature that a distribution of local Tc values spanning ±40 K (the authors' own picture) would smear out, much more than a broad maximum like −1/q(T).

   The same inhomogeneous-averaging model the authors propose should be applied to both quantities: convolve the 18L/Au or 7L/Al2O3 curves (Fig. 4K,M and P,R) with a Tc distribution matching Fig. 3T, plus the reduced effective weight of each layer, and test whether the 4L/Au data in Fig. 4O and 4T then actually differ in the way the argument needs. Other issues:
   - The 4L/Au signal is ~50× weaker (Fig. 4E, T).
   - The low-frequency window lies near a Rayleigh leak that had to be removed at 15 cm⁻¹ for the Au samples (p. 18), so a sensitivity limit is a plausible reason the peak is "undetectable".
   - Doping and screening also suppress a lattice-driven hybridization instability, by changing band filling and screening the relevant electron-phonon coupling. So Tc suppression by a conductor does not by itself single out excitonic interactions.
   - The authors note that ref. 60 reaches a different conclusion with gating.

   The claim should either be backed by such a quantitative comparison or toned down to "consistent with".

B. Should address

4. The layer-resolved Tc gradient is inferred, not measured, and the optical weighting argument appears to run the wrong way (pp. 13–14, Fig. 5B inset). The electric field of the light goes nearly to zero at a metal surface, so the Ta2NiSe5 layer next to Au should contribute least to the Raman signal, and the top layers most. This fits the much weaker signal from 4L/Au compared with 4L/Al2O3 (×50 scale factor vs. none). The statement that "Raman scattering from the interfacial Ta2NiSe5 layers becomes detectable" and that the measured signal averages over all layers therefore needs a transfer-matrix calculation of the depth-dependent field and Raman efficiency for the Au/Cr and Al2O3/Au stacks at 632.8 nm. If the top layers dominate the signal and the transition onset in 4L/Au is still only at 220 K (Fig. 3T), that would mean the outermost layers are also strongly suppressed, which is hard to reconcile with a gradient confined to "one or two layers" (p. 14). Relatedly, the gradient picture predicts that 6L/Au should be markedly less broadened than 4L/Au, but both show ±40 K. The authors should discuss this, and ideally test the gradient directly, for example with a 2L or 3L flake on Au, Au on top versus Au beneath, or an Al2O3-capped Au sample.

5. The broadening and Tc^avg estimates rely on an ad hoc assumption (p. 10). The ±40 K width is derived by assuming the 60 K separation between the first kink and the second Ag(m4) kink in sharp samples is intrinsic, and that any extra separation in 6L/4L on Au is broadening. The physical origin of the second kink is not discussed, nor whether it should shift with Tc. In addition, Tc^avg is defined as the first kink (onset), which sits at the top of the broadened range, not at its centre. The authors should justify the second-kink criterion, give an independent width estimate (e.g. fitting the integrated Ag(m5) intensity vs. T to a distribution of Tc values; Supplementary Note 2 is cited but not included here), and state clearly what "average" means.

6. The size of the substrate effect is overstated in the abstract and discussion. "Reduced by over 100 K" and "at least 100 K" (abstract, pp. 13 and 15) compare Tc^avg = 220 ± 40 K with bulk 326 K. That gives 106 ± 40 K, not a lower bound of 100 K. At matched thickness the Au-specific effect is 4L/Al2O3 (280 K) against 4L/Au (220 ± 40 K), about 60 K, because thinning alone already lowers Tc by ~15%. The excitonic energy scale argued for on p. 15 should be stated with these uncertainties and the matched-thickness reference.

7. The sample temperature under illumination is not documented (Methods, p. 17). Laser power is ~1 mW at 632.8 nm on few-layer flakes, on two substrates with very different optical absorption and thermal conductance (Au/Cr vs. Al2O3/Au on SiO2/Si or sapphire). Laser heating that differs between substrates would shift the apparent Tc directly. The authors say the Stokes/anti-Stokes ratio was checked but give no values. They should report the Stokes/anti-Stokes temperatures for each sample near Tc and a laser-power dependence of the transition for at least 4L/Au and 4L/Al2O3. They should also say how measurements above 300 K were done (the cryostat is mentioned only for T < 300 K) and how the two temperature setups were cross-calibrated, since several Tc values (280–330 K) sit right at that boundary.

8. Thickness assignment and flake quality on Au are not shown (Methods, p. 17; Supplementary Note 3 is cited but not included). Layer numbers are central to the argument (4L vs. 6L vs. 7L), and these flakes sit on rough amorphous Au or on 50–100 nm ALD Al2O3. The AFM height profiles, step heights, and an estimate of thickness uniformity over the laser spot should be in the paper. AFM or optical images after the temperature cycles would also show whether degradation or delamination occurred.

9. The Fano fitting details are insufficient (Eq. 1, Fig. 4K–O). The fit window 20–80 cm⁻¹ includes the leaked Ag(o1)* mode near 30–35 cm⁻¹ and, below Tc, Ag(m1). Eq. 1 has no background term, even though the low-energy continuum is itself temperature dependent (the quasi-elastic peak). The authors should state how the other modes and background were handled, which parameters were free, and how stable −1/q is against the choice of window. The stated error bars are fit errors only. Repeated measurements or alternative windows would give a more realistic uncertainty, which matters because the 0.46 vs. 0.38 comparison carries the argument in item 3.

C. Minor

10. Fig. 4 caption: "The dashed curves in (H) and (J) are guides to the eye". The dashed curves are in (H) and (I), and the text (p. 11) refers to H and I.

11. Fig. 3P–T caption and text: the first-kink criterion ("temperature below which the peak position begins to decrease") is applied to Ag(o3)*, a polarization-leakage mode whose intensity depends on alignment. The authors should confirm the result does not depend on the leakage amplitude, or show the same kink in the aa channel, where Ag(o3) is intrinsic.

12. The structural and electronic Tc for 18L/Au differ by 14 K (320 vs. 306 ± 14 K) and for 7L/Al2O3 by 10 K in the opposite direction (320 vs. 330 ± 10 K). The text says the values "agree" (p. 13). A sentence on the systematic differences between the 15%-of-maximum criterion and the kink criterion would help.

13. p. 3: "exctonic" should be "excitonic". p. 4: the 15% reduction for 4L/Al2O3 is 280/326 ≈ 14%; consider quoting it with its uncertainty (+7/−10 K).

14. The Supplementary Notes 1–3 referred to throughout were not available with the submission. The mode-symmetry azimuthal analysis (Note 1), the extra structural analysis (Note 2) and the AFM (Note 3) should be supplied.

15. Fig. 5B: the inset cartoon shows the hypothesized layer gradient, but no data resolve individual layers. The caption should say it is a schematic interpretation.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Add an insulating-spacer or alternative-conductor control (e.g. Ta2NiSe5/hBN or thin Al2O3/Au, or a different metal) to separate charge transfer and screening from interfacial chemistry and strain, together with orthorhombic-phase phonon frequencies to bound strain.
- Measure several 4L (and if possible 2L/3L) flakes on each substrate, with spatial maps, to show reproducibility and in-plane homogeneity.
- Model the depth-dependent Raman sensitivity (transfer matrix) and use an explicit Tc-distribution model to fit −1/q(T), χ''ac(10 cm⁻¹, T) and the Ag(m5) onset together. This would turn the gradient and excitonic arguments from qualitative to quantitative.
- Report Stokes/anti-Stokes temperatures and power dependence, and give AFM thickness data, in the main text or the supplement.

5. Verdict: major revision
