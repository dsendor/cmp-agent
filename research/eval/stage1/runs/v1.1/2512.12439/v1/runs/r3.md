Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript introduces an evaporation-assisted exfoliation and Au cold-welding transfer route to few-layer Ta2NiSe5 on Au and on ALD Al2O3, and uses polarized Raman spectroscopy (onset of the monoclinic Ag(m4)/Ag(m5) modes, the Fano asymmetry of B2g(o1), and the low-energy quasi-elastic response) to track the 326 K transition. Its central result is that 4L and 6L flakes on Au show a transition lowered by roughly 70–100 K and strongly broadened, whereas 4L on Al2O3 stays close to bulk. The authors read this as an interfacial charge-transfer and screening effect, and as evidence that excitonic interactions account for at least ~100 K of the bulk Tc. Substrate tuning of an excitonic-insulator candidate is a timely idea, and the transfer method may be useful well beyond this material. The quantitative partition between excitonic and lattice contributions, however, rests on a chain of interpretation that the data do not yet pin down.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- A new, dry and broadly applicable fabrication route that gives 10–100 µm few-layer Ta2NiSe5 flakes on chosen substrates (Fig. 1), which is technically valuable for vdW materials that adhere poorly.
- A careful Raman protocol. Selection rules are confirmed by azimuthal dependence, the bare substrates are measured as controls (Fig. 2F, G), and the ac channel is chosen to reject the substrate contribution.
- Three independent Raman markers (structural phonon onset, Fano asymmetry, quasi-elastic peak) are tracked against temperature for five flakes, and the structural and electronic Tc values agree with each other for the sharp-transition flakes (Fig. 5).
- The substrate contrast between 4L/Au and 4L/Al2O3 is visually clear in Figs. 3 and 4.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. **One flake per thickness/substrate combination; the headline contrast is not shown to be reproducible.** The whole paper rests on five flakes (7L and 4L on Al2O3; 18L, 6L and 4L on Au), each apparently measured once (p. 7). The key comparison, 4L/Au against 4L/Al2O3, therefore compares two individual flakes made by different deposition steps (thermal Au evaporation directly onto the crystal versus ALD at 100–150 °C followed by Au). Flake-to-flake variation in the interface, local strain, wrinkles or partial delamination could alone produce a lowered, broadened transition, and so could the AFM-determined layer count on rough amorphous Au. A top-tier referee would require at least a second 4L (or 3–5L) flake on each substrate, ideally from separate fabrication runs, showing the same Tc shift and broadening. Spatial mapping across one flake would also help, to show that the broadening is not inhomogeneity within the laser spot. The authors should also say whether the results survive thermal cycling (the measurements span 10–400 K).

2. **Alternatives to the charge-transfer/screening attribution are not excluded, so the "excitonic" conclusion is not yet supported.** The paper concludes (pp. 15–16) that the Au effect is charge transfer and screening, and hence that excitonic mechanisms contribute "at least 100 K" of Tc. The Au and Al2O3 samples differ in much more than conductivity:
   - (i) Au is thermally evaporated directly onto the freshly cleaved Ta2NiSe5. Hot Au adatoms are known to react with or intercalate into selenides (Au–Se bonding, Se vacancies, interdiffusion), which would create disorder and chemical doping in the interfacial layers.
   - (ii) Strain differs. The paper itself stresses that the deposited material conforms to the rippled layers (p. 4), and the transition is a volume-preserving shear that is highly strain-sensitive (Refs. 14–17). Clamping to a stiff metal film with a different thermal expansion could suppress or smear the shear distortion.
   - (iii) Electron doping lowers Tc in both the excitonic and the hybridization/band-Jahn-Teller pictures, so a doping-induced Tc reduction does not by itself discriminate between them. The cited gate-tuning study (Ref. 60), which reached a different conclusion, is precisely such a clean doping experiment.

   The authors should characterize the interface (cross-sectional TEM/EDX, XPS of the Au/Ta2NiSe5 interface, or Raman of the bare crystal surface after Au deposition and removal). They should add a control that separates conductivity from deposition chemistry and strain, for example a thin ALD Al2O3 or hBN spacer between Ta2NiSe5 and Au, or a flake mechanically transferred onto pre-deposited Au. Without such controls, the statement "manifesting the presence of excitonic interactions" in the abstract and the quantitative partition of Tc on p. 15 are not justified. At minimum, these conclusions should be recast as one possible interpretation.

3. **The layer-gradient picture is not quantitatively consistent with the optics of a flake on a metal.** The mixed-phase model (pp. 13–14, Fig. 5B) holds that the layers nearest Au, with Tc suppressed by "over 100 K", dominate the broadened signal. At the 632.8 nm excitation, however, the optical field has a node at a good metal surface, so the Raman response of the first one or two layers above Au should be strongly suppressed relative to the top layers. The very small signals for 6L/Au and 4L/Au (scaled ×25 and ×50 in Figs. 3 and 4, against ×3 for 18L) fit this picture. The paper also argues that the interface effect is confined to one or two layers, yet 6L/Au (where ≥4 of 6 layers would be bulk-like) shows the same ~100 K kink separation and an electronic Tc_avg of 246 K. Taken together, these points make it unclear how a 1–2-layer interfacial region could pull the averaged response down by ~80–100 K. The authors should:
   - compute the depth-dependent Raman sensitivity (a transfer-matrix calculation of the incident and scattered fields for the Au/Cr/SiO2 and Al2O3/Au stacks);
   - fit the χ''ac(ω,T) maps with a weighted superposition of layers with distinct local Tc values;
   - show that the proposed gradient reproduces both the 6L and 4L data.

   The abstract claim that the layers closest to Au have "T_c ... reduced by over 100 K" is an inference from this model, not a measured quantity, and needs this support.

B. Should address

4. **Effective sample temperature.** The laser power is ~1 mW at 632.8 nm (p. 17). Few-layer flakes on Au and on Al2O3/Au/Cr/SiO2 dissipate heat differently, and laser heating would shift the apparent Tc by exactly the amount being discussed. The text says the Stokes/anti-Stokes ratio was checked, but no data are shown. The authors should show the Stokes/anti-Stokes temperatures for each flake (especially 4L/Al2O3, whose 15% reduction is otherwise unexplained, and 4L/Au), or a power dependence of the transition features.

5. **The Tc extraction and its uncertainties are heuristic.** The structural Tc is the temperature of the first kink, with error bars set by the temperature spacing (p. 8). For 6L/4L on Au, Tc_avg ≈ 250±40 K and 220±40 K come from assuming that the 60 K kink separation of the sharp flakes is intrinsic and that any excess is symmetric broadening (p. 10). That assumption is not justified, and the "average" is not the midpoint of the reported kinks (the first kinks are at 250/220 K and the second at 150/120 K, so the midpoints are ~200/170 K). The electronic Tc uses a 15%-of-maximum criterion. The authors should define Tc_avg operationally (for example, the midpoint of the integrated Ag(m5) intensity versus T, fitted with a broadened order-parameter form), give model-based error bars, and state how robust the "at least 100 K" figure is to this choice. For 6L/Au the reduction is ~76 K, so "over 100 K" in the abstract applies only to 4L/Au, and there only to its central value.

6. **The Fano and quasi-elastic analysis cannot separate electron–hole from electron–lattice effects as claimed.** On p. 15 the near-constant maximum −1/q (0.38 against 0.46) is read as unchanged electron–lattice coupling, while the loss of the quasi-elastic peak is read as destroyed excitonic fluctuations. But q depends on the continuum density of states and on the ratio of the electronic and phonon Raman vertices, not only on the coupling strength. The quasi-elastic peak in 4L/Au sits in a spectrum scaled ×50, near a removed Rayleigh leakage at 15 cm⁻¹ (p. 18), and close to the noise floor. Its "suppression beyond detection" may be a signal-to-noise effect. The authors should show raw χ''ac(10 cm⁻¹) normalized to an internal reference (for example the B2g(o1)/Ag(m2) integrated intensity) with error bars. They should also show the fit parameters (ω0, γ, A) with uncertainties and explain why Fano parameters can be compared across flakes. The interpretive sentence "This suppression affirms the nature of the quasi-elastic peak as being related to critical excitonic fluctuations" should be softened.

7. **Incomplete thickness matrix.** The Au series has 18L, 6L and 4L, and the Al2O3 series has 7L and 4L. A 6L flake on Al2O3 is missing, and so are thinner flakes (2–3L) on either substrate. These would show whether the Au effect grows as expected when the interfacial fraction increases (and would test the 1–2-layer confinement estimate). They would also show whether the 15% reduction in 4L/Al2O3 is an intrinsic thickness effect. Adding them, or explaining why they could not be measured, would strengthen Fig. 5.

8. **Hysteresis and the transition on warming against cooling.** It is not stated whether the data were taken on cooling or warming, or whether runs were repeated. Since a broadened transition is the central observable, the authors should state the protocol and, if possible, show a second run.

9. **The Supplementary Notes referenced (1–3) are not available.** The selection-rule analysis, the additional structural analysis and, above all, the AFM thickness determination (Supplementary Note 3) are essential for evaluating the layer counts. They should be included. Layer counts on amorphous Au need quoted uncertainties.

C. Minor

10. The 15% Tc reduction in 4L/Al2O3 (280 K) is larger than the ≤9% reported for 5L on SiO2 (Ref. 45). It deserves comment (substrate strain, ALD processing, heating), since the paper uses this flake as the "no interface effect" control.

11. The text (p. 11) refers to the dashed guides "in Fig. 4, H and I", while the Fig. 4 caption says "(H) and (J)". In the figure the dashed curves appear in H and I.

12. Fig. 5B: the purple "mixed" band and the cartoon imply a defined interfacial Tc for the first layers. Since this is not measured, label it as schematic.

13. The discussion of Ref. 60, which reaches a different conclusion with gate-tuned, hBN-encapsulated flakes, is limited to one sentence (p. 16). Given its direct relevance to the excitonic attribution, compare the two results quantitatively (doping level, Tc shift per carrier).

14. Estimate the charge transferred from Au (work function difference, expected carrier density in the first layer) and compare it with the doping levels known to shift Tc (for example K-dosing, Refs. 26–29). This would make the charge-transfer argument concrete.

15. "exctonic" (p. 3) is a typo. "Polarizer-resolved" (p. 4) should probably read "polarization-resolved".

16. The statement that surface layers remain "orthorhombic and marginally semimetallic" in the mixed region (p. 14) is not tested and should be framed as a hypothesis.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Add a spacer control (thin hBN or Al2O3 between Ta2NiSe5 and Au) and a "transferred-onto-Au" control. Together they separate electrostatic screening and charge transfer from deposition chemistry and strain; this is the experiment that would make the excitonic conclusion convincing.
- Compute the depth-resolved Raman sensitivity of each stack and fit the temperature-dependent spectra with a layer-resolved Tc model. This turns the gradient cartoon into a quantitative statement.
- Measure at least one more flake for each key configuration, and show the Stokes/anti-Stokes temperature for each.
- Support the interface picture with interface-sensitive characterization (XPS core-level shifts, or cross-sectional STEM).

5. Verdict

Verdict: major revision
