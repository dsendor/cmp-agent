A. Must address

1. **The Au–Al2O3 comparison does not isolate "conducting versus insulating", so the charge-transfer/screening attribution is reached only by elimination.** The Discussion (pp. 13–15) concludes that "charge transfer and electrostatic screening ... are the likely culprit" for the lowered, broadened transition on Au. The two substrates differ in much more than conductivity. Au is thermally evaporated directly onto the freshly cleaved Ta2NiSe5. Hot Au adatoms can bond to or react with selenides (Au–Se bonding, Se vacancies, intermixing or intercalation at the first layer), adding disorder, chemical doping and thermal load; the strong Au–chalcogen binding is what makes the cold-weld exfoliation work. The deposit also conforms to the ripples (p. 4), which adds roughness. Al2O3 is grown by ALD at 100–150 °C with TMA/H2O, a different chemistry and thermal history. The stacks also differ in thermal-expansion mismatch and in how strain is transmitted on cooling: a 50–100 nm amorphous ALD oxide clamps the flake differently from a 20–50 nm Au film welded to Au/Cr. The transition is a volume-preserving shear, and the authors' own work (Refs. 7, 14–17, in particular Ref. 17) shows that uniaxial strain strongly tunes Tc and the excitonic fluctuations, so a strain- or disorder-induced Tc gradient near Au would give the same signature with no screening at all. The Al2O3 sample excludes oxidation (p. 15) but none of the Au-specific alternatives. The authors should: (i) use phonon frequencies and linewidths of all five flakes, at room temperature and in the orthorhombic phase (340–400 K), as an internal strain gauge, compared with bulk and with the uniaxial-strain coefficients of Ref. 17; (ii) add a control that separates the conductor from the chemistry and strain, ideally a few-nm Al2O3 or hBN spacer between flake and Au, a flake mechanically transferred onto pre-deposited Au, or a different metal or conducting substrate with a different work function; (iii) characterize the interface (cross-sectional TEM/STEM with EDX, XPS core-level shifts of the Au/Ta2NiSe5 interface, or Raman of the crystal surface after Au deposition and removal). Without such evidence the paper supports only a narrower claim, an interface effect specific to evaporated Au, and the charge-transfer/screening attribution should be stated as a hypothesis.

2. **Each thickness/substrate condition rests on a single flake, so the headline result is not shown to be reproducible.** The paper measures five flakes (7L and 4L on Al2O3; 18L, 6L and 4L on Au), each apparently once (p. 7). The central ≥100 K suppression and broadening comes from one 4L/Au flake whose signal is about 50× weaker than the reference (Fig. 3E, 4E against 3B, 4B), and the supporting 6L/Au result is also one flake. The key comparison, 4L/Au against 4L/Al2O3, is between two individual flakes made by different deposition steps. Flake-to-flake variation from folds, bubbles, wrinkles, partial thickness steps, local delamination of the cold weld, local strain, oxidation during the long temperature series, or a misassigned layer count on rough amorphous Au could alone produce a lowered, broadened transition. The authors should show at least one more 4L (or 3–5L) flake on Au, preferably also another 4L on Al2O3, ideally from separate fabrication runs and measured the same way. They should add spatial Raman maps (or several spots) across the 4L/Au flake to show whether Tc^avg and the broadening are uniform, which would also test whether the broadening is lateral inhomogeneity within the laser spot. A second cooldown of the same flake would show whether the result survives thermal cycling over 10–400 K.

3. **The excitonic attribution is not unique, and the low-energy evidence for it in 4L/Au is at the noise and artifact level.** The abstract and conclusions (pp. 15–16) state that the sensitivity to conducting Au "establishes an excitonic component", "manifest[s] the presence of excitonic interactions", and that at least ~100 K of Tc is excitonic. Three problems. (a) Uniqueness: electron doping and screening also suppress a Q = 0 hybridization-gap/band-Jahn-Teller instability (Refs. 11–13, 36), by moving the chemical potential away from the hybridization point and screening the relevant electron–phonon coupling, so a Tc reduction by a conductor does not discriminate between the scenarios without a quantitative model; a strain origin (concern 1) would discriminate even less. (b) Quasi-elastic peak: the 4L/Au low-energy signal (Fig. 4T, ×50) is extremely weak and appears comparable to the bare Au/Cr ac background (Fig. 2G), though units across figures are not stated to be comparable. A Rayleigh leak at 15 cm⁻¹ was removed by interpolation in exactly the 6L and 4L Au spectra (Methods, p. 18), and χ''(10 cm⁻¹) lies near the notch/edge cutoff, so "suppressed beyond detection" may reflect sensitivity rather than physics. Moreover, a sharp critical peak in χ''ac(10 cm⁻¹) is exactly what a distribution of local Tc values spanning ±40 K (the authors' own picture) would smear out, much more than a broad maximum like −1/q(T). (c) Fano comparison: the claim that the electron–lattice channel is "not strongly reduced" rests on −1/q falling from 0.46 to 0.38, a 17% drop the authors dismiss as temperature broadening, while the loss of the quasi-elastic peak is read as destroyed excitonic fluctuations; the two observables are treated inconsistently. In addition q depends on the continuum density of states and on the ratio of electronic and phonon Raman vertices, not only on coupling strength, and is sensitive to the continuum shape and the leaked Ag(o1)* mode in the 20–80 cm⁻¹ window. The authors should: show the raw, unscaled, same-unit low-energy ac spectra of 4L/Au next to bare Au/Cr at the same temperatures, with noise levels and before Rayleigh removal, and χ''ac(10 cm⁻¹) normalized to an internal reference (e.g. the B2g(o1)/Ag(m2) integrated intensity) with error bars; demonstrate that a quasi-elastic peak of the size expected from a broadened transition would have been detectable; apply the same inhomogeneous-averaging model to both observables by convolving the 18L/Au or 7L/Al2O3 curves (Fig. 4K, M and P, R) with a Tc distribution matching Fig. 3T plus the reduced layer weighting, and test whether 4L/Au (Fig. 4O, T) then differs as the argument needs; and provide a model that distinguishes excitonic from hybridization/lattice suppression under doping. Otherwise the abstract and conclusions, including "This suppression affirms the nature of the quasi-elastic peak as being related to critical excitonic fluctuations", should be softened to "consistent with".

4. **The interfacial-gradient model is inconsistent with the measured onsets and ignores the optical depth sensitivity of a flake on a metal.** The model (pp. 13–14, Fig. 5B inset) says the layers nearest Au have Tc suppressed by over 100 K, the surface layers stay near bulk ("Tc closer to room temperature"), and the Au effect is confined to one or two layers. (a) Onsets: if two or three of four layers in 4L/Au were near-bulk, or at least like 4L/Al2O3 (~280 K), the first kink and the Ag(m4)/Ag(m5) onset should appear near 280–300 K followed by a tail. Instead the first kink is at ~220 K (4L/Au) and ~250 K (6L/Au) (Fig. 3S, T), 60 K and 30 K below 4L/Al2O3, which indicates that all layers are suppressed. The 6L/Au flake should have four or five unperturbed layers, yet its onset is below the thinner 4L/Al2O3 flake, it shows the same ~100 K kink separation and ±40 K width as 4L/Au, and its electronic Tc_avg is 246 K; the gradient picture predicts markedly less broadening in 6L. (b) Depth sensitivity: at 632.8 nm the standing-wave field has a near-node at an optically thick Au surface, so the Raman response is weakest for exactly the interfacial layers the model says are perturbed, while the Al2O3 flakes sit on a 50–100 nm dielectric spacer that weights the layers entirely differently. The very small signals for 6L/Au and 4L/Au (scaled ×25 and ×50 against ×3 for 18L) fit this. The statements that "Raman scattering from the interfacial Ta2NiSe5 layers becomes detectable" (pp. 13–14) and that in 18L the "fraction of interfacial layers is too small to contribute to the Raman signal" (p. 13) therefore need a quantitative basis. The authors should compute the layer-resolved Raman sensitivity (transfer-matrix calculation of incident and scattered fields) for the Au/Cr/SiO2 and Al2O3/Au stacks; fit the χ''ac(ω,T) maps and the Ag(m5) onset with a weighted superposition of layers with distinct local Tc; and show that the proposed gradient reproduces the onset, width and spectral evolution of both 6L and 4L. Otherwise the microscopic picture should be revised; uniform suppression of the whole flake or lateral inhomogeneity within the spot are alternatives. Direct tests would include a 2L or 3L flake on Au, Au on top versus Au beneath, or an Al2O3-capped Au sample. The abstract claim that the layers closest to Au have Tc "reduced by over 100 K" is a model inference, not a measured quantity.

B. Should address

5. **Laser heating and the effective sample temperature are not documented.** The Methods (p. 17) state ~1 mW at 632.8 nm and that the actual temperature was checked from the Stokes/anti-Stokes ratio, but give no values. Few-layer flakes on 50–100 nm amorphous Al2O3 and on welded Au films differ in absorption (the Au stack absorbs at 633 nm) and thermal anchoring, and the flake–Au interface may be imperfect. A local temperature offset shifts the apparent Tc directly, by the amount under discussion, and a gradient across the spot would mimic broadening. The authors should report Stokes/anti-Stokes temperatures against nominal temperature for each flake near Tc (especially 4L/Au and 4L/Al2O3) and a laser-power dependence (e.g. 0.25, 0.5, 1 mW) of the Ag(m4)/Ag(m5) onset or second-kink temperature.

6. **Tc^avg, its ±40 K width and the electronic Tc rest on ad hoc criteria.** The broadening is derived (p. 10) by assuming that the 60 K separation between the first kink and the second Ag(m4) kink in sharp flakes is intrinsic and that any excess (100 K) is symmetric broadening of ±40 K. The physical origin of the second kink is not explained, nor why it should shift rigidly with Tc. Tc^avg is set equal to the first kink (onset), at the top of the broadened range rather than its centre: for 4L/Au this implies an onset near 260 K that the data do not show (Fig. 3T), while the midpoints of the first and second kinks (250/150 K and 220/120 K) would give ~200/170 K. The structural Tc error bars come only from temperature spacing (p. 8), and the electronic Tc uses an arbitrary 15%-of-maximum criterion (Fig. 4P–S). The authors should define Tc^avg operationally, for example by a global fit of the integrated Ag(m5) intensity (an order-parameter proxy) or the Ag(m4) position against T with a distribution of Tc values, applied identically to all five flakes, giving a mean and width with model-based uncertainties; show that conclusions are unchanged for 10% or 25% thresholds; and state how robust the "at least 100 K" figure is. Supplementary Note 2, cited for this, was not available.

7. **The size of the Au effect is overstated.** "Reduced by over 100 K" and "at least 100 K" (abstract, pp. 13 and 15) compare Tc^avg = 220 ± 40 K with bulk 326 K, which gives 106 ± 40 K (roughly 66–146 K), not a lower bound. For 6L/Au the reduction is ~76 K, so the figure applies only to the central value of 4L/Au. At matched thickness the Au-specific effect is 4L/Al2O3 (~280 K) against 4L/Au (220 ± 40 K), about 60 K, because thinning alone already lowers Tc by ~15%. The magnitude, and the excitonic energy scale argued on p. 15, should be stated with these uncertainties and the matched-thickness reference.

8. **The ~15% Tc reduction in the 4L/Al2O3 control is not discussed.** This flake is described as showing "minimal influence from the substrate" (p. 4) and "nearly bulk-like properties" (abstract), yet Tc drops from 326 K to ~280–287 K (Fig. 5A), more than the error bars and more than the ≤9% reduction reported for 5L on SiO2 (Ref. 45). 10. **The relationship to the gate-tuning study of Ref. 60 needs substantive discussion.** The authors note in one sentence (p. 16) that gate-tuned, hBN-encapsulated atomically thin Ta2NiSe5 leads to "a different conclusion", without saying what it is or why the results differ. Gate doping is the cleanest test of "charge transfer suppresses Tc". The authors should compare quantitatively the carrier densities reached and the Tc shifts observed (Tc shift per carrier). If gating at comparable density does not suppress Tc by ~100 K, this bears directly on concerns 1 and 3.

11. **Fano fitting details are insufficient.** The 20–80 cm⁻¹ window of Eq. 1 (Fig. 4K–O) includes the leaked Ag(o1)* mode near 30–35 cm⁻¹ and, below Tc, Ag(m1); Eq. 1 has no background term even though the low-energy continuum (the quasi-elastic response) is temperature dependent. The error bars, stated as smaller than the symbols, are fit-statistical only. A realistic uncertainty matters because the 0.46 versus 0.38 comparison carries the argument in concern 3.
