A. Must address

1. The model has a single odd-mode relaxation rate, and the physical claims depend on it. Eq. (2) relaxes every odd harmonic m ≥ 3 at one rate 1/ℓo, and every even harmonic m ≥ 2 at 1/ℓe. The microscopic studies the paper cites to motivate the tomographic regime (Refs. 15–18) find odd-mode rates that depend strongly on m (roughly ∝ m⁴ at low m, with logarithms, in Ledwith et al., Ref. 16). That dependence is what produces the superdiffusion and scale-dependent viscosity of Refs. 15, 16, and it means higher odd modes relax much faster than m = 3. The boundary layer is exactly where higher harmonics matter: the layer equation, Eq. (16), has v_x² ∂²/∂χ² acting on the whole odd sector, and the histograms in Fig. 1(d,e) show substantial weight up to m ≈ 5 at x = −0.5 and −0.45. With m-dependent rates the layer could become multi-scale rather than having a single width. Several results inherit the single-ℓo assumption: the layer width √(ℓeℓo); the O(keko) slip and its ko/(4(1+(3ko/rc)²)) coefficient in Eq. (17); the Lorentzian field scale (3ko)⁻¹ that sets the low-field feature of the magnetoconductance in Eq. (5) and Fig. 1(c); and the claim that the conductance "can be used to measure both the even- and odd-mode mean free paths". With an m-dependent spectrum, "ℓo" has no single meaning and the extracted value may be a model-dependent average. The Discussion also says Eq. (2), not "a dual-relaxation time description", is "the appropriate starting point", yet Eq. (2) is itself a relaxation-time model with one more rate. The authors should either (a) solve the channel problem numerically with an m-dependent odd spectrum (for example γ_m ∝ m⁴ for odd m, or the exact rates of Refs. 17, 18) and show how far the velocity profile, slip, layer width and Fig. 1(c), including the position of the minimum near Rc ~ ℓo, change, stating which effective ℓo is measured; or (b) give a controlled argument for why only the slowest odd mode controls the O(ke) results; or (c) reframe the claims explicitly as results of a three-rate model and narrow the statements about measuring ℓo in real tomographic Fermi liquids.

2. There is no comparison with the conventional (non-tomographic) kinetic description, so the "new distinguishing features" and the "unequivocal macroscopic signature" claims are not shown. In Fig. 1(d,e) and in the Hall-resistance discussion the only comparison is with the no-slip Stokes–Ohm solution. A classical gas with diffuse walls (ko = ke, as in the authors' Refs. 23, 24) already has a Knudsen layer, an O(ke) slip and rarefaction corrections: the O(ke) slip 64ke/(15π) in Eq. (5) does not depend on ko, Knudsen-layer slip is also suppressed by a field at Rc ~ ℓe, and ballistic and Ohmic channels with diffuse edges show negative and non-monotonic magnetoresistance (the ballistic peak near W/Rc ≈ 0.5, Ref. 12; finite-G Gurzhi crossover; Refs. 1, 34, 36). Likewise, Ref. 23 already reports that the near-hydrodynamic Hall field departs from the Hall-viscosity prediction, and Knudsen-layer corrections with ko = ke may also exceed O(ke²), in which case the problem with the Scaffidi protocol is generic to diffuse boundaries rather than tomographic. The authors should add to Fig. 1(c) and Fig. 1(d), and to the ΔRxy comparison of Eq. (11), numerical curves for ko = ke at the same ke (solved numerically, since the expansion assumes ko = O(1)) and for one or two intermediate ko, show at least one finite-G case, state how much of the minimum's depth and position, of the enhanced conductance, and of the positive sign of ΔRxy is specific to ko ≫ ke, and explain how the tomographic dip is distinguished from the other mechanisms in the same field range.

B. Should address

3. The regime of validity of the expansion is not stated, and both the figure parameters and the physically motivated limit sit at or beyond its edge. The expansion is in √ke with ko held at O(1) (Methods, Eq. 13), but the results contain keko and √(keko) as effective small parameters: the layer width is √(keko) in units of L, the slip term in Eq. (5) is keko/(1+(3ko/rc)²), and the tomographic-layer contributions to the integrated conductance, of order ke√(keko), are dropped as "o(ke)". In Fig. 1(d,e) (ke = 0.1, ko = 2, keko = 0.2) the layer width is ≈ 0.45 against a half-width of 0.5, so the layers overlap and the bulk/layer separation behind Eq. (16) (semi-infinite χ) is not obviously valid; in Fig. 1(c) (ke = 0.025, ko = 5, keko = 0.125) it is ≈ 0.35, so the dropped terms are a ~35% correction to the O(ke) terms kept. The Discussion motivates the regime with ℓe ~ (TF/T)² and ℓo ~ (TF/T)⁴ at T ≲ 0.1 TF, which implies ℓo/ℓe ≳ 100, and Fig. 1(a) labels the regime ℓo ≫ ℓe; in this deep tomographic regime keko grows past unity, the layers fill the channel, and the ko-linear slip term in Eq. (5) cannot be trusted. Agreement with numerics is good at the chosen points, which is reassuring, but an experimentalist fitting Eq. (5) needs to know where it fails. The authors should state the distinguished limit explicitly (for example ke → 0 with keko → 0, i.e. √(ℓeℓo) ≪ L) with the size of the neglected terms, give an error map of Eq. (5) and Eq. (3) against the numerics over the (ke, ko) plane or as a function of keko at fixed ke up to keko ~ 1, and show numerically whether the ko-linear enhancement saturates when ℓeℓo ≳ L².

4. The magnetic-field prefactor 1 + (2ke/rc)² in Eq. (5) is inserted by hand ("where we also include as a prefactor"), not derived. The expansion treats rc as O(1), so the regime Rc ~ ℓe (1/rc ~ 1/ke) lies outside the derived result, yet Fig. 1(c) exercises it up to 1/rc ≈ 50–100, where 2ke/rc ≈ 2.5 and Rc < ℓe. The high-field upturn, half of the non-monotonic signature, therefore rests on a heuristic factor that also multiplies the slip and rarefaction terms, which have their own field dependence. The authors should state how rc is ordered relative to ke and ko, derive the uniformly valid form (for example a distinguished limit rc ~ ke) or justify the multiplicative combination and label the high-field part as an interpolation, and show the residual between Eq. (5) and the numerics across the whole field range of Fig. 1(c), particularly around the minimum where ℓe and ℓo would be extracted.

5. Estimates for real materials and finite-G results are missing. The paper says the regime is "readily accessible" and the signatures "should be readily detectable in present-day experiments" but gives no numbers. The figures use ko/ke = 20 (Fig. 1d,e) and 200 (Fig. 1c) while ℓo ≪ ℓMR must also hold; by the quoted scalings, large ratios need T ≪ 0.1 TF, at which ℓe itself becomes long and ℓe ≪ L is harder to satisfy. The authors should take a representative graphene or GaAs channel (density, width, temperature), quote estimated ℓe, ℓo and phonon/impurity ℓMR from Refs. 17, 18, show that ℓe ≪ L ≲ ℓo ≪ ℓMR can be met, and give the resulting ke, ko and G, the fields corresponding to Rc = 3ℓo and Rc = 2ℓe, and the predicted fractional depth of the conductance minimum. Because the conductance is given only for G → ∞ (Eq. 5) while real devices have G ~ 1–10, and finite G acts on the same field scales, the finite-G conductance (contained in the full Eq. 3) should be given or plotted.

6. The claim that ℓe and ℓo can be extracted from the magnetoconductance is not tested as an inverse problem. The low-field feature is a broad Lorentzian in 3ko/rc multiplied by keko, and the upturn is set by 2ke/rc. The authors should show that fitting Eq. (5), or its finite-G form, to synthetic numerical data (with noise, at realistic parameters) recovers ke and ko independently, and with what uncertainty, for example using the ratios of the minimum's position and depth to the high-field rise.
Raised by: r1, r2

7. Only fully diffuse edges are treated (Eq. 12). Slip coefficients and boundary layers in kinetic theory depend strongly on the accommodation coefficient, and real graphene or GaAs edges are partly specular, varying between devices (for example etched versus encapsulated graphene). The slip coefficient 32/(15π), the ko/4 second-order term in Eq. (17) and the layer functions 𝒴, 𝒯 will change with specularity, so the magnetoconductance route to ℓe and ℓo would be confounded by an unknown edge parameter. The authors should at least discuss how a Maxwell-type specularity parameter enters Eqs. (16)–(17) and whether the ko-dependent slip survives partially specular edges, or show one numerical case.
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

8. The Hall-viscosity conclusions rest on a single parameter point. The statement that ΔRxy "does not provide an accurate avenue" for the Hall viscosity is supported by one evaluation (ke = 0.025, ko = 5, 1/rc = 0.02, G → ∞; ΔRxy = +0.20 asymptotic against +0.19 numerical). There 2ke/rc = 0.001, so the hydrodynamic Hall-viscosity term is about −0.004 against the tomographic +0.20. The authors should plot ΔRxy against 1/rc and against ko (including ko → ke), asymptotic and numerical, overlaid with Eq. (9), to show over what range the ke^{3/2} boundary-layer term dominates the O(ke²) Hall-viscosity term and where, if anywhere (for example when Rc ≲ ℓo suppresses the layer), the Hall viscosity could still be extracted. For the Holder et al. protocol (Eq. 7), the extra term is of order ko²/G² and vanishes as G → ∞; its size relative to the first term should be quantified for realistic G.

9. The numerical methods are not described well enough to reproduce or judge the main results. Every main result is validated against "direct numerical solutions to the linearized Boltzmann equation", but the method is not given: discretisation in x and θ or angular-harmonic cutoff, spatial grid, treatment of the diffuse boundary, convergence tests. The cutoff matters especially near the boundary, where many harmonics contribute. The Methods also omit the derivation of Eqs. (14)–(15), the intermediate O(√ke) problem, and how the two-dimensional (χ, θ) layer problem Eq. (16) was solved for 𝒴0, 𝒴1, 𝒯0, 𝒯1 (angular truncation, far-field matching, treatment of χ → ∞). Since the agreement in Fig. 1 and the 0.20 against 0.19 comparison are the main evidence for the asymptotics, these details should be added to the Methods or a supplement.

10. Feature (iii), the bulk rarefaction correction that decreases the curvature of the profile, is not generic. The third square bracket in Eq. (3) comes from I₂ in Eq. (15c), which involves ∂⁴u⁽⁰⁾; this vanishes for the Poiseuille profile, so the term scales as keko/G² for large G and disappears as G → ∞. The effect therefore exists only through momentum-relaxing scattering, whereas the Discussion stresses ultraclean devices. The abstract and introduction should state this dependence on G, and the authors should show the size of the term at experimentally relevant G.
