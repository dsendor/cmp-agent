A. Must address

1. **The magnetic-dipole (nonlinear Edelstein) origin of the emission is not separated from other second-order sources with the same symmetry.** The main evidence for the magnetic-dipole assignment is the normal-incidence pattern |S_THz| ∝ |cos(2φ − 2δ)| with a non-universal δ (Fig. 2d,e; Eq. 3; p. 2 right column, p. 3 left column). This form is not unique to an axial-vector response in 3̄. At the surface the symmetry drops to the polar group 3 (C3, no mirrors), where an in-plane polar vector transforms as the same E doublet as an in-plane axial vector. The surface electric-dipole rectification tensor then has exactly this form (P_x = a(E_x² − E_y²) − 2bE_xE_y, P_y = −b(E_x² − E_y²) − 2aE_xE_y), so surface optical rectification, a surface shift or injection current, a near-surface strain gradient or a stacking-fault region gives the same fourfold amplitude pattern with an arbitrary phase δ′. In centrosymmetric bulk, photon drag (j_i ∝ T_ijkl q_j E_k E_l with q ∥ ĉ) and electric-quadrupole rectification are allowed and give in-plane sources of the same form; the authors' own theory invokes intra-cell electric quadrupoles (Fig. S6b). At normal incidence an in-plane P_x and an in-plane M_y radiate the same detected x-polarized field, so detecting one THz component cannot separate them. "Surface" versus "bulk" is also not sharply defined: at 1.2 eV the penetration depth is only tens of nanometres, inside the region where surface fields and band bending act. The two exclusion arguments on p. 2 are not decisive. Argument (i), that a surface term "would not track the bulk magnetic ordering", fails because the signal itself barely tracks the order (concern 3). Argument (ii), linear versus saturating fluence scaling, fails because surface emitters (surface-field screening, photo-Dember, shift currents) routinely saturate at 0.1–1 mJ/cm² through carrier screening, absorption bleaching and heating. No measurement probes the magnetic character of the source, even though the 45° data were taken in a magneto-optical cryostat (OptiCool, SI Sec. II.A). The authors should add discriminating measurements, most importantly: (a) emission versus applied magnetic field (an out-of-plane field of both signs that saturates M_z; an in-plane field along y that saturates M_y and should quench a dM_y response; field reversal), which a pure electric-dipole source would not follow; (b) the orthogonal THz component E_y versus φ, predicted by Eqs. IV.63–IV.64 to follow sin(2φ − 2δ) with equal amplitude; (c) the signed peak amplitude, or waveforms at φ and φ + 90°, to show the predicted sign change between adjacent lobes (Fig. 2d,e plot only magnitudes); (d) front- versus back-side excitation or a thickness series, which separates photon drag (odd in q) and surface dipoles from a bulk magnetization; (e) circularly polarized pumping, for which the symmetric E⊗E term of Eq. (2) predicts no in-plane M_y at normal incidence; and (f) a non-magnetic or isostructural reference under the same conditions. Without at least the field dependence, the headline claim of magnetic-dipole radiation from a light-induced magnetization is unsupported.

2. **There is no direct evidence that the ferromagnetic order is manipulated.** The title and abstract claim "manipulation of ferromagnetism" and "ultrafast optical control of ferromagnetism" (also pp. 4–6), but the only observable is the far-field THz waveform. There is no time-resolved Kerr or Faraday measurement of the light-induced M_y and no dependence of the emission on an external field; Fig. S2 shows that about 0.1 T saturates M_z at 2 K, so a field test is easy in the existing setup. The emission persists, with an unchanged pattern and larger amplitude, at 80 K and 200 K (Fig. 2e,f), well above T_c, which shows that ferromagnetic order is not needed for the effect. Even if the emission is magnetic-dipole in nature, the radiating moment could be the transient itinerant spin density of the photocarriers (the NLEE spin itself, Eq. IV.55) rather than the localized Cr³⁺ moments; the paper does not distinguish these. The authors should measure field-dependent THz emission at fixed temperature with out-of-plane (both polarities) and in-plane fields, and ideally a time-resolved magneto-optical measurement under the same fluence, polarization and temperature showing a d-wave, polarization-dependent ΔM of the Cr sublattice. If the field dependence is absent or weak, the claims should be narrowed to polarization-dependent THz emission whose origin is consistent with, but not proven to be, a nonlinear Edelstein mechanism acting on the order parameter.

3. **The temperature dependence runs opposite to what the proposed mechanism predicts at the experimental fluence, and is too sparse to test the model.** In Fig. 2e,f the emission at 100 µJ/cm² (φ = 60°) is largest in the paramagnetic phase: it rises from T_c up to about 180 K, and at 200 K (about 3T_c) it is roughly twice its 7 K value. The feature at T_c is a modest change in slope, with error bars of ±0.1–0.15 on a ~0.5–0.9 scale. If the signal is M_y = χ_∥(T)·H^EZ, it should fall above T_c as the susceptibility falls: the low-field model gives an in-plane susceptibility constant below T_c and decaying as 1/(T − T_c/(1+κ)) above it (Eq. V.23, Fig. S7b), and the measured low-field M(H) slopes at 150 K and 300 K are far smaller than at 80 K (Fig. S2). The SI reproduces the rise only in the high-fluence regime (I_0 = 5T_c⁰, Figs. S8–S9), an effective field at the exchange scale (tens to ~100 T), whereas 100 µJ/cm² lies in the nearly linear part of the measured fluence dependence (Fig. 4c). The unchanged four-lobe pattern at 7, 80 and 200 K is at least as naturally explained by a magnetism-independent emitter modulated by temperature-dependent absorption, carrier lifetime, gap shifts or THz index; a surface χ(2) that changes with the band structure or magnetostriction would also show a kink at T_c. The data are also sparse: full φ-maps exist at only three temperatures, and the fluence dependence only at 7 K. The authors should give temperature dependences at several fluences including the lowest usable one, fluence series at 80 K and 200 K, and a finer scan through T_c; compare the amplitude quantitatively with the measured χ(T) and M(T) (Figs. S1–S2); explain the rise within the model using the same parameters that fit the fluence data; and explain why the paramagnetic signal exceeds the ferromagnetic one. As written, argument (i) for excluding surface mechanisms is not supported by the data.

4. **The 45° interpretation (competition between M_y and M_z) conflicts with magnetic-dipole selection rules, the domain state and oblique-incidence optics.** The polarity reversal, 90° rotation of the twofold pattern and spectral-weight minimum near 0.2 mJ/cm² (Figs. 3b–d, 4d,e; p. 6) are presented as evidence of fluence-driven reorientation of the magnetization. First, Fig. 3a and Fig. S3 state that p-polarized THz is detected (wire-grid along lab x, in the plane of incidence). A magnetic dipole radiates E ∝ n × m̈; for emission in the plane of incidence, m_z (and m_x) radiate purely s-polarized light and only m_y radiates p, so an M_z term cannot interfere with M_y in the detected channel. The model quantity |M_y − M_z| in Fig. 4d, with equal weights and no emission-geometry, refraction or Fresnel factors, has no basis, and the position of the minimum depends on that weighting. Moreover, Fig. 4d uses the total M, which starts at 1 at zero fluence because of the static M_z; a static moment does not radiate, and the quantity to compare is ΔM(I_0) = M(I_0) − M(0) projected onto the detection geometry. Second, Cr2Ge2Te6 is a soft ferromagnet with negligible coercivity (Fig. S2). At zero field a bulk crystal is multidomain, and over a 1.9 mm spot ±M_z domains average to nearly zero; since an in-plane field reduces |M_z| in both domain types, the induced change in M_z has opposite sign in the two and cancels. Figs. 4d and S9 assume a single domain starting from M_z = 1, and the paper does not say whether a field was applied. Third, with n ≈ 3–4 at 1.2 eV the internal angle for 45° incidence is only about 10–15°, strongly reducing the E_z² terms in Eqs. (3), (V.38) and (V.39). The absorbed fluence and internal field also differ for s and p pump polarization, so polarization-isotropic mechanisms (photo-Dember, surface-field screening, ultrafast demagnetization with a canted moment), which radiate p-polarized THz, acquire a twofold φ-dependence; the statement that these are "largely isotropic with respect to pump polarization" (p. 3) ignores this. A fluence-dependent competition between such an absorption-weighted isotropic term and the anisotropic term would produce the rotation, reversal and minimum without any M_y/M_z competition. Fourth, the spectrum in Fig. 3d moves from a peak near 0.4–0.5 THz at low fluence to about 1 THz at high fluence, and the high-fluence waveform in Fig. 3b is more oscillatory rather than simply polarity-reversed; this points to two components with different dynamics or a changing magnetic resonance, which a single spectral-weight number does not capture. The authors should state the field conditions and detected polarization; measure s and p THz components separately; repeat the fluence series in a saturating out-of-plane field of both signs; correct for internal angle and Fresnel absorption and report absorbed fluence; decompose the φ-dependence at each fluence into isotropic, cos 2φ and sin 2φ terms and show how each scales; show the waveforms at both φ maxima; discuss the spectral shift; and model the detected field with the correct radiation geometry.

5. **The claimed "quantitative" agreement with the model rests on free scales, and the size of the effect is never checked for plausibility.** The abstract says the polarization, fluence and temperature dependences are "quantitatively captured". In Fig. 4b–e the model fluence axis (I_0 ∝ |E_0|², in units of T_c⁰) is mapped onto mJ/cm² by an arbitrary factor and the y-axes are normalized, so any saturating function would "agree" with Fig. 4c. The parameters are chosen, not fitted or independently fixed: κ = 0.2 (not tied to the measured anisotropy), χ_x,∥ = χ_z,xx = 0.5/T_c⁰, I_0 = 5T_c⁰, and δ_∥ = 50° "chosen to match". "Spectral weight", presumably an integral of |E(ω)|², is compared directly with |M| rather than with a quantity linear in the radiated field. Neither the paper nor the SI estimates H^EZ in tesla, χ(2) or J_sd. At I_0 = 5T_c⁰ the Zeeman energy is about 30 meV, of order 100 T for a 3 µ_B moment, and rotating macroscopic Cr moments by an angle of order one within 1 ps requires fields of order γ⁻¹/τ, tens to hundreds of tesla; nothing links such fields to the photocarrier density at 0.1–1 mJ/cm² (≈10²⁰ cm⁻³ at 0.1 mJ/cm² for a ~50 nm absorption depth) and a realistic J_sd. Nor is the ΔM (fraction of M_s, or µ_B per Cr) needed to radiate the observed field estimated. The authors should state which parameters are fixed and which are fitted; fit the normal-incidence fluence dependence (Fig. 4c), the 45° minimum (Fig. 4e) and the temperature dependence (Fig. 2f) simultaneously with one calibrated I_0 ↔ fluence mapping and stated uncertainties; estimate H^EZ in tesla from the computed χ(2), the carrier density and J_sd and show it reaches the regime the model needs; check through the magnetic-dipole radiation formula that the implied ΔM gives the measured ~0.1–1 V/cm; compare quantities with the same scaling; and soften "quantitatively" if this cannot be done.

B. Should address

6. **A static equilibrium model is used for sub-picosecond dynamics.** The mean-field treatment (Eq. 4, SI Sec. V; Fig. 4b,d) minimizes a static free energy F(M) and assumes M follows H^EZ adiabatically. The drive lasts about as long as the 160 fs pulse or the interband coherence or carrier lifetime, and the radiated far field is set by ∂²M/∂t² on a ~1 ps scale, comparable to the pump plus detection response; substantial rotation of an ordered moment in this time requires precession frequencies of order 1 THz. For M ∥ z, the leading response to a short in-plane field pulse is precessional (δM ∝ M × H), which is rotated by 45° in φ relative to the longitudinal response assumed and is odd in M_z. In the paramagnetic phase, building up a longitudinal M within ~1 ps requires spin relaxation that the model does not include. The paper never explains how the adiabatic M(I_0) maps onto a radiated spectral weight. The authors should provide a time-dependent treatment (LLG, Bloch equations or time-dependent mean field driven by a pulsed H^EZ(t)), state which timescale sets H^EZ(t), and compare the computed ΔM(t) and radiated waveform and spectrum with Figs. 2c, 3b and 3d.

7. **Heating and the effective sample temperature are not addressed, although the mechanism is called "non-thermal".** At normal incidence 0.46 mJ/cm² corresponds to up to 330 mW average power at 50 kHz before chopping (about 25 kHz after) on a 1.9 mm spot (SI Eq. II.1), a load of order 100 mW on a sample nominally at 7 K in exchange gas. Per pulse, 0.46 mJ/cm² (up to 1.5 mJ/cm² at 45°) deposited within the 1.2 eV penetration depth, given the small low-temperature heat capacity, may transiently raise the excited volume above T_c. The two geometries also use different cryostats (Janis exchange gas versus OptiCool closed-cycle) and repetition rates (50 kHz versus 3 kHz). Because the signal grows with temperature (Fig. 2f), steady-state heating or ultrafast demagnetization could produce the sub-linear fluence dependence (Fig. 4c), the polarity reversal (Fig. 3) and blur the temperature dependence; the paper calls the mechanism "unlike … thermal effects" without testing this. The authors should estimate or measure the steady-state and transient temperature rise, for example by varying repetition rate or chopping duty cycle at fixed fluence, measuring fluence series at several base temperatures including above T_c, or tracking a temperature-sensitive signature.

8. **Established opto-magnetic mechanisms and prior work are not discussed.** A magnetization or effective field ∝ E_iE_j from linearly polarized light is phenomenologically the inverse Cotton–Mouton effect or photoinduced magnetic anisotropy; below T_c, impulsive stimulated Raman excitation of magnons and ultrafast demagnetization also act on ferromagnets, and some of these give polarization-dependent torques with the same E⊗E symmetry. The statement that the Edelstein–Zeeman field "provides tensorial control … unlike conventional magneto-optical … effects" (p. 6) needs a direct comparison. The authors should explain which observable, if any, separates the nonlinear-Edelstein origin from these mechanisms (otherwise the novelty lies in the microscopic interpretation, not in a new phenomenon), and state clearly what is new relative to Ref. 8 (nonlinear-optics-driven spin reorientation in ferromagnets) and Refs. 9 and 15 (hidden spin polarization probed optically and by THz emission in centrosymmetric materials).

9. **The microscopic theory is incomplete and not connected to Cr2Ge2Te6.** The tight-binding model (SI Secs. III–IV) is a generic two-sublattice Rashba model with C_n symmetry, not the electronic structure of Cr2Ge2Te6; it places the inversion partners along z (Fig. S5), whereas in Cr2Ge2Te6 the inversion-related Cr pair sits within the buckled honeycomb layer. The argument that ⟨S^(2)⟩ ≠ 0 (Sec. IV.B.4; Eqs. IV.48, IV.52–IV.55) rests on individual trace terms being even under inversion; the time-reversal constraint on a T-odd spin density rectified from T-even linearly polarized light is not analysed. For real absorption in a Kramers-degenerate system, contributions at ±k with opposite spin cancel, so a net result must come from a specific dissipative, resonant coherence term (analogous to the shift current), whose size depends on photocarrier and spin relaxation; the model treats δ⁺ as infinitesimal and declares relaxation "beyond the scope", and no Brillouin-zone integral is actually evaluated. The split into itinerant and localized sectors in the s–d coupling (Eq. V.28) is a strong assumption, since 1.2 eV transitions involve hybridized Cr d / Te p states. None of the six χ(2) components, their ratios (which set δ and the 45° crossover) or their magnitudes is computed for the real material. The authors should evaluate χ(2) numerically for the model and ideally for a DFT-derived Wannier model of Cr2Ge2Te6, explicitly verify T-parity and survival under zone integration, identify the responsible term, and report the magnitude and photon-energy dependence; data at a second pump energy would test the claimed interband resonance.
