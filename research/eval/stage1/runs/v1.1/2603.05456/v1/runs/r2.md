Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper reports THz emission from bulk centrosymmetric Cr2Ge2Te6 under 1.2 eV pumping. It attributes the emission to magnetic dipole radiation from a magnetization driven by a light-induced "Edelstein–Zeeman" field, which arises from a resonant interband nonlinear Edelstein effect at locally non-centrosymmetric Cr sites. It supports this with a point-group analysis, a minimal two-sublattice Rashba model and a mean-field Heisenberg/Landau description of how the field competes with uniaxial anisotropy. If the assignment were established, a non-thermal, polarization-tunable optical handle on magnetization in centrosymmetric magnets would be of broad interest. As it stands, the experiment shows a polarization-dependent THz emission signal. It does not show that this signal comes from a magnetization, still less from manipulation of the ferromagnetic order.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The idea is timely: hidden spin texture plus resonant second-order response may give an effective field acting on local moments in globally centrosymmetric magnets. The symmetry analysis for point group 3̄ (Eq. 2–3, Table S1, Eq. IV.62–IV.66) is clear and gives a concrete, testable angular form.
- The normal-incidence data show a clean fourfold |E_THz|(φ) pattern whose orientation does not change with fluence or temperature (Fig. 2d,e). This matches the predicted cos(2φ − 2δ) form, with the axis set by the material rather than locked to a crystal axis.
- The 45° data show a striking fluence-driven polarity reversal, a 90° rotation of the twofold pattern and a minimum in spectral weight (Fig. 3b–d, Fig. 4e), which any explanation must account for.
- The Supplementary Information gives a detailed and largely self-contained derivation of the interband spin response and of the mean-field magnetic response.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. **Electric-dipole and other non-magnetic mechanisms give the same normal-incidence signature and are not excluded.** The main evidence for a magnetic-dipole, nonlinear-Edelstein origin is the fourfold φ-dependence with a non-universal angle δ (Fig. 2d,e; Eq. 3). That form is not unique to an axial-vector response in 3̄:
   - At the surface the symmetry drops to the polar group 3 (C3). There, in-plane surface optical rectification or surface shift current gives P_x, P_y ∝ cos/sin(2φ − 2δ′), the same fourfold |E_THz| pattern with an arbitrary phase.
   - In the bulk, photon drag (j_i ∝ T_ijkl q_j E_k E_l with q ∥ ĉ) is allowed in a centrosymmetric crystal. It also has trigonal in-plane components that give the same pattern.
   - At normal incidence an in-plane electric dipole P_x and an in-plane magnetic dipole M_y radiate the same detected x-polarized field. Detecting only one THz polarization component cannot separate them.

   The two arguments on p. 2 against a surface mechanism are not decisive:
   - Argument (i) says a surface contribution "would not track the bulk magnetic ordering". But the signal also barely tracks the ordering. It grows monotonically from 7 K to 200 K (Fig. 2e text, Fig. 2f) and is larger in the paramagnetic phase than in the ferromagnetic phase. A gradual temperature dependence can come from absorption, carrier lifetime, gap shifts, or THz absorption and index changes in the crystal.
   - Argument (ii) says a surface contribution would scale linearly with fluence. Saturation with fluence is generic for photocarrier-driven emission (absorption saturation, screening, heating). It also occurs below the fluences at which the claimed magnetic saturation sets in.

   This is the central claim of the paper, so the authors need measurements that separate magnetic-dipole radiation from electric-dipole and photon-drag currents, for example:
   - flip the sample, or compare front- and back-side excitation. Photon drag reverses with q, and a surface dipole reverses on going from the top to the bottom surface; the bulk magnetic-dipole term transforms differently;
   - measure both THz polarization components as a function of φ;
   - measure the dependence on sample thickness;
   - most directly, show the dependence on an applied magnetic field (see concern 2).

2. **No direct evidence that the ferromagnetic order is manipulated.** The title and abstract claim "manipulation of ferromagnetism" and "ultrafast optical control of ferromagnetism", but no measurement probes the magnetization.
   - There is no time-resolved Kerr or Faraday measurement of the light-induced M_y.
   - There is no dependence of the emission on an external field, even though the oblique-incidence data were taken in a magneto-optical cryostat (OptiCool, SI Sec. II.A). A signal that couples to the ordered moment should change when M_z is saturated up or down by a small out-of-plane field. Fig. S2 shows that about 0.1 T suffices at 2 K. It should also change when M is pulled in-plane.
   - The polarity-reversal explanation at 45° (p. 6) relies on competition with a "frozen easy-axis component" M_z. SI Fig. S2 shows a soft ferromagnet with negligible coercivity. At zero field a bulk crystal will therefore be multidomain, with ±M_z domains averaging to nearly zero over a 1.9 mm spot. In the model the change in M_z produced by an in-plane field has opposite sign in +M_z and −M_z domains (both decrease |M_z|). Its contribution to the emitted field would then cancel in a multidomain sample.
   - The emission persists, with an unchanged pattern and larger amplitude, at 80 K and 200 K (Fig. 2e,f), well above T_c ≈ 67 K. This shows that ferromagnetic order is not needed for the effect.

   The authors should do the following:
   - field-dependent THz emission at fixed temperature, including reversing a saturating out-of-plane field;
   - the same measurement with an in-plane field;
   - ideally, a time-resolved magneto-optical measurement that detects the transient in-plane magnetization directly.

   If the field dependence is absent or weak, the claims should be narrowed to a polarization-dependent THz emission whose origin is consistent with, but not proven to be, a nonlinear Edelstein mechanism.

3. **The model–data comparison is not quantitative and is partly inconsistent with what is measured.**
   - The mean-field treatment (SI Sec. V) minimizes a static free energy. It assumes the magnetization reaches equilibrium in the instantaneous Edelstein–Zeeman field. The drive, however, is a ~160 fs pulse.
     - Rotating macroscopic Cr moments by an angle of order one in less than 1 ps needs fields of order γ⁻¹/τ, that is, tens to hundreds of tesla.
     - The calculations use I0 = 5 T_c^0 (Fig. S8, S9), an exchange-scale field.
     - Neither the paper nor the SI estimates the actual magnitude of H^EZ in tesla, the values of χ^(2) or J_sd, or the precessional timescale, so the adiabatic picture is not justified.
     - The paper needs a time-dependent treatment (Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert or a time-dependent mean-field model), or at least an order-of-magnitude estimate. That estimate should show that the field and the magnetization change ΔM it implies can produce the measured THz field.
   - The radiated far field is set by the time derivatives of the transient change ΔM(t), not by the total M. Fig. 4d plots |M_y − M_z| of the total magnetization. That curve starts at 1 at zero fluence because of the static equilibrium M_z, and a static moment does not radiate. The quantity to compare with Fig. 4e is ΔM(I0) = M(I0) − M(0), projected onto the detection geometry.
   - In Fig. 4b,c the fluence axis of the model ("I0 ∝ |E0|²") has an arbitrary scale, and the y-axes are normalized. Any saturating function would then "agree" with Fig. 4c. The authors should state which parameters are fixed and which are fitted, and how fluence is mapped onto I0. They should also show whether one set of parameters reproduces, at the same time, the normal-incidence saturation (Fig. 4c), the 45° minimum at ~0.2 mJ/cm² (Fig. 4e) and the temperature dependence (Fig. 2f).

B. Should address

4. **Heating and the effective sample temperature.**
   - At normal incidence the pulses arrive at 25 kHz after chopping, with up to 330 mW incident before chopping (SI Eq. II.1). That is a time-averaged load of order 100 mW on a cryostat sample nominally at 7 K.
   - Per pulse, 0.46 mJ/cm² (up to 1.5 mJ/cm² at 45°) absorbed within the 1.2 eV penetration depth gives an energy density that, given the small low-temperature heat capacity of Cr2Ge2Te6, may raise the excited volume transiently above T_c.
   - The fluence "saturation" (Fig. 4c) and the polarity reversal (Fig. 3) may therefore reflect ultrafast demagnetization, or steady-state heating towards or through T_c, rather than an Edelstein–Zeeman field. The paper calls the mechanism "non-thermal" and "unlike … thermal effects" without testing this.
   - The authors should estimate the steady-state and transient temperature rise. They should check the emission against repetition rate at fixed fluence, and measure the fluence dependence at several base temperatures, including above T_c.

5. **Analysis of the 45° data.**
   - At oblique incidence the absorbed fluence depends on pump polarization through the s/p Fresnel coefficients. A polarization-isotropic mechanism (photo-Dember, surface-field screening, ultrafast demagnetization with a canted moment) then acquires a twofold φ-dependence.
   - The statement that these mechanisms are "largely isotropic with respect to pump polarization" (p. 3) ignores this.
   - The spectra in Fig. 3d also move from a peak near 0.4 THz at low fluence to near 1 THz at high fluence. This suggests two components with different dynamics, possibly of different origin, whose relative weight changes with fluence. A crossover between two mechanisms would also produce the polarity reversal and the minimum in Fig. 4e.
   - The authors should decompose the φ-dependence at each fluence into isotropic, cos 2φ and sin 2φ terms after correcting for Fresnel absorption, and show how each term scales with fluence. They should show the time-domain waveforms at both φ maxima and discuss the change in spectrum.

6. **The theory does not connect to Cr2Ge2Te6 quantitatively or to established opto-magnetic effects.**
   - The microscopic model (SI Sec. III–IV) is a generic two-sublattice uniaxial Rashba model. It is not a Cr2Ge2Te6 band structure, and it gives no estimate of χ^(2) at 1.2 eV.
   - A rectified spin density from linearly polarized light in a time-reversal-symmetric state must be a dissipative, resonant (time-reversal-odd) response. Its size then depends on the photocarrier relaxation and spin lifetime, but the model treats δ⁺ as infinitesimal and declares relaxation "beyond the scope".
   - Phenomenologically, a magnetization or effective field ∝ E_iE_j from linearly polarized light is the inverse Cotton–Mouton effect, or photoinduced magnetic anisotropy, which is well known in magnets. The statement that the Edelstein–Zeeman field "provides tensorial control … unlike conventional magneto-optical … effects" (p. 6) needs a direct comparison with these established mechanisms. The authors should explain what observable, if any, separates the nonlinear-Edelstein origin from them. Otherwise the novelty is in the microscopic interpretation, not in a new phenomenon.

7. **Magnitude of the emitted field is inconsistent across the paper and is not connected to the claimed mechanism.**
   - The main text describes the emission as "relatively large-amplitude … (several V/cm)" (p. 2).
   - Fig. 2c shows a peak of about 0.8 V/cm, and Fig. 3b shows about 0.4 V/cm.
   - SI Sec. II.C estimates "on the order of 0.1 V/cm".
   - The "unexpectedly large" argument depends on this number. The authors should give one calibrated value, including EO detection, Fresnel and collection corrections. They should then convert it into the ΔM (in μB per Cr) needed for magnetic-dipole radiation, and compare that with the saturation moment.

8. **Temperature and fluence dependences are sparse.**
   - The temperature dependence (Fig. 2f) uses a single fluence (100 μJ/cm²) and a single polarization (φ = 60°). The full φ-maps cover only three temperatures.
   - The fluence dependence is measured only at 7 K.
   - The model makes distinct predictions above T_c: for a paramagnet in a field, the susceptibility falls above T_c at low drive (Eq. V.20), yet the data rise.
   - Fluence dependence at 80 K and 200 K, and a finer temperature scan through T_c at several fluences, would test the model's crossover picture directly. The temperature rise at 100 μJ/cm² should be explained within the model using the parameters that fit the fluence data, not with the separately chosen I0 = 5 T_c^0 of Fig. S8/S9.

9. **Samples, reproducibility and orientation.**
   - The paper does not say how many crystals were measured, or whether the normal- and oblique-incidence data come from the same crystal. They were taken in different cryostats, at different repetition rates (50 kHz and 3 kHz).
   - Device-to-device spread of δ and of the fluence at which the polarity reverses is not reported.
   - The paper does not state how the crystallographic â-axis was determined (Laue, XRD), with what uncertainty, or whether the surface was freshly cleaved. All of these matter for the claim that δ is non-universal and for the surface-mechanism discussion in concern 1.
   - The origin of the error bars in Fig. 2f, 4c and 4e is not given.

C. Minor

10. T_c is quoted as ~66 K (p. 2), 67 K (Fig. 2f) and ≈67 K (SI). The text says |S_THz|max is plotted against temperature, but the axis of Fig. 2f reads "Spectral Weight". The paper should define "spectral weight", including its integration range, and use it consistently with "peak amplitude" (Fig. 3c, 4c,e).
11. SI Sec. I:
    - The text says M(H) shows a "nearly linear response". Fig. S2 at 2–40 K shows a step-like saturation near zero field.
    - The saturation moment (~3.2 μB/f.u.) is about half the ~6 μB/f.u. expected for two Cr3+ per formula unit. The M(T) curve in Fig. S1 saturates at 1.45 μB/f.u. under an unstated field.
    - The Fig. S2 caption reads "300 14K".
    - The authors should check the normalization and state the field and field orientation.
12. Figure captions:
    - The Fig. 4a caption is garbled: "above the Curie temperature T_c and in the presence of the planar…".
    - The gray line in Fig. 4c is not defined.
    - The Fig. 4b legend lists |M_z| and |M_y − M_z|, but only one curve is shown.
    - The low-temperature point in Fig. S9 is 0.104 T_c^0 in the caption and 0.107 T_c^0 in the SI text.
13. Fluence calibration (SI Sec. II.B):
    - The oblique formula uses f = 3 kHz, but Eq. II.2 (0.017·P) implies an effective rate of 1.5 kHz after chopping. The authors should state where P is measured.
    - Fluences are incident rather than absorbed. Absorbed fluence matters when comparing normal and 45° data, given the polarization-dependent Fresnel losses at 45°.
14. Fig. 3b is described as having "comparable amplitudes", while Fig. 3c scales the low-fluence data by ×3. The waveforms differ by roughly a factor of two.
15. Typos: "Kramer's" should be "Kramers'"; "ETHz"/"tdelay" in the Fig. 2c caption; "we find can write" (SI p. S-9).

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Measure the THz emission against an applied magnetic field, out-of-plane and in-plane, reversing a saturating field. This is the single most decisive test of whether the signal couples to the ferromagnetic moment.
2. Separate magnetic-dipole from electric-dipole and photon-drag emission: compare front- and back-side excitation or flip the sample, measure both THz polarization components against φ, and compare crystals of different thickness.
3. Replace the static free-energy minimization with a time-dependent spin model, driven by an H^EZ whose magnitude is estimated from the microscopic model. Compare the computed ΔM(t) and the radiated field quantitatively with Fig. 2–4, using one parameter set.
4. Quantify heating (steady-state and transient) and add fluence series above T_c and at several repetition rates. This would separate the proposed crossover physics from demagnetization and thermal effects.

5. Verdict

Verdict: major revision
