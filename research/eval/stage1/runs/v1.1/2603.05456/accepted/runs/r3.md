Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper reports polarization-dependent THz emission from the centrosymmetric van der Waals ferromagnetic semiconductor Cr2Ge2Te6 under 1.2 eV above-gap excitation. It attributes the emission to magnetic-dipole radiation from magnetization dynamics driven by a light-induced "Edelstein-Zeeman" field, which arises from the second-order nonlinear Edelstein effect. If the mechanism is established, this would be a symmetry-allowed, non-thermal route to optical control of magnetization with linearly polarized light in centrosymmetric magnets, and it would draw broad interest. The experiment is clean and the phenomenology is interesting: a fourfold polarization pattern at normal incidence, a fluence-driven 90° rotation and polarity reversal at 45° incidence, and a change in slope at Tc. The main question is whether the data single out the proposed magnetic mechanism over other second-order radiation sources that are allowed by the same symmetry.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- A sizeable (several V/cm), reproducible THz signal from a nominally centrosymmetric crystal, with a clear fourfold dependence on pump polarization that holds across fluence (Fig. 2d) and temperature (Fig. 2e).
- A non-trivial observation at 45° incidence (Fig. 3): the polarity reverses, the twofold lobe pattern rotates by 90° with fluence, and the spectral weight has a minimum near 0.2 mJ/cm². A simple monotonic scaling mechanism does not easily explain this.
- A compact symmetry analysis for point group 3 (Eqs. 2–3) and a Landau model (Eq. 4) that qualitatively reproduces the saturation at normal incidence and the non-monotonic fluence dependence at 45° incidence (Fig. 4).
- A conceptually appealing link between the nonlinear Edelstein effect, hidden spin texture at locally non-centrosymmetric Cr sites, and the control of slower magnetic order.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The data do not separate bulk magnetic-dipole radiation from other second-order sources with the same symmetry. The headline claim is that the THz is magnetic-dipole radiation from the magnetization (abstract: "we directly observe magnetic dipole radiation"). The evidence is the polarization pattern and the temperature dependence. Neither is discriminating.
   - Polarization pattern. In point group 3, an in-plane polar vector driven by E⊗E (for example a surface or interface electric dipole, or a shift current, on a cleaved surface whose symmetry is reduced to C3) has the same form, P_y ∝ χ1(Ex² − Ey²) + χ2(2ExEy). This gives the same |cos(2φ − 2δ)| pattern that is not locked to the crystal axes. The argument on p. 3 that the surface lacks a mirror and so "would not lock the surface dipole emission to the crystal axes" applies equally to the bulk axial-vector response. The pattern therefore cannot tell the two apart.
   - Electric quadrupole. Bulk electric-quadrupole radiation is the same multipole order as magnetic-dipole radiation and is also allowed in centrosymmetric crystals. The authors themselves invoke "intra-cell electric quadrupole transitions" (p. 5). It is not separated from the magnetic-dipole channel.
   - Temperature. The signal persists with the same pattern at 80 K and 200 K, and changes only gradually across Tc (Fig. 2f). So the temperature dependence does not show that the emitter is magnetic.
   - Fluence. The argument that a surface contribution would scale linearly with fluence (p. 2) is weak. Sublinear scaling and saturation (Fig. 4c) are generic for above-gap optical rectification and photocurrents, through absorption bleaching, carrier screening and two-photon absorption.

   The authors should provide at least one discriminating test. Options include:
   - thickness dependence, or transmission versus reflection emission geometry, which distinguishes bulk from surface;
   - sample rotation about the surface normal at fixed pump polarization, together with detection of both THz polarization components, to check that E_THz ∝ M × n with the M_x/M_y ratio predicted by Eq. 2;
   - a quantitative estimate showing that a plausible δM (in μB per Cr) radiates several V/cm through the magnetic-dipole channel, compared with the expected size of surface electric-dipole emission;
   - a comparison with a non-magnetic isostructural or centrosymmetric reference measured under matched conditions.

2. There is no direct evidence that ferromagnetic order is being manipulated. The title and abstract claim "manipulation of ferromagnetism". The links to ferromagnetism are a modest change in slope of |S_THz|max at Tc (Fig. 2f, about 30% suppression) and the 45° crossover, which is interpreted through competition with the easy-axis M_z.
   - No external magnetic field was applied, although the Methods list a magneto-optical cryostat (OptiCool).
   - The sample's domain state below Tc is not discussed. In zero field, an easy-axis ferromagnet is generally multidomain with ±M_z. The Landau picture in Fig. 4a, where the equilibrium M_z is canted into the plane, would then give contributions from opposite domains that partly cancel at 45° incidence.

   The authors should repeat the 45° incidence measurements after field-cooling in ±H along c, and as a function of in-plane and out-of-plane field. If the M_z-derived component is tied to ferromagnetic order, it should reverse sign with the magnetization, and the crossover fluence should shift with field-tuned anisotropy. A time-resolved Kerr or Faraday measurement of the light-induced magnetization (the authors mention XMCD only as future work) would provide the direct evidence the title implies.

B. Should address

3. The temperature dependence is not compared with the model. Fig. 2f shows the THz amplitude decreasing on cooling, with a faster drop below Tc. The text explains this qualitatively as easy-axis anisotropy competing with the in-plane Edelstein-Zeeman field (p. 5), but no calculated T dependence is shown. In addition:
   - Temperature-dependent optical absorption at 1.2 eV, THz refractive index and absorption, and the known magneto-optical changes of the Cr2Ge2Te6 band structure across Tc could all modulate the emitted amplitude without any magnetic mechanism.
   - The authors should overlay the mean-field prediction on Fig. 2f with stated parameters.
   - They should correct or normalize for the temperature-dependent pump absorption and THz out-coupling, or show that these are flat.
   - The error bars come from varying the Fourier-transform window, not from repeated measurements. Repeatability across scans and cooldowns should be reported.

4. The two-component superposition test in Fig. 3d is not well posed. The text states that the 0.4 mJ/cm² waveform "closely overlaps" with E_0.1 + E_0.75, the unscaled sum of the waveforms at two other fluences. In the proposed model, the M_y and M_z contributions each depend on fluence (Fig. 4d). There is no reason the raw traces at 0.1 and 0.75 mJ/cm² should add to the trace at 0.4 mJ/cm² without fluence-dependent weights, so the agreement may be coincidental.
   - The authors should fit the full fluence series with two fixed temporal basis waveforms and fluence-dependent amplitudes (for example by SVD or global fitting).
   - They should show that the extracted amplitudes follow the M_y and M_z curves of Fig. 4d.
   - The polarity reversal at 45° could also come from two non-magnetic mechanisms with different fluence scaling, for example an out-of-plane surface photocurrent, which becomes accessible at oblique incidence through E_z, competing with in-plane rectification. A global decomposition combined with field dependence (concern 2) would address this.

5. The comparison between model and data is only qualitative. In Fig. 4b and 4d the fluence axis is "I0 ∝ |E0|²" in arbitrary units, and the parameters r_z, r_0, u_z, g, J and χ(2) are not stated in the main text. The agreement in Fig. 4c and 4e is therefore one of shape.
   - The authors should list the parameters, say which are fitted and which are fixed, and state how H^EZ is scaled to fluence.
   - They should test whether a single parameter set reproduces Fig. 4c, Fig. 4e and Fig. 2f together. In particular, does the crossover fluence at 45° (about 0.2 mJ/cm²) follow from parameters constrained by the normal-incidence saturation?
   - A saturating function (gray line in Fig. 4c) fits many sublinear mechanisms, so the normal-incidence agreement alone is weak support.

6. The "resonant" and "non-thermal" assignments are untested.
   - "Resonant" (abstract) implies a dependence on photon energy, but only 1.2 eV is used. A sub-gap pump, or at least two photon energies, would test whether the response tracks interband absorption, as the Edelstein mechanism requires.
   - At fluences up to 1.5 mJ/cm² above the gap, transient heating of the electrons and lattice is substantial, and in Cr2Ge2Te6 (Tc ≈ 66 K) could approach or exceed Tc locally. The authors should estimate the temperature rise per pulse and the steady-state heating at their repetition rate (not stated), and discuss how this affects the 7 K data and the role of the "frozen easy-axis component".
   - The polarization dependence argues for a non-thermal driving term. A thermal demagnetization contribution to the M_z channel at 45° incidence is still not excluded.

7. The paper does not distinguish the nonlinear Edelstein effect from other second-order magneto-optical routes. The inverse Cotton-Mouton effect and impulsive stimulated Raman excitation of magnons also couple E⊗E to the magnetization in time-reversal-symmetric media. The paper says the inverse Cotton-Mouton effect "requires time-reversal symmetry breaking" (p. 1–2), yet the signal is present well above Tc. The authors should explain which observable separates a dissipative, interband-coherence-driven nonlinear Edelstein response from these alternatives, for example the sign and phase of α(2), the dependence on photon energy, or the temporal profile relative to the pump envelope.

8. Sample information is missing. The paper does not state how many crystals were measured, their thickness, whether surfaces were freshly cleaved, how long they were exposed to air (Cr2Ge2Te6 surfaces degrade), or whether the 45° crossover was reproduced on a second sample or after thermal cycling. Because surface contributions are a leading alternative explanation (concern 1), the authors should report surface preparation and show the key Fig. 2d and Fig. 3c results on at least two samples.

C. Minor

9. Tc is quoted as "~66 K" in the text and "Tc = 67 K" in Fig. 2f. The authors should use one value and state how it was determined, for example by magnetization measurement on the same crystal.

10. In Eq. 3 the left side is M_y, but the coefficients carry subscript x (α(2)_{x,xy}, α(2)_{x,xx}, α(2)_{x,yz}). The authors should check the index convention against Eq. 1 and Eq. 2.

11. The fluence ranges in Fig. 4c (up to about 0.45 mJ/cm²) and Fig. 4e (up to 1.5 mJ/cm²) differ, and the normal-incidence data do not extend to the fluences where the 45° crossover appears. The authors should extend Fig. 4c, or explain the difference.

12. The repetition rate, spot size, and the fraction of pump power absorbed are not given in the Methods. These are needed to convert fluence to excitation density and to estimate heating.

13. The polar plots in Fig. 2d and Fig. 3c multiply the low-fluence data by 3, while the sign of the waveform is lost in the amplitude representation. Signed polar plots would make the polarity reversal at 45° visible in Fig. 3c.

14. The Supplementary Information is cited for central steps: the symmetry-allowed coefficients, the surface-symmetry argument, the mean-field theory and the hidden spin texture. The main text should give at least the key equations and parameter values for the mean-field model.

15. Reference 35 is given as Cr2Si2Te6 in the text and CrSiTe3 in the reference list. Reference 33 is written "Tinkham, M. \mmfnt" and similar entries contain a LaTeX artifact.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Apply an external magnetic field. The most direct way to tie the THz emission to the ferromagnetic order is to apply ±H along c and in-plane at 45° incidence and show that the M_z-derived component reverses with magnetization.
- Detect both THz polarization components and rotate the sample azimuthally, to test the full vector relation E_THz ∝ M × n predicted from Eq. 2.
- Add a photon-energy dependence (sub-gap versus above-gap) and a thickness or geometry dependence, to support the "resonant" and "bulk" assignments.
- Do a global fit of the fluence and temperature series with one parameter set of the Landau and mean-field model, and give an order-of-magnitude estimate of the induced δM and H^EZ.

5. Verdict: major revision
