Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper reports THz emission from the centrosymmetric van der Waals ferromagnet Cr2Ge2Te6 under 1.2 eV near-resonant excitation. It attributes the emission to magnetic-dipole radiation from Cr magnetization driven by a light-induced "Edelstein-Zeeman field" generated by the nonlinear (second-order, time-reversal-even) Edelstein effect. If the assignment holds, it would be a new and general non-thermal route to optical control of magnetization in centrosymmetric magnets with linearly polarized light, which would interest the ultrafast-magnetism and nonlinear-response communities. The data (polarization, incidence, fluence and temperature dependence of the emitted THz field) are interesting. However, the link between the emission and a change in the Cr magnetization is inferred rather than measured.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- A clear, symmetry-based framework (point group -3, Eqs. 2-3) predicts which magnetization components linearly polarized light can drive in Cr2Ge2Te6, and the measured fourfold |cos(2φ − 2δ)| pattern at normal incidence (Fig. 2d,e) agrees with it.
- A striking observation at 45° incidence: as fluence increases, the THz waveform reverses polarity and the twofold polarization pattern rotates by 90° (Fig. 3b-d), with a minimum in spectral weight at an intermediate fluence (Fig. 4e). Any mechanism must explain this.
- A simple Landau model with a competition between the in-plane and out-of-plane components (Eq. 4, Fig. 4a,b,d) gives an intuitive explanation of saturation at normal incidence and of the non-monotonic 45° response.
- The idea is broadly applicable to materials with locally non-centrosymmetric sites and hidden spin texture.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. There is no direct evidence that the THz emission comes from the magnetization or that the Cr magnetization is changed at all. The title and abstract claim "manipulation of ferromagnetism" and "directly observe magnetic dipole radiation arising from optically driven magnetization dynamics". The only evidence is indirect: the shape of the polarization patterns, a modest change in the temperature dependence near Tc (Fig. 2f), and the fluence dependence at 45° (Figs. 3, 4c,e). The Methods state that the setup includes a magneto-optic cryostat (OptiCool), but the paper shows no measurement under an applied magnetic field. The standard tests for a magnetic-dipole origin of THz emission are missing:
   - (i) the emitted waveform at 45° incidence under +B and −B along c, and after field-cooling in ±B. The term in the emitted field that comes from Mz, E ∝ n_y Mz, should reverse sign or change, while the in-plane-driven My term at normal incidence should not;
   - (ii) the dependence on in-plane field, which should change the competition between the anisotropy and the in-plane field that the model invokes;
   - (iii) ideally, a time-resolved Kerr or Faraday measurement in the same pump geometry showing that a magnetization change follows the pump polarization as |cos(2φ − 2δ)|.

   Without at least (i) and (ii), the central claim (that light manipulates ferromagnetism, rather than producing a polarization-dependent rectification signal) is not established. The authors should add the field-dependent emission data. They should also state the magnetic history of the sample: zero-field-cooled or field-cooled, and the domain state within the beam spot.

2. Other second-order rectification mechanisms are not excluded, and the arguments given do not separate them from the proposed one.
   - Polarization pattern: the paper itself states (p. 3) that the surface has no mirror symmetry, so a surface electric-dipole shift or rectification current also gives a fourfold |cos(2φ − 2δ)| pattern that is not locked to a crystal axis. A bulk electric-quadrupole or magnetic-dipole rectification that does not involve the Cr magnetization has the same transformation properties, since E⊗E → Ag ⊕ 2Eg. The polarization pattern therefore does not identify the mechanism.
   - Temperature dependence: the argument rests on Fig. 2f. There the emission is largest in the paramagnetic phase and nearly flat from ~70 K to 200 K, and decreases by only ~30-40% below Tc. Cr2Ge2Te6 has strong magneto-elastic and magneto-optical changes at Tc (band-edge shift, magnetostriction). Any non-magnetic rectification resonant near 1.2 eV would therefore also change across Tc. A paramagnetic induced moment M = χ H_EZ would also be expected to show a Curie-Weiss enhancement near Tc and to fall off above it, and this is not observed.
   - Fluence dependence: the claim that a surface contribution "would scale linearly with pump intensity" (p. 2) is not a discriminator. Saturable absorption, carrier screening and saturation of the interband coherence make any resonant rectification sublinear at 0.1-1.5 mJ/cm². The normal-incidence data in Fig. 4c are close to linear below ~0.2 mJ/cm² anyway.

   The authors should (a) measure an isostructural or closely related non-magnetic or differently ordered reference under matched conditions, or failing that, give a quantitative estimate of the expected surface and quadrupole rectification amplitudes; (b) compute the temperature dependence that their own model predicts (r_z ∝ T − Tc) and compare it with Fig. 2f; and (c) combine these with the field tests in concern 1.

3. The time scale of the proposed dynamics and the static Landau model are inconsistent, and no magnitude is given for H_EZ. The emitted field has ~1 ps duration and spectral weight at 0.3-1.5 THz (Fig. 3d). Magnetic-dipole radiation requires d²M/dt² on that time scale. The model, however, obtains M by minimizing a static free energy F(M) (Eq. 4) at each fluence, i.e. it assumes the Cr moments follow H_EZ adiabatically.
   - In Cr2Ge2Te6, magnetization reorientation driven by a Zeeman-like torque proceeds by precession at γH_eff (anisotropy fields ~0.5 T give FMR in the tens of GHz). Rotating M "completely into the plane" (p. 5) within ~1 ps would require fields of order tens to hundreds of tesla.
   - The paper gives no estimate of J, χ(2) or the resulting H_EZ in tesla, and no estimate of the induced δM that would be needed to radiate the measured several V/cm.
   - Fig. 4b,d use arbitrary units for both M and fluence (I0 ∝ |E0|²), and the gray curve in Fig. 4c is not specified (fit or prediction, and which parameters are free). Yet the abstract says the dependences are "quantitatively captured".

   The authors should (a) give the order of magnitude of H_EZ and δM implied by the measured THz amplitude; (b) replace or support the static minimization with a Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert or time-dependent Landau calculation showing that the Cr moments respond on the ps time scale; (c) state which parameters (r_z, r_0, u_z, g, and the fluence-to-H_EZ conversion) are fitted and which are fixed; and (d) consider whether the radiating moment is instead the itinerant photocarrier spin/orbital density itself. That alternative would also explain the weak temperature dependence, but it would not amount to "manipulation of ferromagnetism".

B. Should address

4. Possible net Mz at zero field and polarization-independent backgrounds at 45° incidence. The fluence-driven rotation of the 45° pattern is attributed to competition between My (driven by H_EZ) and Mz (the equilibrium moment). However, at zero field Cr2Ge2Te6 is a soft magnet with labyrinth or stripe domains, so the net Mz averaged over the beam spot should be small unless the sample was field-cooled. In addition, ultrafast demagnetization of any net Mz, and photo-Dember or surface-field currents, give polarization-independent p-polarized emission at oblique incidence. An isotropic term competing with a cos(2φ) term produces a two-lobed pattern whose apparent orientation changes as their relative weight changes with fluence. This would mimic Fig. 3c without H_EZ rotating M. The authors should fit the 45° data in Fig. 3c (and at all fluences in Fig. 3d) to a Fourier decomposition A0 + A2 cos(2φ − 2δ2) + A4 cos(4φ − …), and report how each component depends on fluence and field.

5. Only one projection of the emitted field is detected. Only the p-component along a is measured (Fig. 2b, 3a). The symmetry analysis (Eqs. 2-3) predicts the full in-plane vector (Mx, My), and hence both Ey and Ex at normal incidence, with fixed relative phases set by the α coefficients. Measuring the orthogonal THz polarization component (or doing full THz polarimetry) would test whether the emitted field transforms as M × n of an axial vector with the predicted coefficient relations. That would be a much sharper test than one fourfold pattern.

6. The crystal-axis reference and polarization artifacts are not controlled. The pump polarization is rotated with a half-wave plate. Small changes in transmitted power, ellipticity or beam pointing with wave-plate angle can produce twofold or fourfold modulations. The paper does not show the complementary experiment: rotating the sample azimuth at fixed polarization. That experiment would also determine whether the pattern is tied to the crystal axes (δ) or to the lab frame. The Methods do not say how the a-axis was determined (Laue, XRD, edge facets), or give the value of δ and its uncertainty. These should be added.

7. Heating and the effective spin temperature are not discussed. Fluences up to 1.5 mJ/cm² at 1.2 eV are used at a base temperature of 7 K, with Tc ≈ 66 K. The transient electron, lattice and spin temperatures within the optical penetration depth may exceed Tc, especially at high fluence where the 45° pattern rotates. Thermal demagnetization of Mz would then lead to the same "crossover from Mz-dominated to My-dominated" emission as the proposed mechanism. The authors should estimate the absorbed energy density and the peak temperature rise (using heat capacity and absorption depth), and show whether the polarity reversal persists when the equilibrium temperature is raised at low fluence.

8. Samples, reproducibility and error bars. The paper does not say how many crystals were measured, whether the fluence-induced reversal at 45° (Fig. 3) and the temperature dependence (Fig. 2f) were reproduced on more than one sample or spot, or whether they survive thermal cycling. The error bars in Figs. 2f and 4c,e come only from varying the Fourier-transform window (2-3.5 ps or 3-4 ps). These are not statistical uncertainties from repeated scans or laser noise. Figure 2f shows considerable scatter above 100 K, so the "more rapid reduction upon crossing Tc" needs a quantitative significance estimate.

9. Bulk versus surface, and the depth probed. The argument for a bulk origin would be stronger with the optical penetration depth at 1.2 eV, the THz absorption and phase-matching (coherence) length in Cr2Ge2Te6, and the crystal thickness. A thickness dependence, or a comparison of freshly cleaved and aged surfaces, would directly test the surface contribution that the paper argues is small.

10. The relative weights of My and Mz at 45° incidence. The interpretation of Fig. 3 and Fig. 4d,e relies on E_THz,x ∝ n_z My − n_y Mz. For a high-index material (n ≈ 3-4 at 1.2 eV), the refracted pump angle inside the crystal is only ~12-15°. The out-coupling of THz radiated by Mz through the surface also depends on the THz refractive index and the Fresnel factors. The weights used to construct |My − Mz| in Fig. 4d should be derived from these. As plotted, the zero crossing in Fig. 4d depends on an unstated choice of weights and fluence scaling.

11. The spectral changes with fluence are not discussed. In Fig. 3d, the spectral peak shifts from ~0.5 THz at 0.1 mJ/cm² to ~1 THz at 0.75 mJ/cm². If the low- and high-fluence signals came only from two magnetization projections with the same dynamics, their spectra would be similar. The different spectra suggest different temporal responses, possibly different mechanisms. The authors should discuss this, together with the detection bandwidth (CdTe EOS with ~160 fs pulses).

C. Minor

12. Inconsistency between text and Fig. 3. On p. 4 the text says the 0.1 and 0.75 mJ/cm² waveforms at 45° "have comparable amplitudes but opposite polarity". Fig. 3b shows peak amplitudes of roughly 0.1-0.15 versus 0.4 V/cm, and Fig. 3c scales the low-fluence data ×3. The two waveforms also do not look like simple sign inversions of each other. Please rephrase, or show the normalized waveforms overlaid.

13. Fig. 3d does not state the pump polarization angle φ. Its 0.75 mJ/cm² trace differs in shape and timing from the 0.75 mJ/cm² trace in Fig. 3b. Please give φ and the time-zero convention for each panel.

14. Fig. 2f labels the axis "Spectral Weight", but the caption says "THz emission amplitude". Please use one quantity and define it, including the integration range. Tc is given as ~66 K in the text and 67 K in the figure.

15. The superposition test in Fig. 3d (E0.4 ≈ E0.1 + E0.75) has no stated scaling. In any model the intermediate-fluence signal should be a fluence-weighted combination, not an unweighted sum of waveforms taken at other fluences. Please justify the comparison, or fit the coefficients.

16. Fluence values should state the spot sizes of the pump (and probe) and whether they are incident or absorbed fluences. The fluence axes of the model panels (Fig. 4b,d) should be related to the experimental fluence.

17. The magnitude "several V/cm" (p. 2) should be stated as a field at the sample or at the detector, with the collection and EOS calibration used.

18. References 32 and 33 contain LaTeX artifacts ("\mmfnt") and should be corrected.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Add THz emission at 0° and 45° incidence under ±B along c and along the a-axis, and after field-cooling with each sign. This is the most direct way to show that the signal is magnetic-dipole radiation from the Cr magnetization, and that the 45° polarity reversal comes from Mz.
- Add a time-resolved MOKE or Faraday measurement with the same pump, showing a δM that depends on polarization as |cos(2φ − 2δ)|. This would turn "THz emission consistent with" into "manipulation of ferromagnetism".
- Make the model quantitative: estimate H_EZ in tesla and δM in μB per Cr, solve the dynamics (LLG or time-dependent Landau) on the ps time scale, and compute the temperature dependence to compare with Fig. 2f, with all free parameters listed.
- Report harmonic decompositions of the polarization patterns versus fluence and temperature, together with sample-azimuth rotation and an orthogonal THz polarization channel, to separate isotropic backgrounds, surface terms and the predicted bulk axial-vector response.

5. Verdict
