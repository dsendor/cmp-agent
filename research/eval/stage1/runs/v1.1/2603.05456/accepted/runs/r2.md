Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper reports polarization-dependent THz emission from the centrosymmetric van der Waals ferromagnetic semiconductor Cr2Ge2Te6 under 1.2 eV excitation. It attributes the emission to magnetic-dipole radiation from magnetization dynamics driven by a second-order "Edelstein-Zeeman" field, that is, a nonlinear Edelstein spin density generated by linearly polarized light. If established, a time-reversal-even, linear-polarization route to optical control of magnetization in centrosymmetric materials would be of broad interest to ultrafast magnetism and nonlinear optics. The THz emission data are interesting in their own right, especially the fluence-driven polarity reversal and 90° rotation at 45° incidence. However, the link between the signal and the magnetization, and the exclusion of non-magnetic second-order emission channels, rest mainly on symmetry and phenomenological arguments, not on direct tests.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- Clean, well-resolved THz emission from a nominally centrosymmetric crystal, with a systematic dependence on pump polarization, fluence, incidence angle and temperature (Figs. 2–4).
- A non-trivial observation at 45° incidence: the waveform polarity reverses with fluence and the twofold pattern rotates by 90° (Fig. 3b–d). At intermediate fluence there is a minimum in spectral weight, consistent with two competing contributions.
- A symmetry analysis of the allowed second-order magneto-electric tensor for point group 3 (Eqs. 2–3) and a compact Landau description (Eq. 4) that reproduces the qualitative features: saturation at normal incidence, and a non-monotonic response at 45°.
- The proposed mechanism is general and gives testable predictions for other spin–orbit-coupled centrosymmetric materials with locally non-centrosymmetric sites.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. No direct test that the emission tracks the magnetization: no external field, no domain control. The central claim is that the THz signal is magnetic-dipole radiation from the ferromagnetic order parameter M, which is pushed by H_EZ, and that the 45° data reflect competition between a "frozen" out-of-plane M_z and an induced in-plane M_y (pp. 4–6, Fig. 4d). No measurement in the paper varies M directly. The Methods say the sample sits in a magneto-optic cryostat (OptiCool), yet there are no field-dependent data. The sample is presumably zero-field cooled, so the domain state is unspecified. In a multidomain easy-axis ferromagnet, the net M_z averaged over the beam spot should be close to zero. In that case the M_z contribution that the model needs at 45° incidence (n_y M_z in E_THz ∝ M × n) would largely cancel, unless the sample is poled. The paper should show:
   (i) THz waveforms at 45° incidence after field-cooling in +B_z and −B_z, and in-field at ±B. If the low-fluence component comes from M_z, it should reverse sign with the poling direction, while any non-magnetic contribution should not.
   (ii) Waveforms under an in-plane field, which should alter the M_y channel.
   (iii) The same tests above T_c, where the model predicts no M_z contribution.
   Without such data, the attribution to the ferromagnetic order parameter rests only on the polarization symmetry (which does not single out magnetism, see item 2) and on a modest change of slope at T_c in Fig. 2f.

2. Alternative non-magnetic bulk and surface second-order emission channels are not excluded. The paper rules out surface electric-dipole emission because (a) the pattern orientation does not change with fluence or temperature, and (b) the temperature dependence "tracks the magnetic ordering" (p. 3). Neither argument is decisive.
   - For in-plane fields in a threefold-symmetric bulk or surface, any second-order response quadratic in E (polar or axial) has the generic form ∝ cos(2φ − 2δ) with an unconstrained δ. The fourfold |cos(2φ−2δ)| pattern in Fig. 2d,e is therefore what a surface electric dipole, a bulk electric quadrupole (photon-drag-like, q-dependent) or a magnetic dipole response would all produce.
   - Electric-quadrupole and magnetic-dipole second-order responses are both allowed in centrosymmetric R3̄ bulk, and they enter at the same multipole order. The Supplementary Information is said to include "intra-cell electric quadrupole transitions" as part of the microscopic mechanism, but the main text never separates quadrupolar emission from magnetic-dipole emission.
   - Shift- or injection-type currents at the surface, or at depletion fields within the absorption depth, are also possible, as are contributions from carrier-density gradients along c.
   Discriminating measurements are needed:
   - rotating the sample azimuthally at fixed pump polarization, to test whether δ is fixed to the crystal axes or follows the surface;
   - detecting both orthogonal THz components to obtain the full emitted vector;
   - a front- versus back-side excitation comparison, or a thickness dependence (surface and bulk terms scale differently);
   - a circular-polarization and helicity test, to bound inverse-Faraday and injection contributions;
   - the field tests of item 1, which separate any M-independent part.
   Alternatively, the authors could give an order-of-magnitude estimate showing that magnetic-dipole radiation from the inferred δM can account for the observed field of "several V/cm", while the quadrupole or surface terms cannot.

B. Should address

3. The temperature dependence appears to contradict the proposed mechanism. In Fig. 2f the THz spectral weight decreases on cooling and drops further below T_c, while emission at 200 K, well into the paramagnetic phase, is the largest. In the Landau picture the induced in-plane magnetization above T_c is δM_y ≈ χ_⊥ H_EZ, with χ_⊥ ∝ 1/(T − T_c), so the response should be largest near T_c and decrease on warming. The data show the opposite trend. The text describes this as a "crossover" (p. 5) but does not compute the temperature dependence the model predicts. The authors should calculate the model's T dependence at fixed H_EZ and compare it with Fig. 2f, including any temperature dependence of the 1.2 eV absorption, the gap and the carrier lifetime. Otherwise they should state which temperature-dependent ingredient makes the emission grow at high T. If absorption changes dominate, the T dependence cannot be used as evidence for magnetic origin.

4. The timescale is inconsistent with "driving the slower dynamics of the ferromagnetic moment". The Introduction, Fig. 1 and the Discussion describe H_EZ as driving slow, subvalent Cr^3+ magnetic dynamics. Yet the emitted waveforms are single-cycle pulses about 1 ps long, with spectra centred at 0.5–1.2 THz (Fig. 3d). This is far above the magnon/FMR frequencies of Cr2Ge2Te6, which are of order tens of GHz, and close to the duration of the pump and the detection bandwidth. Magnetic-dipole radiation scales as d²M/dt². For the localized moments to radiate on sub-picosecond timescales, they would have to tilt within the pump pulse. The paper should address the following:
   - Which magnetization radiates: the itinerant photo-induced spin density or the Cr moments.
   - What sets the emission bandwidth.
   - How a quasi-static minimization of F(M) (Eq. 4) connects to a THz-frequency radiated field. An LLG-type or time-dependent treatment, or at least a timescale estimate, is needed.
   - Whether any slower, GHz-scale component appears at longer delays. The traces shown end at about 2 ps.

5. Heating and absorption saturation are not controlled. At normal incidence the spectral weight saturates (Fig. 4c), and at 45° the response is non-monotonic up to 1.5 mJ/cm² (Fig. 4e), all at a base temperature of 7 K with T_c ≈ 67 K. The paper attributes both behaviours to the H_EZ-driven competition in F(M). Several other effects could produce saturation or a fluence-dependent change in the M_z contribution:
   - absorption bleaching or saturation at 1.2 eV;
   - two-photon absorption;
   - screening of the THz emission by photocarriers;
   - laser-induced demagnetization and heating towards or above T_c. The heating would progressively remove the M_z contribution and so mimic the reversal in Fig. 3.
   The authors should give the absorbed energy density and the estimated lattice and electron temperature rise per pulse, and the repetition rate and the resulting steady-state heating. They should also measure the transmitted or reflected pump fluence to check for absorption saturation, or else show that the fluence dependence at T > T_c (no M_z) differs qualitatively from that at 7 K.

6. The superposition test in Fig. 3d is not quantitatively justified. The 0.4 mJ/cm² waveform is compared with the plain sum E_0.1 + E_0.75 of the waveforms measured at 0.1 and 0.75 mJ/cm². In a two-component picture, each component should scale with fluence in its own way, so the intermediate trace should be a weighted combination with fluence-dependent coefficients, not an unweighted sum of two other measurements. The close match therefore looks partly coincidental. The authors should fit every fluence with a two-component decomposition, aE_A(t) + bE_B(t), and show the extracted a(F) and b(F) against the model's M_y(F) and M_z(F) (Fig. 4d).

7. The model–data comparison is qualitative, but the abstract says "quantitatively captured". Fig. 4b,d use arbitrary fluence units (I_0 ∝ |E_0|²). The main text does not give r_0, r_z, u_z, g or the scale of H_EZ, nor say which parameters were fitted. Fig. 4c shows a grey curve without stating what was fitted, and Fig. 4e has no model overlay at all. The text also assumes, without justification, that the THz spectral weight is proportional to |M_y| or to a linear combination of M_y and M_z. The authors should:
   - list the parameters and how they were fixed, for example from the known anisotropy field and T_c of Cr2Ge2Te6;
   - overlay the model on the data in Fig. 4c and 4e with the same parameters;
   - state whether one parameter set reproduces the normal-incidence and 45° data, and the temperature dependence.
   If not, "quantitatively" in the abstract should be softened.

8. "Resonant" is asserted but not tested. The abstract and text call the effect a "resonant nonlinear Edelstein effect" (1.2 eV is "near-resonant with the direct gap at Γ"), but only one photon energy is used. A pump photon-energy dependence, even two or three wavelengths, or at least a comparison with the absorption spectrum, is needed to support the resonance claim. Otherwise the wording should be changed.

9. The magnitude of the effect is not estimated. There is no estimate of the nonlinear Edelstein susceptibility, the induced δM or the H_EZ that the observed several-V/cm THz field requires, and no comparison with theoretical values of α^(2) for related materials (refs. 4–9). Such an estimate would test whether a magnetic-dipole mechanism can plausibly reach the observed amplitude (see item 2). It would also put a number on "control of ferromagnetism": by how many degrees, or what fraction of M_s, is the magnetization actually tilted?

10. Sample statistics and characterization. The paper does not state how many crystals or spots were measured, the crystal thickness relative to the optical penetration depth, or whether the key observations (the fourfold pattern, and the reversal and 90° rotation at 45°) were reproduced on more than one sample or after thermal cycling. Magnetic characterization of the measured crystals is also missing: M(T) or susceptibility to fix T_c (the text gives "~66 K", Fig. 2f gives 67 K), and the anisotropy field. Please add these, with the device-to-device spread.

11. Error bars. In Figs. 2f, 4c and 4e the error bars are the spread of spectral weight across different Fourier-transform window sizes. This is a systematic of the analysis, not a measurement uncertainty. The paper should also give the statistical uncertainty from repeated scans or noise-floor estimates. The size of the drop at T_c in Fig. 2f is small compared with these bars, so its significance needs to be quantified.

C. Minor

12. Fig. 2d,e: the low-fluence data are multiplied by 3, but the polar plots give no absolute amplitude scale. Please report |S_THz|max in V/cm for each panel, so that the fluence scaling at normal incidence (Fig. 4c) can be checked against Fig. 2d.

13. Eq. 3 and the statement |M_y| ∝ |cos(2φ − 2δ)|: please give the relation between δ and the ratio α_x,xx/α_x,xy explicitly, and report the fitted δ at each temperature and fluence, to support the statement that the orientation is unchanged.

14. In Fig. 3c the low-fluence data are scaled ×3 and the pattern is noisy near the minima. A fit with an uncertainty on the rotation angle would support the claim of a 90° rotation.

15. The definition of "spectral weight" (the integration range in frequency, and the window) should be given in the Methods.

16. Ref. 35 is cited in the text as "Cr2Si2Te6", but the reference title is CrSiTe3. Refs. 32–33 contain a LaTeX artifact ("\mmfnt"), and ref. 42 reads "TThickness". The sample-preparation section should state the molar ratio and the confirmation of phase purity (XRD) for the measured crystals.

17. Fig. 1b shows M_eff(t) and H_eff(t), while the text uses H^EZ and M. Please harmonize the notation.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Add magnetic-field-dependent THz emission measurements (field-cooled ±B_z poling, in-plane field, and a check above T_c) to separate the M-odd component from the M-independent one. This is the most direct test of the central claim.
- Measure both THz polarization components with azimuthal sample rotation, and add a front/back-side or thickness comparison, to separate bulk magnetic-dipole emission from quadrupolar and surface electric-dipole emission.
- Extend the Landau description into a time-dependent (LLG-type) model with stated parameters. Compare it with all of the data in one framework: the fluence dependence at both geometries and the temperature dependence. Include an order-of-magnitude estimate of δM and H_EZ.
- Quantify pump heating and absorption saturation, and add at least one other pump photon energy to support the "resonant" description.

5. Verdict
