A. Must address

1. The 1/√F rise-time scaling in Fig. 4e does not identify excitonic correlations above Tc, the fit cannot tell it apart from other forms, and there is no null measurement (Fig. 4b,e; pp. 9–11; abstract). The paper's headline claim, "direct time-domain evidence for incoherent excitonic correlations" in the normal state, rests entirely on the fluence dependence of the window-iii (Se peak ②) initial response time. There are about four fluences per temperature (0.2, 0.6, 1.8, 2.9 mJ/cm² at 23 K; 0.2, 0.8, 1.5, 1.9 mJ/cm² at 300 K), fitted to τ = a/√F + τ0 with a free offset τ0 = 26 fs, introduced ad hoc as the "time needed for creating mobile Se 4p holes". Three problems follow. (i) Alternatives without excitons. A faster rise at higher excitation density is generic in any semimetal or narrow-gap semiconductor, and 1T-TiSe2 above Tc is one. Carrier–carrier scattering and the cascade of hot carriers created far from EF (1.2–2.5 eV pump) into Se 4p hole states near Γ, density-dependent screening of the free-carrier plasma, band-gap renormalisation, state filling and Pauli blocking, saturation of the absorption change, and a fluence-dependent shift of t0 absorbed into the rise parameter would all produce it. (ii) The fit does not discriminate. With a free prefactor and a free τ0 over about one decade of fluence, 1/√F cannot be separated from 1/F, logarithmic, linear-decreasing or saturating forms. The trend leans on the single 0.2 mJ/cm² point at each temperature (about 190 fs), which has the largest error bar (about ±20–30 fs). The scatter is substantial: the 300 K points are non-monotonic (about 65 fs at 1.5 mJ/cm², about 87 fs at 1.9 mJ/cm²), and the 23 K point at 2.9 mJ/cm² (about 81 fs) lies above the curve. (iii) The proposed mechanism predicts a temperature difference that is not seen. Plasmon screening is set by the total carrier density, n0(T) + αF, not the photoexcited density alone. The equilibrium density in this semimetal changes substantially across Tc and with temperature, so the 23 K and 300 K curves should differ in prefactor or offset. That they collapse onto one curve "within experimental uncertainties" argues against, not for, the specific mechanism. Rough estimates also suggest plasmon periods well below 50 fs at these densities, against measured times of 50–190 fs. The authors should: fit the 23 K and 300 K data separately and report prefactors and offsets with uncertainties; report fits with and without τ0 and compare competing functional forms by χ² or an information criterion; add fluences, especially below 0.6 mJ/cm², where the forms differ most; give a quantitative estimate of the photoexcited and equilibrium carrier density and the resulting plasmon period at each fluence and compare it with the measured times (as in ref. 40); measure the same window and fluence series, analysed identically, on a reference where excitonic correlations are absent or much weaker (an isostructural non-CDW compound such as TiS2 or ZrSe2, a metallic 1T dichalcogenide, Cu-intercalated or otherwise doped TiSe2, or TiSe2 well above 300 K, e.g. ≥400 K if reachable); and, if feasible, use the TDDFT capability already in the paper to simulate the rise time of this feature at several fluences in a model without excitonic interactions. Without such controls, "direct time-domain evidence" should become "consistent with".

B. Should address

2. Window iii is used both as a reporter of long-range order (Fig. 3e) and as a probe blind to long-range coherence (Fig. 4), and the two uses conflict. On p. 9 the Se peak ② transient amplitude "shows a clear onset right at Tc" and is "quasi-constant above Tc", with no fluctuation tail, unlike the ARPES gap and folded-band spectral weight in Fig. 1e,f, which the authors highlight as having one. On p. 10 the same feature is said to be "agnostic about the existence of long-range phase coherence among the excitons", because it reflects localized dipole transitions; this is what licenses reading the 300 K fluence dependence as short-range correlations. If window iii were sensitive to local excitonic pairing, the fluctuations claimed above Tc should give a tail in Fig. 3e. If its amplitude is set by long-range order, the authors must explain why its kinetics would be insensitive to it. The authors should reconcile these readings with a concrete model of what the window-iii amplitude and rise time each measure, plot Fig. 3e and Fig. 1e on a common normalised temperature axis, and report how amplitude and rise time behave together as functions of temperature at fixed low fluence, from about 20 K to the highest accessible temperature. That temperature series is the most direct test of a precursor regime.

3. The order-parameter-like onset in Fig. 3d,e is a single-delay, fixed-window, fixed-incident-fluence quantity and has not been separated from spectroscopic and extrinsic effects (Fig. 3; Fig. 1p; pp. 8–9). The sharp change at Tc in two element-specific windows does show that the transient signal is tied to the CDW, but the specific reading ("additional Se 4p holes created via the breaking of condensed excitons") needs these effects excluded. (a) Delay. The scan is at one delay, 200 fs. In window ii this falls inside the 6 THz A1g oscillation (period about 167 fs), whose amplitude, frequency and damping all change across Tc, so the snapshot samples a temperature-dependent phonon phase. In window iii, the initial response time at 0.2 mJ/cm² is about 150–190 fs (Fig. 4e), so 200 fs lies on the rising edge, and any temperature dependence of the rise time or of time zero during the ramp feeds directly into the plotted amplitude. (b) Fixed window and lineshape. The window-iii transient is derivative-like (negative lobe near 53.2 eV, positive near 53.4 eV at 25 K, Fig. 3c), integrated over a fixed 0.29 eV window (53.04–53.33 eV), while the static peak ② shifts by about 40 meV between 25 and 310 K (Fig. 1p) and narrows on cooling. A fixed-window integral of a derivative-shaped signal is very sensitive to such shifts and widths, so a larger magnitude at 25 K may reflect a sharper static peak rather than more holes. (c) Absorbed fluence. The optical constants of TiSe2 in the 500–1000 nm pump range change across Tc, so the absorbed energy at fixed incident 0.2 mJ/cm² varies with temperature and every transient amplitude can change at Tc for that reason alone. The authors should show full delay traces at several temperatures (at least about 25, 150, 190–200, 210, 250 and 300–310 K) and plot fitted incoherent and phonon amplitudes separately (or the plateau amplitude) against temperature; decompose the transient lineshape into shift, broadening and amplitude components at each temperature, or integrate in a window that tracks the edge; normalise amplitudes to the absorbed fluence at each temperature using measured or literature reflectivity and transmission; show that a non-specific, carrier-dominated window does not display the same onset; and show that the window-ii sign reversal holds at delays other than 200 fs.

6. The fluence scale, the assumed proportionality n ∝ F and the fluence regimes are not established (pp. 7–8, 10; Methods p. 18). The √n argument requires that the excited density be linear in incident fluence. With a 3.4 fs pump, 1.7–2.9 mJ/cm² corresponds to peak intensities of order 10¹² W/cm², where multiphoton absorption and saturation are plausible. The pump penetration depth at 750 nm is comparable to or smaller than the flake thickness, so the transmission probe averages over a depth-graded excitation, which further complicates any scaling with incident F. The regimes are also stated confusingly: 1.7 mJ/cm² is "an order of magnitude above the melting threshold" (p. 7), yet 0.2 mJ/cm² is "commensurate with a fluence regime where the CDW order and excitonic correlations are close to be fully suppressed" (p. 8), leaving unclear whether the Fig. 3 data are perturbative, as reading Fig. 3d,e as a probe of equilibrium order requires. Literature thresholds (refs. 14, 30–34) were measured at other wavelengths, thicknesses and geometries, so comparing incident fluences is not meaningful. The authors should give the absorbed energy density per formula unit and the excited-carrier density (accounting for flake thickness, reflectivity and transmission), the excitation depth profile and its effect on fitted rise times, and the incoherent signal amplitude in each window against fluence to demonstrate linearity; and restate the regimes in absorbed energy, with 0.2 mJ/cm² expressed as a ratio to the electronic and lattice thresholds.

9. The conclusion that excitonic correlations are "instrumental in forming the short-range 2D order in-plane before the long-range CDW is formed" (Discussion p. 11; pp. 5, 9) goes beyond the data. The paper frames the question as whether short-range order above Tc is "purely phononic" or also excitonic. Even accepting the 1/√F argument, the data show at most that some local electron–hole correlation exists above Tc. The measurement has no momentum, dimensional or structural sensitivity, so it cannot tie these correlations to the 2×2 short-range order, or show that they drive rather than accompany lattice fluctuations, rather than being generic correlated electron–hole pairs in a small-gap semimetal. The authors should either link the correlations to the short-range order (e.g. a temperature dependence of τ0 or the prefactor that tracks diffuse-scattering intensity) or restrict the conclusion.

11. Novelty relative to earlier XUV transient absorption on TiSe2 is not delineated (Introduction; Discussion p. 11; abstract). Ref. 22 (Heinrich et al., PRX 2023) reported electronic and structural fingerprints of CDW excitations in XUV transient absorption of 1T-TiSe2 at the same edges, and the present paper relies on it for the static temperature shift and the many-body continuum assignment; ref. 23 reported temperature-dependent hybridisation in core-level spectroscopy. Transient optical and photoemission probes are also well known to show order-parameter-like onsets at Tc in TiSe2. The claims that this is "the first demonstration where an attosecond core-level technique … can simultaneously probe low-energy phase transitions", that core-level spectroscopies are "not traditionally regarded as an appropriate probe for long-range order", and the "unambiguously" wording need reconciling with this work. The authors should state precisely what ref. 22 observed (including any temperature dependence across Tc, amplitude-mode signatures or Ti-edge changes), what is new here (few-fs resolution, simultaneous Ti and Se edges, temperature scans through Tc, the normal-state fluence dependence), and what element-specific core-level data add beyond optical and photoemission probes, and moderate the priority claim.

13. The assignment of the Ti "many-body continuum" and the calculations supporting it are under-documented (pp. 5–6; Methods p. 20; Fig. 1g; Supplementary Note 1, Fig. S3). The broad 38–53 eV feature is assigned to valence excitations accompanying the core transition, based on TDDFT and ref. 22. In early 3d metals the delayed, broadened M2,3 edge is commonly attributed to 3p–3d exchange and multiplet effects (giant resonance with super-Coster-Kronig decay); under that reading, window-ii changes would reflect local 3d occupation or screening rather than CDW-related valence excitations. The Methods describe only ground-state PBE DFT: the TDDFT settings (whether Ti 3p is in the propagation, core-hole treatment, kernel, propagation parameters), spin–orbit treatment, and whether the 24-atom CDW cell is relaxed or experimental, with what distortion and resulting gap, are not given. PBE misdescribes the TiSe2 band overlap and does not by itself stabilise the observed CDW gap, and the comparison with experiment is only qualitative. The authors should describe the TDDFT in the Methods, discuss the multiplet alternative, state the CDW structure and test sensitivity to the functional (e.g. hybrid or DFT+U), and, if feasible, compute the change in windows ii and iii between normal and CDW structures or for a photoexcited distribution, including whether the sign reversal of the continuum signal (Fig. 3d) is reproduced.
