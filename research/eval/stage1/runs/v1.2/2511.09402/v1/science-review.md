A. Must address

1. Measurement artifacts at the size of the effect are not ruled out, and the wavevector differences carry no uncertainties. Where: Figs. 2a–d, 3a, 3c–d, 4d; Methods; Supplementary Table 1. The whole claim rests on differences between q1, q2 and q3 along the three A–H_m directions, quoted as 1.4–2.2 % of |Q_Bragg|. For the field of view of Fig. 1d–g (roughly 80–90 nm), one Fourier pixel is 2π/L ≈ 0.08 nm⁻¹ against |Q_Bragg| ≈ 13 nm⁻¹, i.e. about 0.5–0.6 % of |Q_Bragg|, so the effect is only 2–4 FT pixels. Piezo calibration error, creep, scanner non-orthogonality, nonlinear thermal drift over a multi-hour dI/dV map, and layer-to-layer drift in the multi-energy data set of Fig. 3 routinely distort FT images by 1–3 %. The Methods say a single affine transformation is applied to the Bragg peaks "to restore a perfectly symmetric lattice", but only for Figs. 2b and 4d; it is not stated whether the maps behind Figs. 2a/2c, Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 1 were corrected. An affine correction removes only uniform distortion, and once the Bragg peaks are forced to be symmetric they can no longer serve as an internal symmetry reference. An asymmetric or multi-apex tip can also produce direction-dependent intensities and apparent peak shifts. Fig. 3c makes the problem concrete: near E = 0 the q1 data for the three directions differ by about 0.01 |Q_Bragg| while the horizontal error bars are about 0.005 |Q_Bragg|. The opposite-handedness region (Fig. 2g) was taken "several hundred nanometers away" at a different setpoint (1000 pA, 0.3 mV against 500 pA, 0.5 mV), and it is not stated whether the tip or scan angle changed, so it does not exclude a tip or scan-frame origin. Why it matters: if the asymmetry is instrumental, there is no symmetry breaking to interpret. What to do: state the field of view, pixel count and q-resolution of every map and express the effect in FT pixels; report raw Bragg-peak positions, the affine-correction parameters and the residual Bragg asymmetry after correction for each data set, and show the raw FT next to the corrected one; apply a full Lawler–Fujita drift correction rather than only an affine one; give fit uncertainties for every q_i^m, d12, d13 and d23 and show the three directional values differ beyond them; repeat the same area with the scan frame rotated (for example by 60° or 90°), compare forward and backward scans, and separately repeat after a deliberate tip change, showing that the handedness follows the crystal and not the scan frame or tip; analyse a reference surface known to be C6/C3 symmetric (for example Au(111) QPI) with the same scanner and pipeline and show no comparable asymmetry; and, ideally, image a domain boundary between the two handednesses in one field of view with one tip, presented prominently in the main text.

2. Extrinsic uniaxial strain, or a nematic pinned by it, is not excluded, and it produces exactly the reported symmetry. Where: Fig. 2e–f, Fig. 2g, Discussion, Extended Fig. 6a, Methods (affine transformation). The authors' own model, k_F(θ) = k_F⁰(θ) + η·cos(2θ+φ) with φ = π/2 (i.e. −η·sin 2θ), is a pure l = 2 distortion whose principal axes lie away from the crystalline mirror lines. Any two-fold distortion whose axes avoid the twelve mirror lines θ = nπ/6 breaks all vertical mirrors while keeping C2 (point group C2h), and the two "chiralities" are just the two mirror-related director orientations. A uniaxial strain along a generic in-plane direction, from cleaving, from differential thermal contraction against the holder or epoxy, or from local Ti inhomogeneity, gives three unequal q_i along the three A–H_m directions and two mirror-related "domains", without any electronic instability. A shear of order 0.5 % could plausibly move pocket boundaries by the reported 1.4–2.2 % of |Q_Bragg|. Several observations are consistent with extrinsic pinning: the opposite handedness a few hundred nanometres away fits a director set by random local strain, and the affine correction discards precisely the lattice distortion that would reveal strain. Why it matters: the headline claim is a spontaneous, intrinsic correlated (Pomeranchuk) state, and a symmetry argument alone cannot separate it from strain. What to do: extract the local strain tensor (magnitude and principal axis) from the uncorrected Bragg peaks or Lawler–Fujita displacement fields in every field of view, estimating the part drift cannot account for; compare the strain axis with the fitted director and test for correlation; compile director angle and handedness over many fields of view, cleaves and mountings, and state whether the local Bragg peaks differ in the opposite-handed domain; use DFT or the tight-binding model under strain to estimate how much strain, and at what angle, would reproduce the measured q-vector distortions, and compare with the measured strain; strain-tuned STM would be the most direct test.

11. The model is theoretically incomplete: it ignores the director-pinning term and does not account for the velocity anisotropy. Where: Discussion, Fig. 2e–f, Fig. 3, Extended Fig. 6. For a hexagonal two-component E2g (d-wave) nematic order parameter, the symmetry-allowed cubic (three-state Potts) Landau term pins the director to high-symmetry directions, so a director locked at a generic angle (here 15° from both σv and σv′ lines) needs a microscopic reason or an explicit symmetry-breaking field; the same applies to the proposed intra-unit-cell sublattice charge order (Extended Fig. 6b), where three unequal V densities in a generic ratio are not favoured unless something else breaks the symmetry. Separately, a pure η·cos(2θ+φ) shift of k_F changes Fermi momenta by about 2 % of |Q_Bragg| but no velocities, yet the velocities in Fig. 3 differ by up to about 45 % between directions (for example v_{2,F} = 1554 against 1074 meV/|Q_Bragg|). The statement that the δ-band renormalization "reinforces" the symmetry breaking holds only if the anisotropic renormalization has the same C2 pattern as the k_F distortion. What to do: give a Landau-theory argument for why the director is not pinned to a mirror direction; extend the model to capture the velocity anisotropy or state that it does not; justify the "reinforces" statement; and present the sublattice charge-order mechanism explicitly as a hypothesis with testable predictions, such as site-resolved LDOS contrast on the three V sublattices in the existing topographs.

14. "Chiral" is used for a state that keeps inversion, and the symmetry classification is imprecise. Where: Introduction, Fig. 1a, Discussion, throughout. The proposed state keeps inversion and C2z, and presumably σh, so its point group is C2h, which is achiral in the usual 3D sense. The handedness is a planar chirality of the in-plane Fermi-surface projection, equivalently a nematic director at a non-high-symmetry angle. Readers can easily confuse this with the chiral (possibly time-reversal-breaking) CDW of Refs. 19, 20 and 24, whose chirality is defined differently. The phrase "C6 symmetric (C3 plus inversion)" is also loose: the bulk point group is D6h. What to do: define the term precisely early on, state the parent and residual point groups explicitly, explain physically and experimentally what distinguishes "chiral-nematic" from "nematic with a generic director", relate it to the chiral CDW of Refs. 19, 20 and 24, and consider whether "low-symmetry nematic" is more accurate.
