A. Must address

1. Nothing in the data or model separates excitonic pair breaking from ordinary defect levels in a non-excitonic, hybridization-gapped insulator or semiconductor, and the Conclusion's logic is circular. Every observation offered as evidence would also appear for a vacancy with donor/acceptor or charge-transition levels in a band insulator whose hybridization gap is static (for example, lattice-driven) and of the same size. These observations are: (i) a pair of in-gap peaks with mixed electron-hole character; (ii) energies that depend on the defect site (Fig. 2d) and on intra- and inter-band scattering; (iii) peaks that shift with tip-sample distance; (iv) a shared spatial envelope for Ee and Eh (Fig. 3g,h), since these can be two charge-transition levels of one localized orbital; and (v) bonding/antibonding splitting in dimers. In the two-band model of Supplementary Sec. II, Δ0 is a fixed, non-self-consistent parameter (35 meV in Fig. 2f; 45 and 18 meV in Fig. 4d; an imposed tanh profile Δ(x) in Fig. 4f). With a fixed Δ, the mean-field EI Hamiltonian is mathematically identical to a non-interacting two-band insulator with interband hybridization Δ. Intra-band (J1) and inter-band (J2) scattering produce the same bound states in such an insulator, so reproducing an asymmetric pair of in-gap peaks (Fig. 2f, Figs. S6–S8) says nothing about whether the gap is excitonic. Whether the Ta2Pd3Te5 gap is excitonic or structural/phonon-driven is exactly the open question in refs. 6, 21–23. Defect-bound states of a topological insulator are a further alternative, since the material has been discussed as a quantum spin Hall and topological excitonic insulator (refs. 22, 25). The Conclusion's statement that the results "confirm that the probed insulating gap at impurity-free region arises from the excitonic pairing" therefore assumes what it sets out to show. The authors should identify at least one observable that the excitonic scenario predicts and a single-particle defect-level scenario does not, and test it. Options include:
   - a self-consistent calculation in which the order parameter is suppressed around the vacancy, predicting a gap-edge depression and healing length that can be compared with dI/dV maps of the local gap edge near defects;
   - the same model run for a plain hybridization (band) insulator with the same gap, showing explicitly which features it fails to reproduce;
   - a DFT supercell/slab calculation of the Te vacancy with no excitonic order, checking whether in-gap levels at the observed energies and with the observed orbital shapes (Fig. 2c, Fig. 3) arise anyway;
   - the evolution of the defect states as the host gap closes on warming toward the transition (see concern 4);
   - a dependence on electron-hole imbalance, on the coupling sign or on the order-parameter phase, which a fixed hybridization lacks;
   - the predicted relation between bound-state decay length and excitonic coherence length ħv/Δ, set against the measured 0.6 nm and 2.5 nm widths.
   Without such a test, the title, abstract and Conclusion should present pair breaking as one consistent interpretation ("consistent with"), not as a demonstration or confirmation.

2. The reported "subgap" energies conflict with the paper's own gap, so it is not established that the resonances are in-gap bound states. In Fig. 1d the gap Δ ≈ 85 meV runs from a hole-side onset near −35 mV to an electron-side coherence peak near +50 mV. Yet the most probable separation for VTe3 is 2δm = 104 meV, with histogram tails to about 120 meV (Fig. 2d), which exceeds the full gap. For VTe2, Eh is mapped at −45 meV (Fig. 3f–h) and appears near −45 mV in Fig. 2c, outside the hole-side edge. In Fig. 2c the defect spectra also show a hard, nearly zero-conductance region between the two resonances, with no remnant of the host's +50 mV peak or hole-side onset beyond them. That lineshape looks more like a locally shifted or renormalized gap with enhanced edges than like bound states inside an intact gap. Pair-breaking (YSR-type) states should sit at |E| < Δ with the host coherence features still visible outside, which is the central premise of the title. The gap definition itself mixes criteria (an onset on the hole side, a peak on the electron side), is called "under-estimated" relative to ARPES (about 100 meV at 20 K), and does not say where the gap center lies relative to EF. The gap center is needed to judge whether Ee and Eh sit symmetrically about it, as a Bogoliubov-like pair would. The authors should:
   - define the gap edges with one quantitative criterion (threshold, spatial averaging), give an uncertainty, and discuss surface versus bulk gaps;
   - overlay defect and defect-free spectra on the same absolute scale over a wide bias range (±200 mV);
   - report Ee, Eh and the local gap edges measured next to each defect, for every defect in Fig. S3, and show that both resonances lie inside the local gap;
   - explain how a 104 meV separation fits into an 85 meV gap, or revise the gap definition. If the answer rests on local doping or gap inhomogeneity (Fig. 4), the reference spectrum must be taken adjacent to each defect.

3. Tip-induced band bending (TIBB), voltage division and defect charging have not been ruled out as the cause of the tip-dependent energy shifts, and several observations point toward them. The abstract says the states are "continuously tunable by tip electric field", which is the language of band bending, not pair breaking. Fig. 2e (up to 8 nA) and Fig. 4e (up to 90 nA, junction resistance about 1 MΩ) change the setpoint current at fixed Vb = 0.1 V. This mainly changes tip height, and with it the local field, TIBB, possible tip-induced charging of the defect, and possibly local heating and mechanical tip-sample interaction. In a narrow-gap, low-carrier-density material, a localized defect level generically shows resonances at both polarities (resonant tunnelling plus tip-induced ionization in a double-barrier geometry), and these shift and change intensity ratio with tip height exactly as in Fig. 2e. The model instead treats the tip as a change of J1 and J2, finds that |J1,2| must decrease on approach, which the authors call "unexpected" (p. 4), and then posits a tip potential of opposite sign post hoc. The threshold-like appearance of the lower-energy states above about 25 nA (Fig. 4e) is especially suggestive of a charging transition. The authors should:
   - separate field from distance: measure at fixed current with different setpoint biases (including both polarities), and at fixed tip height with different setpoint biases;
   - show whether the gap edges and coherence-like peak of a defect-free region shift over the same It sequence, which is the band-bending reference;
   - give I(z) curves or the apparent barrier height so It converts to tip height, and give the peak energies extrapolated to It → 0;
   - look for charging rings or bias- and height-dependent contrast in dI/dV maps around vacancies;
   - estimate TIBB from the known gap, carrier density and tip work function, and show that an electrostatic shift of a localized level cannot reproduce the observed shifts;
   - analyse the ≈25 nA threshold in Fig. 4e as a possible charging event, and reconcile the abstract's "tip electric field" with the main text's treatment of the tip as a scattering potential.

B. Should address

4. The temperature dependence (Fig. 2g) does not connect the subgap states to the excitonic order. The data cover only 1.6 to 20 K, whereas the gap is about 85–100 meV and the excitonic order is reported to persist to roughly 10² K (refs. 5, 6), so the order parameter should be essentially constant over this range. The text nonetheless says the peaks decay faster than the Fermi-convolved 1.6 K spectrum "alongside the thermal suppression of excitonic gap", without showing any defect-free spectra at 20 K, and attributes the extra decay to "local suppression of the excitonic gap by tip potential and/or the quantum pairing poison". The latter cites refs. 32 and 33, which concern Majorana/Andreev quasiparticle poisoning. The explanation is not specific to an EI. Phonon-assisted or inelastic broadening of a localized level, thermally activated charge fluctuations of a donor level, temperature-dependent tip state or setpoint, drift, and a tip temperature different from the sample's would all do the same. The convolution includes only the Fermi function, with no lock-in broadening, and it is not stated whether it covers tip and sample or whether the 1.6 K reference used the same tip and setpoint. The authors should show defect-free spectra at each temperature with the same tip. They should plot the fitted peak widths and energies against T with uncertainties, and quantify the claimed shift "toward the gap center", which is not obvious in Fig. 2g. They should include the lock-in modulation and state the effective electron temperature at 1.6 K in the convolution. Ideally they should extend the measurements to temperatures where the gap measurably shrinks and plot the subgap energies against the simultaneously measured local gap: a pair-breaking state should follow Δ(T), whereas a defect level need not.

5. The model parameters are tuned panel by panel and are not tied to the measured gap or band structure, so the agreement with the data is only qualitative. The simulations use Δ0 = 35 meV and μ = 12.25 meV (Fig. 2f); Δ0 = 45 and 18 meV for the higher- and lower-energy states, with J1 flipping from −15 to +20 meV (Fig. 4d); and Δ0 = 30 meV (Fig. 4f). The measured gap is about 85 meV, a factor of about 2 larger. The J1 sign change sets the intensity asymmetry (Fig. S6) but has no physical justification. With Δ0, μ, J1 and J2 free for each panel, reproducing peak positions and asymmetry is a weak test. The model's bands are not shown to represent Ta2Pd3Te5's quasi-1D, multi-orbital, spin-orbit-coupled band structure, including its proposed Dirac/topological character (refs. 6, 22). Nor is the mapping of a Te vacancy onto intra- and inter-band potentials justified microscopically. The authors should state which parameters are fixed by independent data (DFT/ARPES bands, STS gap, doping) and which are fitted. They should explain how Δ0 relates to the measured gap and justify the J1 sign change. They should state the model's dimensionality, band dispersions and spin structure (singlet or not). Most convincingly, they should fit all four vacancy types, both inhomogeneity regions and the tip-height series with one parameter set, Δ0 and μ fixed from the adjacent defect-free spectra and only the quantity claimed to change (J1, J2, tip potential or local Δ) varied, reporting which features the model fails to capture.

6. The type-I/type-II classification and the lower-energy ("secondary pair-breaking") states in Fig. 4 are under-supported and have plausible non-excitonic origins. The regions are defined by a shift of about 20 meV in a single normal-state feature near +120 to +130 meV (Fig. 4b). This feature's origin is not identified, and its use as a measure of electron-hole imbalance is not justified. The same shift could reflect local band bending, and the inference that type-II regions are more electron-doped is not checked against any other measure. It is also unclear whether the gap center lies below EF at all: the Fig. 1d edges at about −35 and +50 mV place it above EF. The lower-energy states appear only above It ≈ 25 nA at 0.1 V (junction resistance ≲ 4 MΩ, up to 90 nA in Fig. 4e), where tip-sample forces, heating and TIBB are substantial. Their threshold onset and low "surviving temperature" (Fig. S12) are typical of tip-induced charging, which gives sharp threshold peaks and rings in dI/dV maps whose radius depends on bias and tip height. The Δ(x) = Δ0 tanh(x/xc) profile with xc = 80 b0 (about 30 nm) in Fig. 4f is put in by hand and forces the gap to zero at the impurity. The authors should:
   - report the local gap magnitude, gap edges and gap center in type-I and type-II regions from many spectra (a locally reduced order parameter should give a smaller gap);
   - show the It dependence of a defect-free type-II spectrum up to 90 nA;
   - provide bias- and height-dependent dI/dV maps around a type-II defect to test for charging rings;
   - give statistics on how many defects of each type were measured in each region and how many type-II defects show the lower-energy states, and show a spatial correlation between the characteristic-state energy and the impurity-state energies across a field of view;
   - justify the Δ(x) profile and its 30 nm length scale, or derive it self-consistently.

7. The "locked" spatial profiles of Ee and Eh are not a distinctive signature of pair breaking. On p. 5 the authors argue that the collapse of the 10 mV and −45 mV linecuts onto one curve (Fig. 3g,h) shows Ee and Eh are "locked as expected for pair-breaking subgap states". For impurity states in superconductors (ref. 19), however, the particle (u) and hole (v) components generically have different spatial profiles and oscillation phases. A single localized defect level seen at two bias polarities (concerns 1 and 3) would give identical profiles. Fig. 3g also shows clear differences in the tails beyond ±0.3 nm. The authors should plot the calculated u and v profiles from the model (Fig. S8) on the same axes as the data, and explain why identical profiles are expected here when they are not in the superconducting analogue.
   Raised by: r1

8. The spatial maps and linecuts in Fig. 3 may contain setpoint-normalization artifacts. The dI/dV maps (Fig. 3d–f) and linecuts (Fig. 3g,h) were presumably taken in constant-current mode at a fixed setpoint. With strong in-gap spectral weight at the defect, setpoint normalization alone can suppress the 60 meV map at the defect, producing the "spectral reversal" read as spectral-weight transfer. It can also make the Ee and Eh profiles look alike. It is not stated whether the profiles were normalized before the collapse was claimed. The authors should state the setpoint and normalization for each map and linecut. They should show constant-height maps or maps at different setpoint biases, and present the raw linecuts.

9. The Te-vacancy identification rests on topography alone. VTe1–VTe4 are assigned from the apparent morphology in cluster-decorated-tip images (Figs. 2a,b, S1, S2). They appear as "elongated protrusions", which is unusual for a missing chalcogen atom. No vacancies are reported on Te5, which is not explained. No DFT-simulated STM images, formation-energy arguments or controlled creation/annealing are presented. Because the configuration dependence of 2δ (Fig. 2d) is central to the "coupling-tunable" argument, the chemical identity and site assignment matter. The authors should add simulated topographs (including defect-induced LDOS) for VTe1–VTe4 and for competing candidates (antisites, substitutions, Pd or Ta vacancies, interstitials, adsorbates, subsurface defects), and explain the absence of Te5 vacancies.

10. The defect-type dependence of 2δ is neither statistically established nor specific to pair breaking. The text says the vacancy-dependent 2δ (Fig. 2d) "highlights their origin from the e–h pair-breaking states", but the bound-state energies of any defect depend on its local configuration. The four distributions overlap strongly; VTe4 and VTe2 differ by only 7 meV in 2δm, with widths of order 20 meV. So "statistically distinct ranges" needs a quantitative test. Because peak energies depend on It (Fig. 2e), the histograms are meaningful only at a common setpoint and comparable tip. The spread within a class might also come from the local type-I/II environment of Fig. 4. The ordering VTe2 → VTe4 → VTe1 → VTe3, claimed to reflect coupling strength, has no microscopic support. The authors should report the setpoint and the number of defects per type, samples, cleaves and tips. They should give Gaussian widths and 2δm with uncertainties and apply a statistical test. They should state whether the Fig. S3 spectra share a setpoint or are corrected for it, and whether within-class spread correlates with the local environment. They should link the ordering to computed vacancy potentials or local orbital weight on each chain.

11. Several statements go beyond the data. The abstract and introduction frame the work as revealing "how macroscopic coherence emerges from locally collapsed pairing" and "the critical behavior across excitonic condensation", but no data near the excitonic transition are presented. Pages 5–6 state that in EIs "both magnetic and nonmagnetic impurities show the pair-breaking effects, regardless of whether the pairing is spin-singlet or -triplet", yet only nonmagnetic Te vacancies were measured. The authors should temper these statements, presenting the impurity-selectivity point as a theoretical expectation rather than an observation, or else supply supporting evidence.

12. The dimer result is generic and rests on single examples. Splitting into bonding/antibonding-like states for one dimer at 1.3 nm (Fig. S10), and its absence for one at about 2 nm (Fig. S11), is expected for any overlapping localized states (cf. ref. 36, Mn in GaAs). It therefore does not specifically probe "quantum pair-breaking states". The text does not give the crystal axis along which each dimer is separated, which matters given the 0.6 nm versus 2.5 nm anisotropy of Fig. 3g,h. The authors should measure several dimers at several separations and orientations (along b versus c) and report the splitting against distance. They should compare it with the anisotropic decay lengths and with a two-impurity calculation in the same model, which would turn this into a quantitative consistency test.

13. Reproducibility across samples, cleaves and tips is not documented. The paper does not say how many crystals, cleaves and tips the key observations (Figs. 2e, 2g, 3, 4e) come from, or whether resonance energies for a given vacancy type agree between them. Because tip state strongly affects these spectra (tip decoration is used deliberately), this matters. Fig. S5 partly addresses it for the tip approach. The authors should state these numbers and show that the tip-approach and temperature trends hold for more than one defect of each type and on more than one sample.

14. The non-magnetic character of the vacancy is assumed, not tested. The model assumes a non-magnetic impurity and a singlet EI, but a Te vacancy may carry a local moment depending on its charge state. Spectra in an applied magnetic field (Zeeman splitting or shift of Ee and Eh) would test this, and would bear on the singlet-versus-triplet question the authors raise. If no field is available, the authors should at least discuss the expected charge and spin state of the vacancy.
