A. Must address

1. The exfoliation comparison (Fig. 1g,h; Methods; Fig. S2) does not yet show that cryogenic shock raises the rhombohedral fraction as a controlled, reproducible effect. This is the paper's central claim: the non-Bernal fraction rises from about 12% to 32% and the mean flake area from 1200 to 3100 µm². The Methods say only that "the same total Si substrate area" was compared. The paper does not give the number of flakes, chips, tape preparations or independent exfoliation runs per method, the number of bulk crystals, whether both methods used the same crystal, or the crystal's source (natural, Kish or HOPG). It also does not show the run-to-run spread. Rhombohedral content is known to depend on layer number, flakes from 3 to about 20 layers are pooled, and the thickness distributions of the two methods are not shown. The two populations also occupy different flake-size ranges (standard flakes cluster near 10³ µm², cryo flakes are systematically larger), so differences in thickness or size distribution alone could shift the averages. The exclusion rules (cracks, wrinkles, dirt, aspect ratio, IR-visible micro-domains, areas < 80 µm²; Fig. S2b) are said to introduce "approximately equal systematic error" for both methods. A thermal shock plausibly creates more cracks and wrinkles, so this assumption needs checking. Finally, the ovals show ±1 standard deviation with no statistical test, and it is not stated whether the 12% and 32% values are per-flake arithmetic means or area-weighted, or how Bernal-only flakes plotted at zero are handled. The authors should give N (flakes, runs, crystals) for both methods with the per-run fraction and its spread; show the fraction against layer number for both methods; compare size-matched subsets (for example, is the fraction for cryo flakes of about 1000 µm² higher than for standard flakes of the same size?); report the excluded area fraction for each method; state how the averages are computed; and give a significance test.

3. The headline "disorder mean free path exceeding 200 µm" does not follow from the TMF analysis as constructed (abstract; Fig. 3j, labelled i in the text; Methods Eqs. 3–6; Fig. S7b). Eq. 5 gives ℓ_mfp = −(πd/2)/ln[A(T)/A_0] with d ≈ 5 µm, and A_0 is the average of the four lowest-temperature points. Any temperature-independent scattering, including the disorder scattering the abstract refers to, is divided out, so ℓ_mfp → ∞ as T → 0 by construction whatever the actual disorder. What Eq. 5 returns is the temperature-dependent part of the scattering, so fitting it with Matthiessen's rule with a finite ℓ_0 is internally inconsistent unless ℓ_0 is left unconstrained; the paper does not say which T range enters the fit or whether ℓ_0 is free or fixed, and α = 1.56 ± 0.05 depends on this. Numerically, ℓ = 200 µm corresponds to A/A_0 ≈ exp(−7.85/200) ≈ 0.96, a 4% drop in peak area, which is comparable to the scatter in Fig. S7b and to changes from the integration window (dashed lines in Fig. 3e–h), the linear baseline under the peak (whose shape visibly changes between 2 K and 198 K), the linear-in-B Hall subtraction, and the saturation level. Eq. 5 also assumes the only T-dependent loss is bulk scattering along a semicircular path; it neglects T-dependent boundary specularity (p = 0.5 in the simulations), diffuse loss at the edge between contacts, contact transmission, and thermal broadening of the lineshape beyond the integration window at high T. The multiple focusing peaks (1R–6R) each have roughly the same path length per bounce and mainly show boundary specularity, not a mean free path far above 8 µm, while the negative bend resistance (Fig. S6c) supports ℓ of order 10 µm at the sign change. Fig. 3j has no error bars. The Methods concede that the analysis "is not well suited to determine the mean-free path at lowest temperatures" and that "we can only note that ℓ_0 ≥ 200 µm", but neither caveat reaches the abstract or main text. The comparison with "best-in-class mono- and bilayer graphene devices [44–46]" also compares quantities obtained by different methods and at different densities. The authors should obtain an absolute rather than self-normalized bound: for example, fit the absolute low-temperature focusing amplitude and the 1R/2R/… peak ratios with the Boltzmann solver (Fig. 3b) as a function of ℓ_mr with p fixed independently; map the measured low-T negative square resistance (about −6.5 Ω, Fig. S6c) onto the simulated R(ℓ_mr, ℓ_ee) map (Fig. S9a); or use focusing over several contact separations. They should add error bars to Fig. 3j propagated from the noise in A_peak, the uncertainty in A_0 and the window and baseline choices; state the lowest temperature at which ℓ_mfp is resolved rather than bounded; and state the bend-resistance cross-check quantitatively. Otherwise the abstract should say that only the temperature-dependent scattering length exceeds 200 µm below 30 K, as a lower bound with the method's sensitivity limit, and the best-in-class comparison should state how those values were obtained or compare mobilities instead.

4. The stacking identification does not distinguish ABC stacking from other non-Bernal or mixed stacking. The y-axis of Fig. 1g is (1 − A_Bernal)/A_total, a non-Bernal fraction, but the text calls it "32% rhombohedral". IR contrast and sMIM separate Bernal from non-Bernal regions, but for 10–20 layers, mixed sequences (for example ABCB, or ABC blocks separated by a buried twin or stacking fault) can give similar contrast, and sMIM was done only on selected regions. For the measured device, a buried stacking fault could leave the surface-polarized phases, and hence the uniform magnetization of Fig. 2c, largely intact, and the capacitance argument ("no additional features", Fig. 2b) is indirect. The authors should state how many IR-identified domains were confirmed as ABC by Raman 2D lineshape, sMIM or a stacking-sensitive IR spectrum, as a function of thickness (a random subset would do); say whether IR classification was done blind to the method; and give a bulk-sensitive stacking check on the device after nanofabrication, since the Fig. S5 IR images were taken after transfer, not after etching and annealing. The TMF effective mass would support uninterrupted 13-layer ABC stacking if its uncertainty were smaller (see concern 12).

5. The "~90% fabrication yield" needs a clear definition, confidence intervals and attention to confounds (abstract; Fig. S4b; Methods "Transfer statistics"). Fig. S4b defines yield as "the fraction of transfers in which RMG is successfully incorporated without relaxation to Bernal stacking", whereas "fabrication yield" in the abstract implies finished devices. It is not stated when and how success was judged (IR after pickup, after full stack assembly, or after nanofabrication and cooldown), whether any surviving area or a minimum retained area counts, whether partial relaxation counts as success, or whether relaxation during later etching, contact deposition, the 200 °C PC melt or the top-gate drop is counted. The cumulative 24/26 comes from flakes pre-screened by sMIM and aligned along crystallographic axes, using all starred techniques, with sub-samples of 12–36. The techniques were adopted over time by seven people of different experience and were not assigned at random or interleaved, so learning-curve effects and simultaneous adoption of several steps cannot be separated. The authors should define success quantitatively and consistently; report the end-to-end yield of measured devices with rhombohedral signatures, with post-fabrication IR or sMIM; give binomial confidence intervals and state which comparisons are significant; indicate which transfers overlap between bars; give the chronological order of introduction or a per-operator breakdown; and report the cumulative yield separately for <10 and ≥10 layers. The claim that cryo shock does not degrade transfer reliability is reasonable; claims of improvement from individual steps are not established.

6. The claimed mechanism, "partial conversion" of Bernal to rhombohedral stacking by differential-contraction stress (Introduction; p. 3), is not separated from selection effects. Bulk natural graphite already contains 10–15% rhombohedral stacking. A cleaving process that yields larger, less fragmented flakes (Fig. 1g) could preserve more native ABC regions, change where the crystal cleaves so as to extract rhombohedral-rich regions, or reduce relaxation during peeling, all without conversion. Discriminating controls: the same protocol with heating but no LN2 quench, LN2 immersion without the 110 °C step, slow cooling, or a varied quench temperature differential or rate, all on the same crystal; the source crystal's rhombohedral fraction by XRD or Raman; IR imaging of the same already-exfoliated flakes on chip before and after a shock cycle; and examination of the flakes left on the tape. If conversion is real, evidence of shear (partial dislocations, domain-wall density, residual strain by Raman 2D/G mapping) would support it. Otherwise the text should present conversion as one hypothesis among others rather than as "likely".

7. The "size-driven crossover from Poiseuille to porous flow" (abstract; Fig. 4g–l) compares two different measurement geometries, not a width series. The 0.5 µm channel (Fig. 4g) is a four-terminal channel resistance; the 10 µm square (Fig. 4j; Fig. 4k; Fig. S6 insets) uses injection at the top corners and voltage across the bottom, a configuration the paper itself shows is dominated by negative bend resistance at low T for reasons unrelated to viscosity (Fig. 4l, Fig. S9a). A monotonic rise of R_s from negative values is therefore expected from a ballistic-to-ohmic crossover driven only by decreasing ℓ_mr, with ℓ_ee playing no role (compare Fig. S6c and the ℓ_mr axis of Fig. S9a), and the paper states that R in the square is "insensitive to ℓ_ee" (Fig. 4l). The absence of a minimum in the square thus does not by itself show porous flow, and with only two sizes there is no crossover to track. The comparison with simulation is also only qualitative: the simulated ballistic channel resistance is about 210 Ω/sq and drops by about an order of magnitude (Fig. 4i), whereas the measured dips are 10–25%, a few tens of Ω/sq on 100–400 Ω/sq (Fig. 4g). The authors should measure channels of several widths (for example 0.5, 1, 2 and 5 µm) in the same stack with identical contact geometry, or a local four-terminal measurement across the square, and show that the dR/dT < 0 window and its amplitude scale with W/ℓ_G; show with the Fig. S9a map along a realistic (ℓ_ee(T), ℓ_mr(T)) trajectory that the measured square R_s(T) requires small ℓ_ee; compare magnitudes quantitatively using parameters from the same device; state that separating porous from ohmic flow in the square relies on the imaging; and confirm that the channel and square lie in the same rhombohedral domain and were measured in the same cooldown (Fig. S6a/b suggests so). Alternatively, present the crossover as consistent with, rather than established by, the data.

8. The Gurzhi-minimum assignment in the 0.5 µm channel (Fig. 4g–i) lacks the standard discriminating tests. The minimum near 15–30 K is shown at (n_e, D) points in the single-surface, spin/valley-polarized regimes, where correlated phases are present (Fig. 2b). 9. The electron–electron length ℓ_ee = 50 nm is imposed rather than extracted, the images barely constrain it, and it conflicts with other data from the same device (Fig. 4c–f; Fig. S8; Methods). The value is taken from bilayer graphene [25]. Fig. S8i shows that y_max(x) barely changes for ℓ_ee from 10 nm to about 10 µm at ℓ_mr = 200 µm, Fig. S8j shows it depends mainly on ℓ_mr, and the experimental y_max in Fig. S8k has wide uncertainty bands and only "best match" curves with no goodness-of-fit measure. Moreover, Fig. 4j (purple, (4, 0.51), same single-surface regime) shows negative square resistance at the lowest temperatures, which by Fig. 4l and Fig. S9a requires ℓ_ee of micrometres or more. 10. Electron heating during current imaging and transport is not discussed. The nSOT images use I = 10 µA at T ≈ 1.6 K (Fig. 4a,c,e).
