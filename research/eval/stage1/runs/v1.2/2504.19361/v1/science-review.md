A. Must address

1. Trapped flux and field history are not excluded as the source of the time-reversal asymmetry, and the TRSB signal is never trained or cycled (Sec. IV, Fig. 2b,c, Fig. S6). The identity Ic+(H) = |Ic-(-H)| holds only if the whole device, including any frozen-in magnetic configuration, is time-reversed along with the applied field. Several ordinary effects break it in a conventional ring: Abrikosov or Pearl vortices pinned in the wide, µm-scale Sr2RuO4 banks, lead arms or Au/Ti leads near one arm; flux trapped during cooldown in a remanent magnet field; and hysteresis or sweep-direction-dependent remanence of the superconducting magnet, since +H and -H are reached with different sweep histories. Each acts as an internal "magnetization" that does not reverse with H and gives an inhomogeneous local phase offset that a single global shift H -> H + H0 (Eq. 1) cannot remove; such flux can also give a finite Ic+ - |Ic-| at nominally zero field. The c-axis Hc1 of Sr2RuO4 is only a few mT, while the sweeps in Fig. 2 reach about ±10 mT, so vortex entry into the banks is likely, and a vortex near a weak link is known to produce exactly this kind of asymmetric, offset Ic(H) pattern. The paper does not say whether data were taken after zero-field cooling with the magnet degaussed, or how the field was swept; Sec. V states that field-cooled and zero-field-cooled results are identical for the bistable states, but this is not extended to or shown for the TRSB data. The domain picture makes a testable prediction: if the asymmetry comes from a spontaneously chosen domain state, its time-reversed partner must also occur, so the sign of ΔIc and of the zero-field diode should be random (or pinning-set) across zero-field thermal cycles and should follow the sign of a small cooling field, as in Kerr training; the paper only says that earlier macroscopic devices changed on cycling. The authors should report ΔIc(H) (a) after repeated zero-field cooldowns from above Tc with the remanent field nulled, (b) after field cooling in small +Hz and -Hz, (c) for both field-sweep directions, and (d) for different maximum field excursions (minor loops), and should show that the symmetric pattern is recovered after warming just above Tc and recooling in zero field. A sign reversal under opposite field cooling would be strong evidence for intrinsic TRSB; a ΔIc that depends on field excursion or sweep history would point to trapped flux. Without this, TRSB of the order parameter cannot be separated from TRSB of the device's flux configuration.

2. Instrumental and analysis systematics are not excluded, and the error bars on ΔIc understate the real uncertainty (Sec. IV, Sec. S2, Fig. 2b,c, Fig. S9). In Ring 2 (Fig. 2c), ΔIc is a nearly field-independent negative offset of about 5–8 µA on Ic of 60–140 µA, with jumps at the lobe minima; this is also what the paper calls a zero-field diode "over the entire interference pattern". A field-independent offset is precisely what extrinsic sources produce: a dc offset of the current source or a ground loop (a shift δI gives ΔIc = 2δI at every field); a polarity-dependent voltage background such as a thermoelectric voltage not removed by subtracting the mean voltage near I = 0; heating after switching at Ic around 100 µA; the order in which polarities are swept; slow temperature drift, which the authors themselves invoke for a drift-induced ΔIc of about 1 µA in the control SQUID (Fig. S9, last half-periods), larger than its error bars; and a polarity-dependent Ic criterion, since the threshold is 0.1 or 0.2 I0RN "depending on the degree of rounding" with RN taken from "a representative portion of the dataset", and the choice for each dataset is not stated. The error bars come from a parametric bootstrap of voltage noise only, which the text says "only accounts for the uncertainty in measured voltage"; they ignore the stochastic spread of switching currents (telegraph noise near Ic is well documented in Sr2RuO4 junctions, Refs. 31–33), slow drifts and sweep-to-sweep reproducibility, and the number of IV sweeps per point is not given. The authors should show raw IV curves for both polarities at zero and a few other fields, with the sweep order reversed and polarities interleaved at each field point; repeat the measurement with the current leads swapped and with the voltage leads swapped (an extrinsic offset follows the wiring, an intrinsic diode follows the device); report the measured zero-current voltage and current offsets; show that ΔIc is unchanged for criteria of 0.05, 0.1 and 0.2 I0RN and for an independent criterion such as a dV/dI peak; show switching-current histograms at a few fields and recompute the significance using the measured distribution width and repeated independent sweeps; and give a systematic error budget (criterion, offsets, remanence, drift) next to the statistical one. Until then, the "statistically significant" TRSB of Figs. 2 and S6 is not established at the level the headline claim needs.

4. The remanent-field correction (Eq. 1) is ambiguous and may interact with the signal it is meant to isolate (Sec. S2, Eq. 1). As written, Eq. 1 minimizes ∫[Ic+(H+H0) − Ic−(H+H0)]², which compares the two branches at the same field (the diode asymmetry), not Ic+(H) against |Ic−(−H)|; taken literally it does not implement the symmetrization described, and it is unclear whether Ic− means |Ic−|. Choosing H0 to minimize the very asymmetry later reported biases ΔIc towards zero, and a SQUID of two φ0-junctions with different anomalous phases shows a field-shifted pattern that any shift-based correction will partly absorb while leaving extrinsic distortions in place. The authors should correct Eq. 1 and state its sign convention, report H0 for each dataset and for each sweep direction, compare it with an independent remanence calibration (Hall sensor or a reference superconductor in the same mount), and show ΔIc with and without the H0 correction.

5. The evidence that the junctions are domain walls, rather than FIB- or geometry-defined weak links, is indirect (Sec. III, Figs. 1, 2a, S1–S4). Shapiro steps (Fig. 1c,d, Fig. S3) show that a Josephson-like weak link exists in series between the voltage probes, but not where it sits or that it is a chiral domain wall; the statement that Shapiro steps "can only originate from Josephson coupling" is too strong, since phase-slip centres and lines in narrow strips also lock to microwaves at hf/2e. Plausible alternatives include FIB-damaged, Ga-implanted or amorphised regions at the necks (TEM limits damage to about 10 nm at edges, but arms are only 0.25–0.7 µm thick and the TEM of Ref. 43 is from other devices), local inhomogeneity, and weak links in the contact or bank regions. The single-arm stage's Shapiro steps and Gaussian Ic(B) (Figs. S3, S4) would arise from any such constriction, so Fig. S3 does not confirm "the presence of a chiral domain wall". The argument from equal retrapping and critical currents does not exclude constrictions, which are overdamped when shunted by a large normal channel or when IcRN ≪ Δ/e; from Fig. S1 (Ic ≈ 45–80 µA, RN ≈ 19–112 mΩ), IcRN is only about 1–8 µV (≈1.5 µV for Ring 1) against Δ/e ≈ 0.2–0.3 mV, and RN is the whole-device value. The argument from pattern symmetry is circular, since symmetric FIB necks would give symmetric constriction junctions. This matters because extrinsic weak links containing magnetic damage or trapped flux could also break time-reversal symmetry. The authors should discuss the junction IcRN and its temperature dependence (Ambegaokar–Baratoff or KO-type versus phase-slip behaviour); test whether the junction follows the necks using rings with different or asymmetric neck widths, or re-milled necks; state what each of Ring 2's four milling stages (Fig. S2) reveals about junction location; compare quantitatively with the TDGL predictions of Ref. 38; state how many nominally identical rings did and did not show junctions; and temper the "cannot be explained by accidental weak links" statement.

Kerr and µSR place the onset of TRSB at Tc in unstrained Sr2RuO4, whereas strain-split scenarios predict a lower onset, so the onset is a key discriminator. Ring 3 shows no significant TRSB at 1200 mK, described as "very close to Tc", yet Fig. S1 lists its Tc as 1.5 K, and TRSB is shown for it only at 1100 mK. In Ring 2, ΔIc stays at about −5 to −8 µA at 400, 800 and 1000 mK while Ic roughly doubles, so ΔIc/Ic falls on cooling, which is not explained. 8. The "unstrained" and "strain-free" claims are asserted rather than measured, yet they are used to reject competing scenarios (Abstract, Sec. III, Sec. VII). The argument rests on Tc ≤ 1.5 K and RRR > 100, which are bulk-averaged; device Tc values of 1.3–1.5 K (Table S1) lie at or below bulk, and Tc rises only gently at small strain while the 3K phase needs dislocations or large strain. Differential thermal contraction between the 0.25–0.7 µm flake, the SrTiO3 substrate and the Ti/Au and SiOx/Au caps can impose inhomogeneous strain of order 0.1%, and FIB milling leaves a damaged, possibly Ga-implanted or amorphised rim at the sidewalls and necks, exactly where the junctions are proposed to sit. The Discussion uses "ultra-pure and strain-free" to reject the strain- or dislocation-texture and accidental-degeneracy scenarios (Refs. 52–56), which are the leading alternative explanations of TRSB at domain walls. The authors should estimate the thermal-mismatch strain for this stack (or from the Tc width), add Raman, cross-sectional TEM or local strain data on a measured device if available, or soften the argument and state that the case against Refs. 55, 56 rests mainly on domain-wall mobility.

9. The conclusions on pairing symmetry go beyond the data (Abstract, Sec. VII, Conclusion). A transport TRSB signal with mobile domains is consistent with any two-component TRSB state with degenerate time-reversed domains, including accidental degeneracies and non-chiral combinations such as s+id or d+ig; only the toy model and the narrow central lobe invoke chirality and domain flux specifically. The statement that Eg (dxz + idyz) pairing "is favored based on the experiments", the Discussion's "Our experiments confirm the long-standing question", and the abstract's "direct evidence of TRSB" overstate what is shown, and the conflict with the absent second heat-capacity anomaly is acknowledged but not resolved. The authors should present the result as consistent with a two-component TRSB state (and with Eg pairing), or add an observable that separates chiral from non-chiral domains, such as the sign relation between cooling-field direction and diode sign requested in concern 1.

10. The narrow central lobe and its domain-wall-motion interpretation rest on a heuristic model with many free parameters, and conventional alternatives are not excluded (Sec. IV, Fig. 2a, Sec. S3, Fig. S10). The central lobe is 0.73 Φ0 in Ring 1 and 0.74 Φ0 in Ring 3 against about 1.00 Φ0 for neighbouring lobes. Conventional explanations should be excluded first: non-linearity or remanent-flux reversal of the superconducting magnet near zero field (the control SQUID, Figs. S8, S9, spans only ±2 mT with a ≈1.6 mT period, so it does not test this range), screening with field-dependent kinetic inductance, flux focusing from the banks, and vortex entry into the banks at |H| ≈ 2–3 mT. The toy model adds an ad hoc linear coupling α(Ψ+ − Ψ−)B (Eq. 3), which is neither defined as a free-energy term nor gauge-invariant as written (a coupling of B to |Ψ+|² − |Ψ−|² would be), an ad hoc sigmoid with free φm and a (Eq. 4), an unexplained scaling Φint ∝ (φD + π/2)/(π/2) with free Φint^max (Eq. 5), and a Gaussian envelope on top of αI and βL, i.e. many parameters for about five lobes; it still overshoots the outer lobes, mismatches lobe widths (0.91 fit vs about 0.97 data) and misses the central-lobe skew and amplitudes (Fig. S10b,c). 11. The current-switchable bistable Ic states under in-plane field have conventional explanations that are not excluded (Sec. V, Fig. 3, Figs. S13, S15, S16). The observed symmetry, in which reversing either the current polarity or the field direction swaps the roles of the training currents, is the symmetry of a J × B Lorentz force on vortices: Josephson vortices between RuO2 layers, Abrikosov vortices in the thin flake or trapped at the necks, or vortices entering the banks at the training currents of about 110–120 µA. A 20 mT in-plane field threads several Φ0 through an arm cross-section of about 0.5 × 0.5 µm², so each junction is strongly field-modulated (the Fraunhofer-like pattern of Fig. S15), and current-driven switching between fluxon configurations in a wide junction is a known source of bistable Ic; the λJ argument (Sec. VI) dismisses only Abrikosov vortex flow. A misalignment of 1–3° of a 10–20 mT in-plane field gives 0.2–1 mT out of plane (1° of 20 mT ≈ 0.35 mT ≈ 0.08 Φ0 through the ring), a sizeable fraction of the ≈4–4.7 mT period, and if βL is not small the ring can host several fluxoid states that a large bias can switch between. No alignment procedure or out-of-plane calibration is given. The ring-dependent optimal angle (20° in Ring 1, 55° in Ring 2; Fig. S16), with no link to crystal axes, the irregular map of Fig. S16b and the irreproducibility after a field reset (Fig. S14) also point naturally to sample-specific pinning. The authors should state the alignment accuracy and how it was set; show whether the bistability survives when the out-of-plane component is compensated or deliberately varied; give βL (kinetic plus geometric inductance) for each ring; show Ic(Hz) patterns of the high- and low-Ic states to test for a relative fluxoid shift; show the in-plane-field and temperature dependence of the switching thresholds and Ic difference; test whether bistability persists below the in-plane vortex entry field; and report the crystal-axis orientation of each ring.
