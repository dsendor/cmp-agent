A. Must address

1. The polarization dependence cannot distinguish magnetic-dipole emission from non-magnetic second-order emission, and no measurement does. In point group -3 an in-plane axial vector (M_x, M_y) belongs to E_g (Table S1). At the (0001) surface the symmetry drops to point group 3, where an in-plane polar vector, such as a surface rectification polarization or a shift/rectification current J_x ∝ a(E_x² − E_y²) + b(2E_xE_y), also transforms as E. Bulk electric-quadrupole rectification, photon drag (proportional to the photon wavevector), anisotropic photocurrents, and electric-quadrupole radiation from the intra-cell quadrupoles invoked in SI Fig. S6b / Sec. IV.B.4 are also allowed in a centrosymmetric crystal and give in-plane emission. All of these give the same normal-incidence form S ∝ cos(2φ − 2δ), with a material-dependent δ that is not locked to a mirror because the group has no mirrors. The fourfold |S| pattern with a non-universal δ (Figs. 2d,e; Eq. 3 and Eq. IV.64 have the same form) is therefore expected from any of them and is not specific to M_y. The two arguments used on p. 2 to exclude a surface contribution are not decisive. (i) "It would not track magnetic order": the paper's own data show the fourfold pattern fully present at 200 K, about three times Tc, at roughly 85% of the maximum amplitude or more (Figs. 2e,f). A smooth temperature dependence can also come from changes in band gap, absorption and carrier lifetime, which Cr2Ge2Te6 shows across Tc through magneto-elastic and band effects. (ii) "It would scale linearly with fluence": sublinear, saturating fluence dependence is common for surface photocurrent and rectification emitters through absorption saturation, band filling, carrier screening and heating. The identification of the radiating channel, which is the paper's central claim, therefore rests on a symmetry argument that does not separate the candidates. The authors should add measurements that do: (a) emission against applied in-plane and out-of-plane magnetic field, with field reversal, at 7 K and above Tc, using the magnet of the OptiCool cryostat they already used; the model predicts a specific H_dc dependence (Eq. V.33); (b) emission in transmission and in reflection from a thin exfoliated flake, since a sheet electric-dipole current and a sheet magnetic dipole radiate with opposite relative sign forward and backward; (c) a surface-versus-bulk test: several sample thicknesses, opposite faces of a crystal, fresh versus aged or capped surfaces, or a different pump photon energy to change the penetration depth; (d) a reference emitter (ZnTe, or a non-magnetic or isostructural centrosymmetric crystal) measured under identical conditions to fix the absolute polarity and the detection-chain response; (e) full vector THz polarimetry, since an in-plane magnetic dipole radiates E ⊥ M, so the predicted E_y ∝ M_x ∝ sin(2φ − 2δ) component should appear in quadrature with the measured E_x component (this checks the tensor form, though not the multipole order).

2. No measurement shows that the Cr magnetization, or the ferromagnetic order, is changed, as the title and abstract claim ("manipulation of ferromagnetism"). The only observable is far-field THz emission; no measurement gives ΔM, its direction, or its relation to the ordered moment. The signal persists at nearly full strength at 80 K and 200 K (Figs. 2e,f), so even within the authors' picture most of the effect is a light-induced magnetization of a paramagnet rather than manipulation of ferromagnetism. In addition, the itinerant non-equilibrium spin density produced by the nonlinear Edelstein effect would itself radiate as a magnetic dipole without involving the Cr moments, so the emission alone does not show that the local moments respond. Statements such as the field "pull[ing] the magnetization completely into the plane" (p. 4–5, Fig. 4d) are model outputs, not observations. The authors should add a direct probe of the pumped magnetic state with the same pump polarizations, such as time-resolved Kerr or Faraday rotation/ellipticity or time-resolved magnetic linear dichroism, and show δM(t) ∝ cos(2φ − 2δ) with the same δ as the THz emission. They should plot the THz amplitude together with M(T) and χ(T) from Figs. S1/S2, and could compare with isostructural CrSiTe3 (Tc ≈ 33 K) to test whether any temperature feature moves with Tc. Otherwise the title and abstract should be narrowed to what the emission data show.

3. The 45°-incidence interpretation relies on a net, single-domain out-of-plane magnetization that the sample is unlikely to have at zero field, and it compares static magnetization with a pump-induced signal. The explanation of the fluence-driven polarity reversal and 90° rotation of the two-fold pattern (Figs. 3b,c; Figs. 4d,e; "|M_y − M_z|") requires a "frozen easy-axis component M_z" competing with the light-induced M_y. Fig. S2 shows a soft ferromagnet with vanishing coercivity and no remanence, so in zero field the bulk should break into up and down domains, and over the 1.9 mm (×√2) pump spot the net ⟨M_z⟩ should be close to zero. The change δM_z from tilting toward +y is odd in the sign of M_z and cancels between domains; only the even δM_y survives, and the proposed M_y/M_z competition would be absent. The paper does not say whether a field was applied during the 45° (OptiCool) measurements. Separately, the THz emission is set by the pump-induced change ΔM(t) (or its time derivatives), not by the static M(I0): Fig. 4d plots the full static |M_z|, |M_y| and |M_y − M_z|, with M_z starting at 1 at zero fluence, so the minimum of the green curve comes from subtracting a large equilibrium M_z that does not itself radiate. Recomputed with ΔM_y and ΔM_z = M_z(I0) − M_z(0), which have opposite signs, the destructive interference and the minimum may not survive. The authors should state the applied field and magnetic history for the 45° data; repeat the 45° fluence series with the sample saturated at +B_z and at −B_z and show that the M_z-derived part, and the pattern rotation, follow the sign of M_z; repeat the series above Tc, where the sign reversal should vanish if M_z is essential; and recompute Fig. 4d using ΔM with the correct geometric projection weights (see concerns 7 and 14).

B. Should address

4. The temperature dependence does not support a ferromagnetism-mediated emission and is not reproduced by the model in the regime where the data were taken. In Fig. 2f the signal grows from about 0.4 at 5–7 K to about 0.85 near 175–180 K, and the 200 K pattern in Fig. 2e is the largest. The text says the signal "decreases gradually with cooling and then undergoes a more rapid reduction upon crossing Tc" (p. 2), but in the figure the amplitude is roughly flat from about 40 K to above Tc, the steep drop occurs below about 30–40 K inside the ordered phase, and within the error bars (about ±0.1–0.15) there is no clear feature at Tc. The petal orientation is identical at 7, 80 and 200 K (Fig. 2e), so the ordered moment, which lowers the magnetic point group from 3' to 3 (SI p. S-17), leaves no visible trace; magnetization-odd contributions allowed below Tc should be discussed and bounded. In the model, the in-plane susceptibility χ_∥ is largest at and below Tc, η_z diverges at Tc (Fig. S7b), and above Tc χ falls as 1/(T − Tc/(1+κ)) (Eq. V.20), which is about ten times smaller at 200 K than at 80 K, yet the measured amplitude is not smaller. An increase with temperature appears only at I0 = 5 Tc0 (Fig. S8b, Fig. S9 right), a deep-saturation regime, while the temperature series uses 100 µJ/cm², which lies in the near-linear part of the fluence curve in Fig. 4c. The authors should define the plotted quantity ("Spectral Weight" on the axis versus |S_THz|max in the text) and the origin of the error bars; give an explicit map from fluence to I0/Tc0 showing which model regime applies, or reconcile the trend another way; add the model curve at the same fluence to Fig. 2f; take polarization maps at more temperatures across Tc; explain why the change is concentrated far below Tc; and extend the measurements toward room temperature.

5. There is no estimate of the size of the Edelstein–Zeeman field or of the magnetization change, so the mechanism's plausibility is untested. Neither experiment nor theory gives a magnitude: the simulations use I0 = 0.005 Tc0 and 5 Tc0 and χ_x,∥ = χ_z,xx = 0.5 (Tc0)⁻¹ (pp. S-20, S-21), and the fluence axes of Figs. 4b,d are dimensionless. The model needs H^EZ of order I0 = 5 Tc0 (about 30 meV, i.e. effective fields of order 100 T) to reproduce the saturation and temperature trends. From the calibrated E_THz (see concern 18), the authors should estimate the transient ΔM (in µB per Cr) and its rise time needed to radiate it as a magnetic dipole, given the excited volume (spot size, absorption depth at 1.2 eV), pulse duration and collection optics; estimate the H^EZ in tesla this requires; and compare it with the anisotropy field of Cr2Ge2Te6 and with J_sd⟨S⟩ for the photocarrier density at 0.1 mJ/cm². If H^EZ must be comparable to the exchange scale, this should be stated and justified. Without this the mechanism remains qualitative, and an implausible magnitude would point instead to an electric-dipole source (concern 1).

6. The model treats sub-picosecond dynamics as static equilibrium. It minimizes a free energy, or solves the self-consistent Eq. V.9, for a static H^EZ (Eq. 4; Fig. 4; Figs. S7–S9) and identifies the emitted amplitude with the resulting equilibrium M. But the far field depends on d²M/dt², H^EZ exists only for about the photocarrier coherence time of a 160 fs pulse, and the emitted pulses are single-cycle transients of about 0.5–1 ps (Figs. 2c, 3b,d). Localized Cr moments respond by precession and damping on GHz to sub-THz timescales (magnon and FMR frequencies) and by longitudinal dynamics; neither reaches the adiabatic minimum within the pulse, and a coherent rotation "pulling the magnetization completely into the plane" (p. 5) cannot be completed by a 100–200 fs torque. The model also does not account for the waveform or spectrum, for example the spectral peak shift from about 0.4 to about 1 THz with fluence in Fig. 3d. The authors should solve a time-dependent model (LLG, Landau–Lifshitz–Bloch or Bloch equations) driven by H^EZ(t) with the experimental pulse envelope, derive the emitted waveforms and spectra and compare them with Figs. 2c and 3d (duration about 1 ps, 0.3–1.5 THz content), and show that the polarization and fluence trends survive; or argue explicitly why the adiabatic limit applies and why the amplitude should track a static M(I0).

8. Heating and the effective sample temperature are not addressed, although the paper claims a non-thermal mechanism and interprets behaviour relative to Tc ≈ 67 K. The normal-incidence data use 50 kHz (25 kHz after chopping) and up to 330 mW average power on a ~1.9 mm spot at a nominal 7 K in an exchange-gas cryostat, where the low thermal conductivity of van der Waals crystals can give a steady-state rise of several to tens of kelvin; the 45° data use up to 90 mW at 3 kHz in a different cryostat. Per-pulse heating may be larger still: an absorbed ~0.3 mJ/cm² deposited within a penetration depth of a few tens of nm gives an energy density of order tens of J/cm³, which literature heat capacities suggest is comparable to or larger than the enthalpy needed to heat Cr2Ge2Te6 from 7 K to above Tc. Heating would blur any Tc feature in Fig. 2f, could produce the saturation in Fig. 4c and the non-monotonic behaviour in Fig. 4e through loss of magnetization, and means the spin temperature is not the 7 K (T = 0.104 Tc0, Fig. S9) assumed in the model. The authors should give the absorbed fluence and penetration depth at 1.2 eV; estimate steady-state and per-pulse temperature rises; vary the repetition rate or chopping at fixed pulse energy (for example tracking the shift of a Tc-related feature); state the effective temperature for Figs. 2f and 4; show that the low-fluence pattern is unchanged after high-fluence exposure (no cumulative damage); and explain what, beyond the polarization dependence, supports "non-thermal" in the abstract and conclusion.

The saturating fluence dependence in Fig. 4c is presented as the signature of magnetization near saturation (p. 6), but it has standard non-magnetic explanations: absorption or band-filling saturation at the near-resonant 1.2 eV transition, Coulomb screening of photocarriers or of the THz-generating field, and cumulative heating (concern 8). 10. The fluence-driven pattern rotation at 45° has simpler two-source explanations that are not excluded, and the spectral change points that way. Any two contributions with different signs and different fluence scaling give a 90° rotation of a two-fold pattern with a minimum at the crossover. Candidates include a polarization-independent term A (photo-Dember, a surface-field current along z radiating p-polarized light at oblique incidence, or ultrafast demagnetization of M_z, which is polarization-independent and nonlinear in fluence) interfering with the fourfold term B·cos(2φ − 2δ), or a term linear in fluence (surface rectification, photocurrent) plus a saturating one. The main text dismisses photo-Dember as "largely isotropic", but an isotropic term is exactly what this interference needs. Fig. 3d shows the spectral peak moving from about 0.4 THz at 0.1 mJ/cm² to about 1 THz at 0.75 mJ/cm² with a more oscillatory waveform; if M_y and M_z were driven by the same H^EZ(t), only their relative amplitude would change and the spectrum would stay fixed, so the shift suggests two sources with different dynamics. Fig. 3c shows only two fluences with sparse points, and the minimum in Fig. 4e rests on about three points below 0.4 mJ/cm². The authors should take signed polarization maps at several fluences through the minimum (about 0.1–0.4 mJ/cm²) with denser sampling and repeat runs; fit them with A(F) + B(F)·cos(2φ − 2δ), or the full oblique-incidence Eq. 3 including E_z terms, and show that the fitted coefficients evolve as the M_y/M_z competition predicts rather than as independent linear and saturating terms; and decompose the waveforms into a fluence-dependent sum of two fixed temporal components.

12. The microscopic theory is a generic toy model and does not treat the real compound or its ferromagnetic phase. The SI Hamiltonian (Eq. III.3, Fig. S5) is a two-site Dirac/Rashba model with opposite-sign SOC on inversion partners stacked along z, with unspecified t'1, t'2 and α̃ and uniaxial C_n symmetry. It shows χ^(2) ≠ 0 but gives neither its magnitude, sign, nor δ for Cr2Ge2Te6, where the 1.2 eV transitions involve Te-p/Cr-d bands on a honeycomb lattice. The hidden-spin-texture argument relies on Kramers degeneracy, which is lifted below Tc where the bands are exchange-split; the text also says intraband dipole-current terms involve Fermi-surface contributions, which an insulator at low temperature lacks. J_sd, the photocarrier density and the coherence lifetime (regularized by δ⁺ → 0), which set the size of H^EZ, are not estimated. Below Tc, established mechanisms also give polarization-dependent torques on M from linearly polarized light: polarization-dependent absorption by spin-polarized bands, photoinduced magnetic anisotropy and the inverse Cotton–Mouton effect. The authors should explain how the Edelstein–Zeeman field differs from these experimentally and in symmetry; estimate χ^(2) and the ratio χ_z,xx/χ_x,∥ needed for the 45° interpretation for the real band structure (ideally DFT with SOC, as in Refs. 6–8, or a tight-binding model fitted to Cr2Ge2Te6); or present the theory explicitly as a symmetry-allowed proof of principle with the magnitude constrained by experiment.
