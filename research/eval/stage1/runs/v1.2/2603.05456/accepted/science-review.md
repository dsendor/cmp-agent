A. Must address

1. **The magnetic-field dependence and the magnetic domain state are missing, yet the ferromagnetic interpretation depends on them.** The paper never applies an external magnetic field, although the Methods say a magneto-optic cryostat was used, and it does not state the magnetic history (zero-field-cooled or field-cooled). The 45° analysis (pages 4–6, Fig. 3b–d, Fig. 4d,e) assumes a single-domain, out-of-plane equilibrium M_z that competes with the light-induced M_y. Bulk Cr2Ge2Te6 is a soft ferromagnet, and in zero field below Tc it forms stripe or bubble domains with net M_z ≈ 0 over a pump spot of tens to hundreds of micrometres. The M_z contribution to E_THz ∝ (M × n), and with it the interference that is said to produce the polarity reversal, would then largely cancel. Without field data, the link between the emission and the ordered moment rests on the 45° fluence series and a modest kink in Fig. 2f. The authors should state the field history and add field-dependent, field-reversed emission: (i) repeat the 45° fluence series after field-cooling in +B_z and −B_z, or under ±B along c; the M_z-derived component must reverse sign with the field while the light-set M_y component should not; (ii) show how the field shifts the fluence at which the pattern reorients and the polarity reverses (Figs. 3d, 4e), which the Landau model ties to the anisotropy terms r_0, r_z and g; (iii) measure the normal-incidence signal under in-plane and out-of-plane fields, below and above Tc.

B. Should address

2. **No evidence separates rotation of the Cr moments from the magnetization of the photoexcited carriers.** The title, abstract and Discussion claim "manipulation of ferromagnetism" and "femtosecond-to-picosecond manipulation of magnetic order". The only observable is far-field THz emission during roughly the first 2 ps: a single-cycle transient with spectral weight at about 0.3–1.5 THz (Fig. 3d), with no feature at the Cr2Ge2Te6 magnon or FMR frequency, which lies in the GHz range. A transient spin or orbital density of the photocarriers (the nonlinear Edelstein δM of Eq. 1 itself) would radiate as a magnetic dipole with the same φ dependence and without involving the Cr sublattice. The paper itself calls the magnetic degrees of freedom "slower", and it does not show that the Cr moments can respond on this timescale. The authors should add a direct probe of the ordered moment with the same pump-polarization dependence and fluences: time-resolved MOKE or Faraday rotation, which should be feasible in the existing setup, the XMCD they propose as future work, or at least a static Kerr measurement before and after pumping. At minimum they should estimate the Cr response time. Without such evidence, the headline claim should be narrowed to THz emission from a light-induced nonequilibrium (nonlinear Edelstein) magnetization, with involvement of the ferromagnetic order inferred only from the 45° data.

3. **Surface electric-dipole, bulk electric-quadrupole and photon-drag emission are not excluded experimentally.** The arguments against surface rectification (page 2–3) are that it would not track the magnetic order and would scale linearly with fluence. Neither excludes it: surface depletion fields, carrier densities and surface χ(2) in a semiconductor are also temperature dependent, Fig. 2f shows a smooth crossover rather than an order-parameter-like onset, and most of the signal is present well above Tc (Fig. 2e at 200 K). The authors acknowledge on page 3 that "there is no mirror symmetry on the surface ... to lock the surface dipole emission to the crystal axes", so an in-plane polar surface response with P_x ∝ a(E_x² − E_y²) + b(2E_xE_y), including surface optical rectification and shift or injection currents, gives the same unlocked |cos(2φ − 2δ)| pattern as Eq. (3). In the bulk, linear photon drag (an in-plane current ∝ q_z E⊗E, allowed in trigonal symmetry) and electric-quadrupole rectification (which Fig. 1 itself invokes) are allowed in centrosymmetric crystals and have the same E⊗E structure. At 1.2 eV the penetration depth is probably only tens of nanometres, so "bulk" and "surface" are not cleanly separated. The authors should add standard discriminating tests: emission from the front and back faces, or reflection against transmission geometry (magnetic-dipole, electric-dipole and photon-drag terms behave differently under sample flip and reversal of the light wavevector); dependence on sample thickness (the thickness is not even stated); dependence on incidence angle; and a nonmagnetic isostructural or same-point-group layered reference measured under identical conditions.

4. **Heating and ultrafast demagnetization could produce the 45° reorientation, although "non-thermal" is a headline claim.** The polarity reversal and 90° rotation at 45° occur between 0.1 and 0.75 mJ/cm² at 7 K (Fig. 3b–d), and fluences reach 1.5 mJ/cm² with above-gap 1.2 eV photons. With absorption within tens to ~100 nm, this deposits tens of J/cm³, and the heat capacity of Cr2Ge2Te6 at 7 K is small, so the excited volume could be heated transiently, or cumulatively depending on the unstated repetition rate, towards or above Tc (66 K). Quenching of M_z or a heating-induced change in anisotropy would suppress any M_z-related contribution and leave the in-plane one, matching Fig. 4d. Alternatively, at 45° incidence M_z radiates into the detected p-polarization, and demagnetization or heating adds a polarization-independent offset A; adding A to a cos 2φ term from M_y gives a twofold pattern that rotates by 90° and passes through a minimum as the relative sign or weight of A changes with fluence, which is what Figs. 3c,d and 4e show, without any Edelstein-generated competition. The saturation at normal incidence (Fig. 4c) could have the same origin. The authors should report the repetition rate, spot size and absorbed energy density; estimate the transient and steady-state lattice and spin temperatures at each fluence relative to Tc; decompose the 45° data at each fluence into isotropic and cos 2φ / sin 2φ parts; show the 45° fluence series above Tc (e.g. 80 K), where a reversal caused by competition with the ordered M_z must vanish; take a 45° temperature series at fixed fluence across Tc; and show that the crossover fluence in Fig. 4e does not shift with base temperature or repetition rate as heating would predict.

5. **The temperature dependence argues as much against a magnetic origin as for one.** In Fig. 2e,f the emission persists at 80 and 200 K, is largest in the paramagnetic phase (near 150 K about twice its 7 K value), is roughly flat from ~70 to 200 K, and falls on cooling through Tc. Ordering therefore suppresses the signal, and most of it is a paramagnetic-phase response. In the model of Eq. (4) with r_z ∝ T − Tc, the in-plane magnetization induced by a fixed H^EZ above Tc scales with the paramagnetic susceptibility, which should peak near Tc and fall as ~1/(T − Tc), crossing over below Tc to the transverse susceptibility set by the anisotropy. The data show no feature near Tc and the opposite trend at high temperature, and the claimed "more rapid reduction upon crossing Tc" (page 3, "tracks the magnetic ordering") is hard to see within the error bars. The text's explanation (weak temperature dependence because H^EZ breaks time-reversal symmetry) is qualitative. The authors should compute the temperature dependence predicted by their mean-field model and overlay it on Fig. 2f; extend the data above 200 K, e.g. to room temperature; measure the fluence dependence at several temperatures above and below Tc, since the model predicts a saturation fluence that tracks r_0(T); consider temperature-dependent non-magnetic factors such as the absorption edge, carrier lifetime and THz absorption; explain how a mechanism that needs a magnetic susceptibility gives its largest signal where the susceptibility is smallest; and, if the high-temperature signal comes from a non-magnetic channel, show how it is subtracted from the 7 K signal. The title claim should be framed in light of the effect existing without ferromagnetic order.

6. **A static free-energy minimum is compared with radiation that depends on the dynamics of M.** The Landau model (Eq. 4) minimizes F(M) under a constant H^EZ and has no timescale, damping or equation of motion. The radiated field is set by ∂²M/∂t² (or ∂M/∂t in the near field), so the rise time, decay time and spectral content of δM(t) set both the amplitude and the shape of the waveform. The waveform and spectrum change markedly with fluence (Fig. 3d; the spectral peak moves from ~0.5 THz at low fluence to ~1 THz at 0.75 mJ/cm²), which the adiabatic picture cannot capture, and the spectral-weight comparison in Fig. 4c,e implicitly assumes the spectrum is fixed. The static picture also assumes the Cr magnetization follows H^EZ adiabatically on a sub-picosecond timescale, which is not justified for precessional dynamics in a weakly anisotropic magnet. The authors should model the dynamics (e.g. LLG or time-dependent Landau/mean-field equations driven by a pulsed H^EZ(t) with the 160 fs pump envelope and the carrier lifetime) and compare the predicted waveforms and spectra with the data, or explicitly justify why the spectral weight should be proportional to the static |M_y| or |M_y − M_z|.

7. **The "quantitative" agreement claimed in the abstract is not demonstrated.** The abstract says the polarization, fluence and temperature dependences are "quantitatively captured". Fig. 4b,d plot normalized M in arbitrary units against a dimensionless I_0 ∝ |E_0|², while Fig. 4c,e use fluence in mJ/cm²; the mapping between them, the values of r_z, r_0, u_z, g, J and χ(2), which are fitted and which are fixed independently (e.g. anisotropy from magnetometry), and the number of fitted parameters relative to data points are not given in the main text. The grey curve in Fig. 4c is not identified as model or empirical fit. No model curve is shown for the temperature dependence (Fig. 2f) or for the polarization patterns. Fig. 4e has six points, one (1.5 mJ/cm²) with a large error bar, and the minimum near 0.2 mJ/cm² rests on few points. If "spectral weight" is an integral of |E(ω)|², it is quadratic in the emitted field, while the model quantity |M_y| is linear in it, so the compared quantities may not scale the same way. The authors should define spectral weight, compare like quantities, list all parameters with values and sources, overlay the model on Figs. 2f, 4c and 4e in physical units, and either fit the normal-incidence fluence, 45° fluence and temperature data jointly with one parameter set or soften "quantitatively".

8. **Only one THz polarization component is detected, and the response is not tied to the crystal axes.** Emission is detected only along a (page 2). Equation (2) also predicts an in-plane M_x (M± terms) radiating in the orthogonal polarization, and point group -3 makes a definite prediction for how the full in-plane vector rotates with φ, with a definite phase relation between components and sign alternation of E_THz between adjacent lobes. The authors should measure both orthogonal THz polarizations (with sign), and rotate the sample azimuth at fixed pump polarization and compare with rotating the polarization at fixed sample. This would test the full tensor of Eq. (2), check the M × n geometry, report and fix δ relative to the crystal axes, and show that the response follows bulk -3 symmetry rather than strain, step edges or a particular surface termination.

9. **The saturation at normal incidence has simpler explanations.** Page 2, point (ii), reads the sublinear growth in Fig. 4c as Zeeman-like saturation of M_y and argues that a χ(2) mechanism would scale as F. But absorption saturation, Pauli blocking or band filling, photocarrier screening, two-photon absorption and a fluence-dependent carrier lifetime all make second-order emission saturate at mJ/cm² fluences. The authors should measure the fluence dependence of absorbed power or transient reflectivity over the same range (if the THz amplitude saturates while the excited carrier density stays linear, that supports the magnetic picture), and ideally compare with the saturation of a nonmagnetic reference, before using saturation as evidence for a magnetic mechanism.

10. **M-linear channels in the ordered phase, inverse Cotton-Mouton and photo-induced anisotropy, are not ruled out.** Below Tc, a response δM ∝ M E E (inverse Cotton-Mouton) is allowed with linear polarization in a magnetized crystal, as is a photo-induced change of the magnetic anisotropy. Either gives polarization-dependent emission that changes at Tc. The introduction dismisses these on symmetry grounds, but in the ordered phase they are not symmetry-forbidden. The authors should state the specific evidence for a time-reversal-even α(2) Edelstein origin of the ordered-phase response as opposed to these channels, and test it, for example by field reversal (concern 1; the inverse Cotton-Mouton term reverses with M) and by pump photon-energy dependence (concern 14).

11. **The size of the induced magnetization and of the Edelstein-Zeeman field is not estimated.** The paper does not estimate the δM (in μB per Cr or as a fraction of Ms) needed to produce the measured several-V/cm field via the magnetic-dipole radiation formula, given the saturation magnetization and absorption depth; the H^EZ implied by the observed saturation, or needed to rotate M away from the easy axis against the anisotropy field of Cr2Ge2Te6; or the nonlinear Edelstein susceptibility J·χ(2) (Eq. 5) expected from the band structure at 1.2 eV. Order-of-magnitude numbers would show whether the Cr moments can plausibly be rotated "completely into the plane" (Fig. 4a,d) at sub-mJ/cm² fluence, and whether the carrier spin density alone could account for the signal (concern 2).

12. **The superposition test in Fig. 3d is under-specified.** The 0.4 mJ/cm² trace is compared with E_0.1 + E_0.75, apparently with unit weights. Under the model both the M_y and M_z contributions change with fluence, so an unweighted sum of the end-point traces has no obvious reason to reproduce the intermediate one, and the agreement may be partly coincidental; also, the spectral-weight minimum is near 0.2 mJ/cm² (Fig. 4e), not 0.4. The authors should state whether any scaling, time shift or normalization was applied, give a residual or goodness-of-fit measure, show the same test at other intermediate fluences (0.15, 0.2 mJ/cm²), and ideally decompose all fluence traces globally (SVD or a fit to two fixed basis waveforms with fluence-dependent weights), plotting the extracted weights against the model M_y(I_0) and M_z(I_0) of Fig. 4d.

13. **Conventional photo-Dember and surface-field emission at 45° are dismissed too quickly.** Page 4 dismisses them as "largely isotropic with respect to pump polarization". At 45° incidence, however, s- and p-polarized light are absorbed differently through the Fresnel coefficients, so any z-directed surface current acquires a twofold dependence on φ. Competition between such a term, which saturates with carrier density, and a polarization-dependent rectification term could also produce a polarity reversal and pattern rotation with fluence. The authors should state explicitly the meaning of φ relative to the plane of incidence and the crystal axes at 45°, and how much E_z the pump carries; measure the polarization dependence of the absorbed fraction at 45°; estimate the photo-Dember or surface-field contribution; and show that their two-component decomposition is not reproduced by these terms.

14. **The interband-resonant (Edelstein) character is asserted, not measured.** Only one photon energy (1.2 eV) is used. Pump photon-energy dependence across the direct gap, or at least a comparison with sub-gap excitation, would test the claim that the response comes from near-resonant interband coherence at Γ rather than from a generic off-resonant magnetic-dipole rectification allowed in any centrosymmetric crystal. A wavelength dependence would also help separate bulk from surface emission (concern 3).

15. **The excitation conditions and possible helicity-dependent contributions need documentation.** The pump is described as "near-resonant with the direct gap at Γ", but the relevant interband transitions at 1.2 eV, the absorption coefficient and the probed depth are not given. The tensor analysis assumes bulk point group -3 throughout the excited volume, which a near-surface excitation makes less safe. Residual pump ellipticity from the half-wave plate, especially at 45° incidence, could add an inverse-Faraday M_z contribution whose sign depends on φ and might mimic part of the twofold pattern. The authors should document these quantities and add a circular-polarization control (which separates helicity-dependent inverse-Faraday, polarization-independent thermal and linear-polarization Edelstein channels) or a measured polarization purity.
