A. Must address

1. The Raman and bulk experiments use different perturbations, so the central claims of laser-induced, non-volatile resistive switching and of a structure–transport link are not demonstrated. Raman cycling was done only by laser heating: a ~50 µm spot, up to 150 mW at 532 nm, "almost instantaneous" heating and cooling, T_B ≈ 38 K, T_T grid 59, 81, 102, ... K. Magnetization and resistance cycling (Fig. 4) was done only on the whole pellet in a PPMS: global heating at 5 K/min, T_B = 30 K, T_T grid 50, 60, ... K. No transport or magnetization was measured on laser-cycled material, and no Raman spectrum was taken after cryostat cycling, so the "correlation" rests on qualitative similarity between two different experiments. The two histories are not equivalent: cooling rates differing by orders of magnitude strongly change the retained FMM fraction (kinetic arrest) in phase-separated manganites. The local Raman effect could also have a non-thermal part: visible light is known to photo-induce melting of charge-orbital order, photo-doping, light-induced metastability and insulator–metal switching in (Pr,La)CaMnO3, and strong thermal gradients and strain under the spot are absent in the PPMS. The standard heating data (Fig. 2) were never run as a cycling protocol. Yet the Abstract, Discussion and Conclusions claim "laser-induced thermal cycling to induce non-volatile structure/resistive-switching", "controlling phase separation and resistive properties through localized laser heating", "a novel method for ... inducing resistive switching", and that switching is "associated with the local structure for the first time". The authors should do at least one of the following: (a) repeat the Raman T_T sequence on a fresh spot using the cold-finger/cryostat heater for the excursion, with read-out at T_B at fixed low power (10 mW or less), at the same T_B, ramp rate and dwell as the PPMS runs, and compare directly with Fig. 3 — this would establish whether the effect is thermal and whether the laser adds anything; (b) measure resistance or magnetization across a region laser-cycled in situ (for example a narrow bar or bridge, a patterned two-terminal gap or constriction illuminated by the beam, or a contacted pellet in an optical cryostat). Otherwise the laser-related claims in the Abstract, Discussion and Conclusions should be removed or reduced to a qualitative correlation between two separate experiments on thermally driven, history-dependent resistivity.

2. The target-temperature scale T_T, the x-axis of the central result, is partly circular and its uncertainty is underestimated. T_T for every laser cycle comes from Table S1, built by "visual inspection and comparison" of power-dependent with temperature-dependent spectra, matched mainly on the width and relative intensity of the 250, 480 and 614 cm⁻¹ structures (Fig. S1: inflections at 75, 100, 125, 150 and 175 K matched to 40, 50, 60, 70 and 80 mW) on a ~25 K grid. These are the same JT-mode intensities and widths whose T_T dependence is then reported and whose thresholds are said to coincide with T_C, T_MI and T_COO, so that coincidence is partly built in. The matching was done on a continuous power ramp, itself a cumulative thermal history. Further problems: below ~75 K (40 mW) the spectra are admitted to be indistinguishable, so T_B = 38(4) K is pure extrapolation; the linear T(P) form and zero intercept are imposed (footnote, p. 12; a free fit gives −3(5) K), and a temperature-independent 21.8 K offset is added, with no explanation of why the intercept was fixed at 0 K rather than the cold-finger temperature; the optical absorption and thermal conductivity of LPCMO change strongly across T_C and T_COO (metallic versus insulating percolation), so linearity is not expected and the temperature reached at a given power should depend on the phase state of the spot, which by the paper's own claim changes from cycle to cycle; a Gaussian spot at these powers has strong radial and depth gradients, so "T_T" is a distribution, smeared precisely where the effect sits; Fig. S5d–f shows laser-heated integrated areas ~40% below cryostat-heated ones near the maximum (~4.5 vs ~7.5 in S5d), contradicting the stated "great agreement"; and the ±4–8 K errors are fit-propagation only. A related issue is that the read-out temperature at 10 mW is not verified after each cycle: if high-power exposure changes absorption, surface roughness or thermal contact, the "T_B" spectrum is taken at a different temperature, and since the JT intensity is a strongly non-monotonic function of temperature (Fig. 2d), a modest offset could mimic the effect. Anti-Stokes signal appears to have been recorded (down to about −250 cm⁻¹, Fig. S2) but absorbed into the background (Fig. S6). The authors should determine the local temperature independently from the Stokes/anti-Stokes ratio of the ~250 cm⁻¹ (or 485 cm⁻¹) mode, at each T_T and for every T_B spectrum in Fig. 3 (before and after cycling); give realistic uncertainties including the matching-grid resolution; estimate the radial and depth temperature profile within the 50 µm spot and the optical penetration depth (heat-diffusion model or spot-size series); and re-plot Fig. 3 on the independent scale.

3. The cumulative protocol confounds the T_T dependence with accumulated history, elapsed time, relaxation and illumination dose, and there is no null measurement. The cycles are cumulative, T_T increases monotonically and the same spot is probed throughout (Fig. 1b; Methods), so the state before cycle n is the state after cycle n−1 (Fig. 4c makes this explicit: M_i(n) ≈ M_f(n−1), and M_i changes from cycle to cycle). LPCMO in the DPS regime relaxes slowly at 30–40 K (Ghivelder2005, Sacanell2004, cited by the authors); the 5-minute wait and several-minute acquisitions per spectrum are comparable to or shorter than those times, so the rise and fall in Fig. 3c–e could partly reflect relaxation or drift at T_B. The observation is also what one expects from ordinary minor-loop hysteresis and kinetic arrest of a first-order transition: on warming above T_C^W ≈ 91 K, FMM converts to COO; on recooling from inside the hysteresis window (T_T < T_COO), part of that COO is retained at T_B; warming above T_COO erases it. To show that the state at T_B is a function of T_T and not of the full history, the authors should add: a waiting-time null (repeated spectra at T_B at 10 mW over the duration of a full sequence with no excursion); non-cumulative cycles (fresh cool-down from above T_COO or 300 K, then a single cycle to one T_T, for several T_T values), compared with the cumulative protocol; repeated cycles to the same T_T, to test saturation or accumulation; a decreasing-T_T or otherwise non-monotonic sequence (for example T_C < T_T < T_MI after a reset above T_COO); and time dependence at T_B (Raman intensity and ρ for at least tens of minutes after each cycle). The same applies to the M and ρ cycles.

B. Should address

4. The paper is not positioned against the existing literature on thermal memory, minor-loop hysteresis and kinetic arrest in phase-separated manganites, so the novelty is overstated. History-dependent FM fraction, hysteresis across the first-order FMM/COO transition, glassy relaxation and aging, cooling-rate dependence, and memory erased by heating above the charge-ordering temperature are well documented in LPCMO and related compounds (Ghivelder2005, Sacanell, Ward2011, Kundhikanjana2015, all cited, and the kinetic-arrest/CHUF literature). The M and ρ behaviour in Fig. 4 resembles what those works predict. Calling the effect a "previously unidentified regime" of structural irreversibility and a "new form of nonequilibrium phase dynamics" requires a direct comparison. The authors should state what is new beyond the local Raman/lattice read-out (for example a quantitative difference in the T_T dependence or a structural signature absent from transport), make that the main new result, and frame the M/ρ results accordingly. "Irreversible" is also a misnomer for a state that resets on heating above T_COO; "history-dependent" or "metastable" would be more accurate.

6. Possible laser-induced modification of the surface is not ruled out. The same spot is irradiated repeatedly up to 150 mW on ~50 µm (roughly 7–8 kW/cm²) over more than 15 cycles. A spurious peak near 660–670 cm⁻¹ (red dashed line, Fig. 2a) is attributed to "local oxygen vacancies" without evidence; a band near this frequency is also the fingerprint of laser-induced Mn₃O₄-like surface decomposition in manganites, and it grows strongly near 180–210 K in Fig. 2b. The background at high Raman shift is visibly higher after some cycles (Fig. 1e,f) and much higher at 150 mW (Fig. S6f). The authors should track the 660–670 cm⁻¹ band and the background through all cycles; show that a reset (and a final warm-up to room temperature and re-cool) reproduces the as-cooled spectrum quantitatively, beyond the visual comparison in Fig. 1h; measure a spot that went through the same cryostat history but was never illuminated at high power; and inspect the irradiated spot afterwards by optical microscopy or a micro-Raman map.

7. The intensity normalization and background subtraction may produce or distort the reported trends. JT intensities are divided by the ~250 cm⁻¹ rotational mode (Figs. 2d, 3c), but Fig. S5d shows this mode's area itself varies about fourfold with temperature, peaking near 120 K where the JT modes also peak, and changing with phase fraction (Fig. S5a,d). The normalization therefore shifts the apparent maximum (Figs. S1 and S5e,f place the JT maximum near 100–125 K; Fig. 2d near T_MI ≈ 150 K), and in Fig. 3c the reference at T_B may itself depend on history. Backgrounds were fitted with a fixed 7th-degree polynomial plus several broad Lorentzians (FWHM 50–120 cm⁻¹) over the whole range, fit quality was "judged based on the visual inspection", and the background shape changes markedly between conditions (Fig. 1e,f; Figs. S6a,b,e). The authors should show the absolute (unnormalized) fitted JT and rotational intensities at T_B versus T_T, including I_rot on its own; show that Fig. 3c–e holds with background-independent metrics (peak heights above a local linear baseline, integrated intensity in fixed windows), with at least one alternative background (lower-order polynomial or electronic-continuum form), with the background left in, and with a different number of Lorentzians; and report how laser focus and collection efficiency were kept constant between cycles.

8. JT-mode intensity is used as a measure of AFMI-COO phase fraction without validation, and structural characterization is limited to room temperature. The Discussion reads intensity gains as "strength of the COO phase" and FMM-fraction changes, and claims the strength "achieved at T_T is retained" by comparing intensities measured at T (Fig. 2d) with intensities measured at T_B after visiting T_T (Fig. 3c), including a "quantitative correspondence for T_T < T_C". But Raman intensity per phase depends also on temperature (Bose factor, polaron dynamics), on optical penetration depth (much shorter in metallic FMM regions), on resonance conditions at 532 nm (near JT-polaron and charge-transfer excitations) and on electronic screening by metallic regions. The interpretation rests on Pnma (FMM) versus P2₁/m (AFMI-COO), yet the synchrotron PXRD (Fig. S7) is at 278 K only. The authors should discuss these factors, and calibrate the JT intensity against an independent phase-fraction estimate: low-temperature synchrotron diffraction after selected cycles (P2₁/m vs Pnma fraction or superlattice intensity; their PAINEIRA access would allow this), saturated DC magnetization at T_B, or magnetic-field conversion of COO to FMM at T_B. A structural probe would also support the claim that the irreversibility is "structural" rather than purely electronic.

9. Non-volatility, the defining property of the claimed resistive switching, is not tested. Retention of the high-resistance state at T_B over time is not measured, there is no repeated set/reset test (T_C < T_T < T_MI followed by T_T > T_COO, several times), and the energy and time cost of a cycle are not given. Erasing requires heating above ~205 K and reading requires staying near 30–40 K; these operating conditions should be stated plainly if neuromorphic relevance is claimed. The authors should add a retention trace and a repeated write/erase test (ideally laser-driven, with four-probe contacts), or tone the claim down to "history-dependent resistance".

10. The "magnetization" is low-field AC susceptibility, not a measure of FMM fraction. M–T was measured in ACMS mode with a 10 Oe, 11 Hz AC field and no DC field, and reported as µB/Mn at the ~10⁻² level, two orders of magnitude below saturation. In a polycrystalline ferromagnet this response is dominated by domain-wall dynamics and demagnetization and is not proportional to FM volume fraction. The authors should label and report it as χ′ (and χ″), and add DC magnetization at a moderate field (of order 1 T) or M(H) loops up to saturation at T_B after selected cycles, so that the Raman and ρ changes can be related to a real FMM fraction.

15. The differences between local laser heating and bulk heating, and between surface and bulk probes, are not discussed. A laser-heated spot is mechanically constrained by a cold surrounding matrix, and phase separation in LPCMO is strongly strain-sensitive (as the introduction notes), so thermal stress may drive part of the Raman effect and is absent in uniform PPMS heating. Raman at 532 nm probes only the top tens of nm of a freshly broken polycrystalline surface, where surface strain and grain-boundary effects may shift the phase balance, while M and ρ are bulk-averaged. The authors should discuss these differences and estimate them where possible.
