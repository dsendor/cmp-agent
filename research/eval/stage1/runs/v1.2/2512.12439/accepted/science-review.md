A. Must address

1. Each condition rests on a single flake, so the central comparison is not shown to be reproducible. Each of the five conditions (7L/Al2O3, 4L/Al2O3, 18L/Au, 6L/Au, 4L/Au) appears to be one flake measured at one spot. The headline contrast, a sharp transition in 4L/Al2O3 (280–287 K) against a broad one in 4L/Au (about 220 ± 40 K), therefore rests on two flakes made by different processes. Thin-flake Tc is known to scatter: the authors cite 309 K for 8L on SiO2 and modest gate shifts in thin flakes. Their own Al2O3 data also show a steep thickness dependence (320 K at 7L, 280 K at 4L), so a one-layer thickness error, or a locally thinner region in the laser spot, could move Tc by tens of kelvin. Flake-to-flake differences in strain (Tc is strongly sensitive to uniaxial stress, Ref. 16), wrinkles, tears, defect density, or ALD and evaporation conditions could give shifts and broadenings of the same size, so the substrate effect cannot be separated from sample variation. The authors should state how many flakes of each kind were prepared and measured; measure at least two or three more 4L flakes on each substrate (ideally from different exfoliation runs, and ideally also 5–6L); show the spread of onset and width in Fig. 5; and give the AFM thickness of the measured spot relative to the laser spot size. The strongest test would be a single flake spanning both substrates, for example a laterally patterned Au/Al2O3 substrate made with the same transfer method, with Raman line scans across the boundary; this removes flake-to-flake variation entirely.

2. The data do not separate excitonic from electron-lattice mechanisms, and the quantitative split of Tc goes too far. The abstract, Discussion and Conclusions say the results "confirm the existence of excitonic effects", that Au "weaken[s] electron-hole interactions" rather than electron-lattice coupling, and that excitonic mechanisms account for about 150 K of the 326 K Tc with "the remaining ~180 K" from the lattice (pp. 15–16). This rests on untested assumptions: (a) the disappearance of the quasi-elastic peak in 4L/Au means excitonic fluctuations are destroyed rather than lost below the noise (see concern 6); (b) the only 16% drop of the maximum of −1/q (0.38 against 0.45) means unchanged electron-phonon coupling, although q depends on the continuum's density of states and its coupling, both of which change directly with doping and screening; (c) the interfacial layers lose their excitonic contribution completely; and (d) excitonic and lattice contributions to Tc add linearly, which is not true in general. Electron doping and metallic screening also suppress a purely hybridization-, band-Jahn-Teller- or phonon-driven instability (as in electron-doped CDW systems), by moving the Fermi level away from compensation and screening interband matrix elements, so a Tc reduction under charge transfer is expected in both pictures. Strain or interface chemistry (concern 3) would also lower Tc without being specifically excitonic. Further, the interfacial layers do not reach a monoclinic ground state above the lowest measured temperature, so "suppression by up to 150 K" is at best a lower bound on the perturbation of those layers, not a measure of the excitonic share; and measured against the 4L/Al2O3 baseline the Au-induced suppression is about 100 K, not 150 K (concern 12). The authors should either support the attribution with a model calculation (for example a minimal two-band model with phonon coupling plus a screened interaction, or a Landau/mean-field model with exciton-lattice coupling in which screening reduces only the excitonic coupling) showing that the observed pattern of Fano and quasi-elastic behaviour discriminates between the scenarios and predicts a Tc shift of the observed size, or soften these statements to "consistent with" and drop or qualify the numerical 150 K / 180 K split.

B. Should address

3. Screening and charge transfer are not separated from strain and interface chemistry, and charge transfer is asserted rather than measured. The paper attributes the whole Au/Al2O3 difference to screening and charge transfer from the metal (pp. 14–15, "the likely culprit"), but the two interfaces differ in much more than conductivity. Au is thermally evaporated at room temperature directly onto the Se-terminated surface, where it conforms to the surface ripples and binds strongly (p. 4); hot Au adatoms could intermix, form Au–Se bonds or diffuse into the first layer. Al2O3 is grown by ALD from TMA/H2O at 100–150 °C, a heated, chemically reactive process that could passivate or modify the first layer. The stacks also impose different interfacial strain and thermal-expansion mismatch on cooling, and Ta2NiSe5 is very sensitive to uniaxial strain (Refs. 14–17). An interfacial strain decaying away from the substrate would produce the same vertical Tc gradient the authors attribute to screening. The sharp 4L/Al2O3 transition rules out bulk oxidation but not an Au-specific chemical or strain effect. No direct evidence of charge transfer is given: no estimate of its amount or sign, no discussion of the work function of polycrystalline Au on Cr against that of Ta2NiSe5 (~5.4 eV, cited), no doping-sensitive phonon shift or change of the electronic background relative to Al2O3, and no XPS/UPS or Kelvin-probe data. The authors should: compare the frequencies and linewidths of strain-sensitive Ag phonons well above Tc (340–400 K, orthorhombic phase, including Ag(o) modes in the aa channel) and well below Tc across all five flakes to bound relative strain and doping; give an order-of-magnitude estimate of the transferred carrier density (for example from the work-function difference and an interfacial capacitance) and compare it with the doping levels at which K dosing or substitution suppress Tc; discuss interfacial strain explicitly; and ideally add controls that keep the Au but block charge transfer (Au on a 1–2 nm ALD Al2O3 or monolayer hBN spacer), an evaporated dielectric deposited the same way as Au, and a metal with a different work function such as Pt, which the authors themselves propose. Interface characterization (cross-sectional TEM/EDX or XPS) and an independent probe of doping and the gap (XPS/ARPES through the evaporated-Au route, which can end in UHV, or low-temperature reflectance of the gap edge) would further strengthen the attribution.

4. The depth-dependent optical weighting on a metal substrate is ignored, and it works against detecting the interfacial layers. The mixed-phase interpretation (pp. 13–14, Fig. 5b inset) assumes the Raman signal is an even average over all layers and that the 1–2 layers next to Au are "detectable in the overall signal". On an optically thick Au mirror, the 632.8 nm standing wave has a near-node at the metal surface, so for a 2.5–4 nm flake the layer next to Au is weighted far less than the top layer in both excitation and collection, while flakes on Al2O3 (50–100 nm above Au) sit in a very different standing-wave pattern. The ac signal of 4L/Au is about 50–100 times weaker than 4L/Al2O3 (scale factors ×25 and ×50 for 6L and 4L on Au in Figs. 3 and 4 against ×3 in Fig. 3c; colour bars 0.57 against 52.6), consistent with this. As a result, the interfacial layer whose Tc is supposedly suppressed by ~150 K may contribute little, and the low-temperature end of the transition (~180 K) is poorly constrained. The authors should give a transfer-matrix calculation of the layer-resolved Raman weight (incident × scattered field intensity) for each stack, including the Al2O3/Au stack under the Al2O3 flakes; use it as the layer weights in the toy model of Supplementary Note 4; and show that the measured lineshapes are consistent with the extent of the proposed interface region.

5. The vertical Tc gradient is inferred, not tested against uniform suppression or lateral inhomogeneity, and the toy model is not specified. Lateral inhomogeneity within the laser spot would also give a broadened, averaged transition: thickness steps, wrinkles or bubbles, strain from rough polycrystalline/amorphous Au, or patchy Au–flake contact. The spot size is not given and no spatial map is shown. The gradient picture also has internal tensions. The model assumes the layers far from Au have Tc "closer to room temperature" (p. 13), yet the dominant top layer of 4L/Au begins its transition at about 220–250 K (first kink, Fig. 3t), well below 4L/Al2O3 (280 K), which already lacks any claimed interface effect; this suggests suppression across the whole flake. And if 1–2 interfacial layers are perturbed, the affected fraction is about 2/4 in 4L but about 2/6 in 6L, yet both show the same ±40 K width and similar kink spacing. The authors should: show Raman maps, or spectra at several positions, on the 4L/Au and 6L/Au flakes at temperatures inside the broadened range, and check whether the width depends on position; state which Tc the toy model assigns to the top layers of 4L/Au (bulk, the 4L/Al2O3 value, or a free parameter) and show that it reproduces Fig. 3t and Fig. 4o quantitatively with physically motivated weights (concern 4); explain the similar widths of 6L and 4L; and present a 5L or 3L flake, or at least discuss why a gradient fits better than uniform suppression plus lateral spread.

6. The absence of the quasi-elastic peak in 4L/Au may be a sensitivity limit. The statement that the quasi-elastic peak is "suppressed beyond detection" (p. 12) is used on p. 15 as positive evidence that "affirms" its excitonic origin. But the 4L/Au signal is ×50 weaker (Fig. 4t), the χ''(10 cm^-1) cut lies near the filter edge, and the Rayleigh leakage at 15 cm^-1 was removed by interpolation in exactly the 6L and 4L Au datasets (Methods, p. 19). The authors should show the raw, uncorrected low-energy spectra and a noise estimate for the 4L and 6L Au data; normalize the 10 cm^-1 intensity to an internal reference such as the integrated Ag(m2)/B2g(o1) intensity in the same spectrum; give an upper bound on the quasi-elastic amplitude and show that a peak of the expected relative size would have been detectable; and demonstrate that the Rayleigh removal does not affect the 10 cm^-1 point.

7. Laser heating and the sample temperature are not quantified per flake, and the >300 K setup is not described. The laser power is about 1 mW and the effective temperature was "checked" with the Stokes/anti-Stokes ratio (p. 18), but no values are given. The two stacks differ in absorption (metal mirror against 50–100 nm Al2O3 over Au) and in thermal anchoring (direct Au contact against an ALD oxide), so a heating offset of 10–30 K that differs between flakes could shift apparent Tc by an amount comparable to the 40 K suppression of 4L/Al2O3. The Methods describe a cryostat only "for measurements below 300 K"; it is not stated how the 300–400 K spectra were taken or whether the setup or window calibration changes near 300 K, exactly where the 7L/Al2O3 and 18L/Au transitions (~320 K) lie. The authors should report Stokes/anti-Stokes temperature against nominal temperature for each flake (at least the two 4L flakes), show a power dependence of a Tc marker (for example the Ag(m5) intensity near Tc) for 4L/Al2O3 and 4L/Au, describe the high-temperature setup and show that the data join continuously across 300 K, and state that the Bose correction of Eq. (2) uses the calibrated temperature.

8. The transition width is defined by an ad hoc "second kink" construction. The ±40 K broadening for 6L and 4L on Au (pp. 9–10) comes from the spacing between the first and second kinks in the Ag(m4) position: 100 K on Au against 60 K elsewhere. This assumes the second kink lies a fixed 60 K below Tc (an offset taken from only three flakes, which could itself depend on doping or strain) and that its broadening maps directly onto the Tc distribution. Neither is justified, and the kinks in Figs. 3s and 3t are "broadened and less defined", on peak positions quantized at 1.3 cm^-1. The authors should justify or drop the second-kink argument and instead fit one model the same way to all five flakes: for example the temperature dependence of the integrated Ag(m4)/Ag(m5) intensity in the ac channel (which scales with the square of the monoclinic order parameter), or −1/q(T), fitted to an order-parameter form convolved with a Gaussian distribution of Tc, reporting fitted centres and widths with uncertainties.

9. Temperature smoothing may create or exaggerate the broadening. The 2D maps are resampled on a 5 K grid and Gaussian-smoothed with σ = 10–15 K in temperature (Methods, p. 19), comparable to the 10 K measurement steps near Tc and to the widths of the sharp transitions. Figs. 3k–o are second derivatives of these smoothed maps, and the peak positions in Figs. 3p–t appear to be taken from them. For the noisy 4L/Au data, smoothing plus differentiation can turn a weak, noisy onset into an apparently gradual one. The authors should state whether Figs. 3p–t use raw or smoothed spectra and whether the same smoothing was applied to every sample, and show peak positions from fits to the raw spectra at each measured temperature, with uncertainties, alongside Figs. 3p–t.

Because the quasi-elastic peak is undetectable in 4L/Au, Tc^avg ≈ 220 ± 46 K (p. 12) is obtained by shifting the 6L/Au −1/q curve by about 30 K and borrowing the 6L width; the ±46 K is copied from 6L. 12. The 40 K suppression of 4L on Al2O3 is unexplained and the thickness series is not matched. 13. The structural Tc of the sharp samples relies on a polarizer-leakage mode. The structural Tc is defined by a kink in the position of Ag(o3)*, an aa mode that leaks into the ac channel through polarizer misalignment (pp. 7, 9). The degree of leakage, and hence the weight of this feature against the intrinsic ac modes, can differ between flakes and cooldowns. The authors should confirm the Tc values with a criterion based only on intrinsic ac modes, for example the onset temperature of Ag(m5) intensity, for all five flakes.
