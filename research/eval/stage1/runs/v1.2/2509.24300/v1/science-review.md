A. Must address

1. No independent evidence for Cooper pairs between Tc^onset and about 220 K. The headline claim (abstract, p. 5, Conclusion) is that preformed, localized Cooper pairs and mobile vortices dual to them exist up to about 220 K in sample 4 and 183 K in sample 5. The only support is the functional form of R(T) (R linear in ln(1/T) rather than G linear in ln T, Fig. 3a,b) and the sign of β'. No measurement in the paper probes pairs or vortices. The supporting diamagnetism (p. 5) is neither shown nor quantified for these samples. It is taken from another sample set (Ref. [20]) at about 140 K, which does not match the proposed 220 K / 183 K onsets and is described as "close" to them. It is argued alongside Ref. [27], which has been retracted, and Ref. [28], which attributes the 220 K diamagnetism to Joule heating. Other mechanisms also give ρ ~ −ln T: Kondo-like or magnetic scattering (plausible given the ferromagnetic transition near 180 K reported in Ref. [20]), electron-electron interaction corrections, and any smooth crossover seen over a narrow window. The authors should add at least one probe specific to pairing and vortices, measured on the same samples. Magnetoresistance in perpendicular and parallel fields across 100-250 K (and its comparison with the 220-300 K window) is the cheapest. Fermionic weak localization gives negative MR of Hikami-Larkin-Nagaoka form with a field scale set by the dephasing length; Kondo or magnetic scattering gives isotropic negative MR; localized pairs and fluctuation superconductivity give positive, orbital MR with a field scale tied to 2e. A Nernst signal for vortex motion, magnetization or diamagnetic susceptibility of the same flakes with measured temperature dependence, a tunneling gap, or the field dependence of the "vertical flow" temperature would also serve. Without one of these, the 220 K Cooper-pair statement should be withdrawn or reworded as "consistent with", not evidence of, bosonic localization, and the diamagnetism discussion should be either backed by nanoflake magnetization data or softened.

2. The ρ ~ ln(1/T) versus σ ~ ln T discrimination rests on temperature windows too narrow to decide it. The boson/fermion assignment for samples 4 and 5 rests on Fig. 3(a,b). For sample 4 the Bose-glass window is 101-220 K (ln T 4.6-5.4, Δln T ≈ 0.8, a factor of about 2.2 in T) and R changes by about 50%. For sample 5 it is 105-183 K (Δln T ≈ 0.55, factor about 1.7), R changes by about 25%, and the data scatter visibly. The "Fermi glass" windows are narrower still: about 220-300 K for samples 4 and 5 (Δln T ≈ 0.3) and about 210-300 K for samples 1-3 (Fig. 2b, a factor of about 1.35-1.4). For small fractional changes, R linear in ln(1/T) and G linear in ln T are nearly the same function and differ only at second order; the lower panel of Fig. 3(a) shows that G versus ln T for sample 4 is also close to linear over much of the window. No goodness of fit, residuals, fit uncertainties or objective criterion for the window boundaries is given; the boundaries appear to be chosen by eye where one fit departs from the other, and the sample 4 window end (101 K) sits next to the resistance maximum at ~80-100 K, so the preference for ln(1/T) may simply reflect the start of the downturn. Physically, quantum-interference corrections at 100-300 K in an oxide need a phase-coherence length longer than the mean free path, which is hard to sustain where phonon scattering dominates. Moreover, samples 4 and 5 have R□/layer of 30-125 kΩ, i.e. g = (ħ/e²)/R of about 0.03-0.15 per layer, well outside the weak-localization regime (g ≫ 1, k_F ℓ ≫ 1) assumed by Das-Doniach and by the −1/g branch of β_VW. Phonon scattering, thermally activated carriers, parallel conduction through secondary phases, Altshuler-Aronov corrections and Kondo-like scattering are not considered. The authors should report residuals or reduced χ² for R ~ ln(1/T), G ~ ln T, activated, VRH and power-law forms in each window, with and without a phonon or background term; show sensitivity to where the window ends are placed, or that the preferred form holds over a wider range; and estimate k_F ℓ, the mean free path and the dephasing length for each sample to justify a quantum-interference description at these temperatures.

3. The β-function "vertical flow" and the β' sign criterion follow from the resistance maximum and do not uniquely identify bosons. β_EXP = −(2/p) d ln g/d ln T is computed from the same R(T) curves. From Eqs. (1)-(4), β' < 0 means only that |β| shrinks as T falls, i.e. the resistance rise slows on a ln T scale; the sign of β' depends only on the curvature of R against ln T, and Eqs. (3)-(4) give opposite signs only if the data follow one of the two pure log forms exactly, which assumes the conclusion. For samples 4 and 5 R(T) has a maximum near 80-110 K (Fig. 1c), where β passes through zero, ln|β| diverges, and β must move vertically at almost fixed ln g, producing the rise in Fig. 5(b) and Fig. 6(a) whatever the physics. Weak localization plus electron-electron interaction corrections plus Aslamazov-Larkin/Maki-Thompson fluctuation conductivity above a transition near 80-100 K would give the same upturn and the same sign of β', as would a crossover between two conduction channels. The MBE samples 6 and 7 never superconduct yet are also assigned β' < 0; their β values hardly vary with T in Fig. 6(a), and their arrow in Fig. 5(b) points left rather than up, while saturation or percolation would also slow their resistance rise. The fixed choice p = 1 in L² = D T^(−p) (p. 7) ties length to temperature without justification. The authors should fit R(T) of samples 4 and 5 with a fermionic WL/EEI background plus 2D fluctuation conductivity (stating the fitted Tc and normal-state background) and show quantitatively that this fails where the bosonic form succeeds, or that β' < 0 survives the subtraction; show that the vertical flow begins well above the resistance maximum; show its absence in a control sample with a resistance maximum of non-superconducting origin (magnetic or CaRuO3-type anomaly); show the dependence on p; and explain the MBE-film assignment separately. Without this, Figs. 5 and 6 restate the R(T) curvature rather than giving independent evidence of localized pairs.

4. The phase identity and structure of the measured flakes are not established, and the "relieved octahedral distortion" mechanism is not measured. The Methods distinguish Ca2RuO4 from CaRuO3 only "by observing the crystal shape using a scanning electron microscope". The c-axis value 12.278 Å attributed to "nanoscale single crystals" appears to come from powder XRD (Rigaku Miniflex) of the parent material, not the measured flakes; no Raman, TEM, electron diffraction or flake-level lattice parameters are given. CaRuO3 is a metal with resistivity of order 10⁻⁴-10⁻³ Ω cm, the same range as the nanofilm resistivities in Fig. 1(a,b) and 3-4 orders below bulk Ca2RuO4. Other Ruddlesden-Popper intergrowths (e.g. Ca3Ru2O7), the metallic L phase, Ru-rich inclusions (Ru metal, RuO2), Ca-deficient surface layers from sonication, or oxygen non-stoichiometry (Ca2RuO4+δ) would each lower the resistivity by orders of magnitude without any change in correlation or distortion, and Ru inclusions or filamentary paths could produce superconducting-like drops. The statements that "the distortion in RuO6 octahedra is eliminated" (p. 6), that thinning relieves the distortion (abstract, p. 4, Conclusion), and that impurity-induced disorder "cannot explain this phenomenon" / "thinning does not introduce disorder" (p. 6, Conclusion) are therefore assertions, not results, although the headline interpretation depends on the conducting channel being Ca2RuO4. The authors should provide flake-level characterization of the measured devices or sister flakes from the same batch: Raman (which separates the S and L phases from CaRuO3), TEM or electron diffraction giving in-plane and c-axis lattice parameters and octahedral tilt and rotation, and EDX/EELS stoichiometry; a resistivity check against CaRuO3 reference values; and ideally temperature-dependent structural data.

6. The two "quantum critical" resistances are taken from finite-temperature data with no critical scaling, and the β_c → R_c mapping is undefined. A quantum critical point needs a continuous tuning parameter and a separatrix: a temperature-independent R at R_c, with collapse of R(T, x) onto F(|x − x_c|/T^{1/zν}). In Fig. 6(a), β_c = −1.6 is a horizontal line drawn between groups of different samples at 100-250 K; it is not a crossing point, no sample sits at β_c with dβ/dT ≈ 0, and samples 6 and 7 near −1 to −1.5 at low T overlap it. The text says the resistance is "estimated from g_c = −1.6", conflating β_c with g (a positive dimensionless conductance), and the conversion is not given; with g = (ħ/e²)σ, R = 65.7 kΩ corresponds to g ≈ 0.063 and ln g ≈ −2.8, and Eq. (1) uses ħ/e² while the critical values are quoted in units of h/e² and h/4e², so factors of 2π must be kept consistent. In Fig. 6(b), 65.7 kΩ is the low-temperature value of the 5000 nA curve and 16.5 kΩ sits near the 150 nA curves: bias current is not a thermodynamic tuning parameter, is not shown to produce a separatrix, the curves at the quoted values are not flat in T, and flat low-T curves may be heating or depinning plateaus (see concern 7). The authors should (i) give the explicit relation from β_c to R_c, (ii) show a separatrix in R(T) against a continuous thermodynamic tuning parameter (thickness series, gate or magnetic field at fixed thickness) with temperature or finite-size scaling and the exponent product zν, and derive β_c from a crossing or scaling analysis, and (iii) quote R_c with uncertainties including the per-layer normalization (concern 5). Without these, the language of "two quantum critical points" (abstract, p. 8) is too strong.

11. Definition of g and the "universal curve" in Fig. 5. g is defined as (ħ/e²)σ, which for σ in S/cm is not dimensionless; for a 2D β function g should be a sheet conductance per layer. The paper does not say how g is computed for the bulk Nakatsuji data (in Ω cm, three-dimensional) or for 25-300 nm films, yet all are placed on a curve whose weak-localization branch is two-dimensional. The collapse onto β_VW in the strongly localized regime is nearly automatic: for any VRH or activated law ln g ≈ c − (T0/T)^x, β ≈ (2x/p)(ln g − c), e.g. slope 2/3 for 2D VRH, so the slope is set by the hopping exponent rather than universality. 15. The Bose-glass and quantum-critical terminology departs from standard usage. In the Fisher framework the Bose glass is an insulating, compressible T = 0 phase with zero conductivity; the abstract's "absence of global phase coherence yet finite conductivity" and "bosonic order" follow the Das-Doniach usage of weakly localized bosons, and a finite-conductivity bosonic state is usually called a Bose or anomalous metal. "Mott insulator" in boson SIT language also differs from the fermionic Mott insulator of Ca2RuO4. A phase entered at 220 K and left at about 100 K on cooling is a finite-temperature crossover, yet its boundaries are called "quantum critical points", and it is unclear what the Bose glass in Fig. 4(a) would be at T = 0. The authors should state which definitions they use, what experimental property distinguishes the phases, and separate finite-temperature crossovers from ground-state claims in Fig. 4(a).
