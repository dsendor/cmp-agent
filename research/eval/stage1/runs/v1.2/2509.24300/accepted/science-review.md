A. Must address

1. No measurement tests whether the carriers in the 100-220 K regime are Cooper pairs. The headline claims (abstract; Results p. 2; Conclusion) are that "signatures of Cooper pairs persist up to high temperatures of ~220 K" and that localized Cooper pairs and moving vortices exist at 220 K in sample 4. Every Bose glass assignment (Figs. 3, 5, 6) comes from zero-field R(T): R ~ ln(1/T) is said to fit better than G ~ ln T over 101-220 K (sample 4) or 105-183 K (sample 5), plus the sign of beta' derived from the same curves. Neither is unique to bosons. The only other support is powder magnetization from another study, tied to a retracted reference (ref. 34) and a debated one (ref. 35), with no data shown (see concern 14). Bosonic weak localization of charge-2e carriers has signatures that fermionic weak localization or electron-electron interaction corrections do not share: a magnetoconductance whose characteristic field is set by h/2e through the localization area; a large positive magnetoresistance in the Bose glass window that turns over at a pair-breaking field, whereas a fermionic regime should give negative magnetoresistance of Hikami-Larkin-Nagaoka form; and a field-tuned insulator transition with a crossing point and finite-size scaling. The authors should measure R(T, B) on samples 4 and 5 (and 6/7, with samples 1-3 as fermionic controls), in perpendicular and parallel field, from about 250 K through the superconducting onset. They should state the bosonic and fermionic predictions for the sign and field scale of the magnetoresistance, show which the data follow, show that the magnetoresistance changes qualitatively at the claimed crossover (about 220 K for sample 4, 183 K for sample 5) but not in samples 1-3 at the same temperatures, extract the phase-coherence length, and report the field dependence of the BG-FG crossover temperature. Hall data for the carrier density would help, and, if feasible, a probe of the 2e charge (Little-Parks or h/2e oscillations in a patterned ring, shot noise), a Nernst signal at 150-200 K, or tunneling evidence of a gap would be direct. Without at least the magnetoresistance, the claim should be reduced to a logarithmic-in-1/T resistance regime of unknown origin, presented as one possibility alongside electron-electron interaction corrections, Kondo-like scattering and the parallel-channel model of concern 4.

2. The data do not distinguish R ~ ln(1/T) from G ~ ln T, and the fit windows are chosen by eye. In Fig. 3a (sample 4) the Bose glass window is ln T = 4.6 to 5.4 (101-220 K), a factor of about 2 in T, over which R changes by about 35%; in Fig. 3b (sample 5) the window is 105-183 K, a factor of 1.7, with R changing by about 25% (roughly 32 to 40 kOhm). Over such ranges a straight line in R vs ln T and in G vs ln T differ only at second order in the fractional change, so the statement that the data "align well" with R ~ ln(1/T) "rather than" G ~ ln T cannot be judged without statistics. The same applies to the Fermi glass windows (for example sample 4, 220 to about 290 K; Fig. 2b, 181-299 K). The window edges (arrows and dashed lines in Figs. 2 and 3) are chosen by eye, and the fit ranges themselves define the crossover temperatures plotted in Fig. 4a. The authors should fit both forms, plus a power law, an Altshuler-Aronov form, a G ~ ln T term plus an Aslamazov-Larkin fluctuation term with T_c0 near 30-90 K, and a two-channel parallel-resistor model, over identical windows on the 100-290 K data of samples 4 and 5; report residuals, reduced chi-squared or information criteria; define the window edges objectively (for example where residuals exceed a stated threshold); show that the preferred form and the crossover temperatures are robust when the edges move; and give uncertainties on the crossover temperatures in Fig. 4a.

3. The beta-function "vertical flow" is not an independent criterion and can arise from any resistance maximum. The Fig. 5 caption calls it "an independent criterion", but Eqs. (2)-(4) show that the sign of beta' is simply the curvature of ln g (or R) against ln T, so Fig. 6a and Fig. 5b restate what Fig. 3 already tests. More importantly, since beta = -(2/p) d ln g / d ln T, beta passes through zero at any extremum of R(T): near it g barely changes while beta sweeps through a large range, drawing a vertical line in beta vs ln g whatever the transport mechanism. Samples 4 and 5 have such a maximum near 80-100 K (Figs. 1c, 3a, 3b), which the authors attribute to superconducting onset, and any R(T) that rises on cooling and then rolls over must bend this way just above it. The vertical segments in Fig. 5b extend to positive beta, which corresponds to dR/dT > 0, the fluctuation side of the maximum rather than the Bose glass window. Mundane causes of the same curvature include Aslamazov-Larkin or Maki-Thompson fluctuation conductance growing on cooling, a parallel metallic or superconducting minority channel (concern 4), and temperature-dependent current redistribution in an inhomogeneous flake. The authors should state which temperature interval each vertical segment in Fig. 5b covers and mark the resistance maximum on it; show that the vertical flow begins near 220 K and persists when only data above the maximum are used; and compute beta for a model fermionic curve (for example G = G0 + G1 ln T plus an Aslamazov-Larkin term, or a two-channel model) and overlay it, to show whether the vertical flow requires bosons.

4. Phase purity of the measured flakes, and the origin of their low resistivity, are not established, so parallel conduction through a metallic minority phase is not excluded. The source crystals contain about 5% CaRuO3 and 6% S-Pbca phase (Methods), and flakes are told apart from CaRuO3 only by crystal shape in SEM, which does not exclude intergrowths. CaRuO3 is a metal with resistivity of order 10^-4 to 10^-3 Ohm cm, overlapping that of samples 4 and 5 (about 2 x 10^-3 Ohm cm, Fig. 1b), which are 3-4 orders of magnitude below bulk Ca2RuO4. A CaRuO3 inclusion or surface layer, oxygen excess (Ca2RuO4+delta is metallic), Ru-rich or oxygen-deficient regions, an interfacial layer on SiO2, or surface damage from sonication in dichloroethane and electron-beam lithography would lower rho and |Delta rho|; a percolating metallic channel in parallel with insulating Ca2RuO4, or a granular metal or Josephson-coupled granular superconductor (Efetov-type sigma ~ ln T), naturally gives logarithmic-like R(T), a broad resistance maximum, and broad or incomplete superconducting transitions. No structural or chemical measurement was made on any measured flake. The authors should characterize the measured flakes, or flakes from the same batch (at minimum for samples 4 and 5), with Raman spectroscopy (which distinguishes L/S Ca2RuO4 and CaRuO3), TEM or electron diffraction including cross-sections, and EDX/EELS stoichiometry, and should show explicitly, including by a two-channel fit, why a mixed-phase or parallel-conduction picture cannot account for Figs. 1-3.

6. "R per layer" and the dimensionless conductance g are not defined. Figs. 3 and 6 and the critical resistances use R_square per layer, presumably R_square times the number of RuO2 layers, which depends on the tilted-SEM thickness estimate (uncertainty not given), the layer spacing, and the untested assumption that layers conduct independently in parallel. g is defined as (h/e^2) sigma, but for samples 1-3 sigma appears to be a 3D conductivity in S/cm, so ln g carries units and is offset arbitrarily relative to samples 4 and 5; Fig. 5 also places bulk 3D resistivities (Nakatsuji 1 and 2) and the 300 nm sample 8 on the same ln g axis without saying how g was obtained. 7. The "quantum critical resistances" are not determined as critical points, and their values depend on assumptions. R^BG-FG = 65.7 kOhm appears to come from the condition beta' = 0 at beta_c = -1.6, read off the Vollhardt-Wölfle curve (Fig. 5a gives ln g of about -0.9, g of about 0.39, about 66 kOhm), at about 150-220 K; the steps from beta_c to a resistance are not given. beta_c is a horizontal line separating two groups of samples in Fig. 6a, not a sign change within one sample, and it scales as 2/p, so p = 2 or 3 would change it, and the inferred resistance, completely (concern 8). The measured resistances at the marked crossovers are about 80 kOhm per layer for sample 4 (Fig. 3a, ln T about 5.4) and about 32 kOhm for sample 5 (Fig. 3b), a factor of 2.5 apart and bracketing 65.7 kOhm. Sample 4 superconducts although its R per layer (about 125 kOhm at maximum) exceeds both "critical" values, and sample 5 (28-40 kOhm) exceeds 16.5 kOhm, contrary to the usual meaning of a critical resistance. In Fig. 6b the 65.7 and 16.5 kOhm lines mark where the highest-current curve levels off and roughly where curves meet at low T; no temperature-independent separatrix or scaling collapse (with exponent z nu) in current, thickness or Delta rho is shown. Finally, 65.7 kOhm differs from (5/2)h/e^2 = 64.5 kOhm, and the paper offers two unrelated explanations for the factor 2.5 (8/pi from ref. 42 and the 5/2 Pfaffian state, ref. 43). The authors should state which sample and temperature gives each value and exactly how it was obtained; propagate thickness, layer-count, geometry and p uncertainties; show whether different samples give the same value; and either show a crossing or collapse defining a critical point or call these characteristic crossover resistances. The 5/2, 8/pi and Pfaffian comparisons (pp. 6-7) should be removed or labelled as numerical coincidences unless the error bar is well below the differences between those numbers.

8. The 2D weak-localization framework of Eq. (1) is applied without justifying its preconditions. p = 1 (electron-electron dephasing) is assumed over 100-300 K, where electron-phonon dephasing (p of about 2-3) usually dominates in oxides; since beta scales as 1/p, the absolute beta values in Figs. 5 and 6 and beta_c = -1.6 depend on this choice. The ln T and ln(1/T) forms assume 2D transport, but L_phi at 100-300 K is likely only a few nm, smaller than the 10-35 nm thickness, and weak localization at 220 K requires a phase-coherence length exceeding the relevant scales; none of this is estimated. The authors should estimate L_phi or the localization length in this range (for example from magnetoresistance, concern 1), compare it with the thickness, and show how the BG/FG classification, Figs. 5 and 6, and beta_c change for p = 2 and 3.

11. The bias-current data of Fig. 6b may be dominated by heating and are inconsistent with Fig. 3a. Ca2RuO4 is known for current-driven metallization and Joule-heating artifacts (refs. 28, 34, 35), and currents up to 5 uA through a 10 nm flake on SiO2 at kOhm to 100 kOhm resistances in a 3He system can raise the electron temperature substantially; the separatrix of a family of R(T) curves at different currents is not a quantum critical point. 12. The phase diagram of Fig. 4a is partly circular, and its localization axis is a proxy for resistivity magnitude. Both coordinates come from the same R(T) curve: the horizontal is a crossover temperature from the ln T fits and the vertical is |rho(120 K) - rho(280 K)|/160 K. 20. Terminology. "Fermi glass" conventionally denotes an Anderson-localized insulator with a finite density of localized states; applying it to a weakly localized sigma ~ ln T conductor is non-standard and should be defined.
