# Merged review

## A. Must address

1. **The upturn is not shown to be diffuse magnon scattering rather than a pump-induced change in the shape of the magnetic Bragg peak.** The paper argues (p. 4–5) that the intensity rise 0.01–0.08 r.l.u. from q_RXD comes from magnons and not from a change of the peak shape, because "the (1/2 0 -1/2) RXD peak width remains nearly constant while the tail intensity rises (Fig. 2C)". The Fig. 2C caption itself says the top of the peak is missing because the detector saturates (the hatched region in Fig. 3), and no unsaturated profile versus delay is shown, so neither width, position nor integrated intensity can be judged. Several lineshape effects would raise the tail intensity with a delayed, q-dependent rise: (i) transient thermal or magnetoelastic strain — the X-ray probe depth is ~18 nm and the pump penetration ~450 nm, so strain fronts cross the probed layer in a few ps, the timescale of the upturn (τ_RDS up to ~4 ps in Fig. 3F), and an inhomogeneous strain gradient broadens the peak symmetrically in ±q, reproducing the symmetric amplitude profile (Fig. 3F) and a faster rise far from q_RXD; (ii) a change of the magnetic correlation length or mosaic; (iii) a small shift of the magnetic propagation vector. Because the surface normal (1 0 -1) is close to q_RXD, a strain-induced shift is most relevant along [h 0 h], yet that path is measured on the negative-q side only (Fig. S9A), where the upturns are largest and grow almost linearly to 4 ps, so the ±q symmetry test is missing exactly where it matters. The resonance (Fig. 2E), π > σ dependence (Fig. 2G) and fluence threshold (Fig. 2F) show the signal is magnetic, not that it is diffuse rather than a Bragg tail. The authors should provide attenuated, unsaturated θ–2θ and rocking profiles (or 2D maps with the 0.5M Jungfrau already used for Fig. S7) at 3 mJ/cm² at several delays (e.g. 0, 1, 4, 20–100 ps, 1 ns), with fitted peak position, width and integrated intensity versus delay; test whether any combination of broadening and shift reproduces the tail increase and show that the upturn survives after these are fitted and subtracted; measure both signs of q along [h 0 h]; and compare the measured q-profile of the upturn with that predicted by Eqs. 1–2 for magnon populations (e.g. the weighting by A_μ(q)/ω(q)). Without this the central identification of the signal is not secure.

2. **There is no equilibrium temperature calibration, so heating towards T_N1 with critical magnetic scattering is not excluded as the origin of the "non-thermal → quasi-thermal" magnon dynamics.** The sample sits at 190 K and the authors' own estimate puts the transient heating at up to ~20 K (p. S7), and the 18 nm probed surface layer is where the absorbed energy density is largest. This places the probed layer within a few kelvin of T_N1 (213 K in the text, 214.5 K in Fig. S3), where equilibrium critical diffuse scattering at small |q| grows steeply with temperature and the RXD intensity changes steeply. A delayed rise peaked at small |q| with a q-dependent rise time is what one would expect if the probed layer were simply heating towards T_N1; this also bears on the ns-scale RDS signal in Fig. 4D, and on the RXD at 12 ns, which recovers to ~-10% after the energy should have passed to the lattice. The authors should measure the static (unpumped) RDS intensity at the q points of Fig. 3A and the (1/2 0 -1/2) RXD intensity on this crystal versus temperature from ~150 K through T_N1 and T_N2; use these to convert the 4 ps plateau, the 4–100 ps RDS and the 12 ns RXD values into effective temperatures, and test whether the pumped RDS and RXD match a single equilibrium temperature (thermal magnons) or not (supporting a non-thermal distribution); redo the heating estimate for the 18 nm probed layer rather than the average; and add a temperature null control at the same detuned q just above T_N2 and well below T_N1 to test whether the upturn and its q-dependent time constant follow the magnon population. This is the central test of the "non-thermal → quasi-thermal" claim; without it the ps dynamics can equally be read as fast local heating plus critical fluctuations.

## B. Should address

3. **The magnon–magnon QBE comparison with the momentum-resolved data is qualitative, and the simulation is not set up for the experimental conditions.** The evidence for ps quasi-thermalization is a by-eye comparison of Fig. 3G (simulation, 0–6 ps, colour scale 0–800) with Fig. 3E (data, 0–4 ps, arbitrary units); simulated rise times are never extracted with the same fit and overlaid on Fig. 3F/3I, and simulated and measured traces are never compared with a single overall scale factor. The excitation B₀Δn(t) was chosen "to reproduce the experimentally observed reduction of the AFM order by approximately 50%" (p. S13), but Fig. 2D shows a ~28% drop in RXD intensity at 3 mJ/cm², i.e. ~15% reduction of the sublattice moment if I ∝ M²; if the simulation is overdriven by ~3×, the magnon density and quartic scattering rates are overestimated. The QBE grid (32 × 48 × 32, spacing ~0.02–0.03 r.l.u.) is as coarse as the measured q points (0.010–0.025 r.l.u.), and the δ-function broadening of 3.0 meV (p. S15) is comparable to the acoustic magnon energies there (gap ~1.3 meV, Fig. S13C), so the simulated near-Γ momentum dependence (Fig. 3G/H, Fig. S16) may be set by interpolation and broadening. The model has two degenerate magnon branches (p. S13), whereas the main text assigns the signal to the acoustic branch because its 1.3 meV gap differs from the 23.2 meV optical gap, and invokes optical-magnon decay in the Conclusions and Fig. S8; the model cannot test either. Because quartic scattering conserves magnon number, the quasi-thermal state should be a Bose–Einstein distribution with non-zero chemical potential, which is the physical origin of the claimed bottleneck and should be shown. The authors should rerun the QBE with the excitation matched to the measured RXD drop, show convergence in grid density and kernel width, fit the simulated traces exactly as the data and overlay τ_RDS(q) and A_RDS(q) from theory and experiment along both b* and [h 0 h], state which gap the model has and how the optical branch enters, and show the chemical potential of the quasi-thermalized distribution.

4. **The two-exponential decomposition and the extracted τ_RDS(q), the key observable, need firmer footing.** Eq. S3 as written, A·exp(−t/τ), decays to zero and cannot describe a step-like drop or a rise (Figs. S6C/F); the actual function fitted, including any (1 − e^(−t/τ)) form, convolution with the ~60 fs instrument response and time-zero handling, must be stated. The fit window is 4 ps (Fig. 3A, Fig. S9A), yet τ_RDS reaches ~4 ps near q = 0.01 (Fig. 3F) and ~13 ps with A_RDS ≈ 18 along [h 0 h] (Fig. S9E), so amplitude and time constant are strongly correlated and the quoted errors may understate the uncertainty; these near-Γ points carry the 1/τ ∝ q claim (Fig. 3I). Fig. S8 shows the upturn is non-monotonic (maximum ~5 ps, plateau after ~10 ps), so a single exponential may bias τ. The decomposition also assumes zero magnon contribution just after excitation, whereas the authors' own model (Fig. S14B, Fig. 3J) creates magnons across the zone instantaneously, adding a prompt positive RDS step that the fit would absorb into the negative "RXD" term. The largest amplitudes and longest τ occur in the APD channels nearest the saturated region, and single-shot XFEL pulses can drive APDs non-linear. The APD momentum acceptance is not stated: Fig. S5 gives Bragg widths of ~10⁻³ r.l.u. along b*, yet Fig. 2C shows substantial intensity at 0.025–0.075 r.l.u. — is this detector acceptance, equilibrium critical diffuse scattering at 0.9 T_N, or static disorder? The authors should give the fitted function, parameter covariances, an attenuator-series linearity calibration at the near-q_RXD channels, the APD acceptance in r.l.u. folded into the analysis, longer delay scans (as in Fig. S8) at several small-|q| points, and test whether τ(q) survives a global fit with the q-independent RXD time constant shared (Fig. 3D) and a fit allowing a prompt RDS step.

5. **The anomalous magnon–phonon mechanism for the ns recovery and the "dispersion-mismatch bottleneck" are inferred, not tested, because the coupling constant is arbitrary.** On p. S17 the coupling is fixed at c^x = 1 eV/Å for both normal and anomalous processes without justification. Rates scale as |c^x|², so the absolute anomalous rate (Fig. 4E/F, Fig. S17, ~0.2 ns⁻¹) and its agreement with ~7 ns is not a prediction; only the relative phase-space structure is. The "effective magnon–phonon coupling ~59 µeV" (p. 9) is back-derived or has no stated derivation, so the claim that coupling is small because of phase-space restriction rather than a small matrix element is not demonstrated, and comparing it with the 6.2 meV Raman spin–phonon coupling (ref. 44) mixes different definitions. The authors should fix the vertex independently (∇J from frozen-phonon DFT+U, which they already run, measured spin–phonon shifts, or magnetostriction) and show that it gives ns rates in Eq. S27; give the formula and inputs for 59 µeV; show the sensitivity of "anomalous scattering dominates recovery" to the assumed equal ratio c^anomalous/c^normal; say whether the omitted optical magnon branch (23 meV) opens other decay channels; and compute the full time-evolved recovery from the QBE, not only Γ(q) = S/f at t = 0, for comparison with the RXD and RDS traces of Fig. 4D.

6. **Long-lived photocarriers are not excluded, either as a ps magnon-scattering channel or as the cause of the persistent demagnetization and its ns recovery.** Magnon–electron scattering is excluded (p. 6), and the ns recovery assigned to magnons, because doublons are assumed to decay within ~200 fs, using refs. 30, 31 on other cuprates; no measurement on CuO is shown. In a 1.4–1.5 eV charge-transfer insulator, carriers can localize as polarons or traps and survive for ps–ns; through the spin–charge coupling of Eq. S5 they could redistribute magnons, keep the order suppressed and set the 7 ns recovery through recombination. The authors themselves invoke defect-related electronic thermal conductivity in this very sample (p. S10). Thermal recovery of the order in a probed layer that stays hot for ≫10 ns is another channel. The authors should measure the photocarrier lifetime on this crystal at the same fluence (transient optical reflectivity/absorption, or tr-XAS at the Cu L3 or O K edge across ps–ns, which the SwissFEL setup can do), show the carriers decay long before the RXD recovers, and compare the RXD level at 10–12 ns with the equilibrium RXD at the estimated lattice temperature.

7. **The tr-NRDS evidence for phonon creation by magnon annihilation (Fig. 4D top, Fig. S10) is weak as a control.** It comes from a different facility under unmatched conditions: ps pump at 1 kHz (with possible average heating) versus 35 fs at 100 Hz, ~100 ps X-ray pulses, pump incidence ~88°, a probe depth ~1.5 µm against ~500 nm pump penetration (versus 18 nm for RXD/RDS), and no stated fluence definition — so absorbed energy density and lattice temperature differ. The kinetics do not obviously fit: phonons created at the ~7 ns magnon decay rate and dissipated over ~0.7 µs should accumulate monotonically over ~10 ns, but NRDS peaks at ~1–2 ns and decays. The ~160 ps feature sits at the 100 ps resolution limit yet is read as the "phonon quasi-thermalization timescale", and the 2 ns enhancement rests on a few delays with large error bars. The PM control is at 300 K, 110 K warmer, where phonon populations, heat capacity, Debye–Waller factors, absorption and thermal expansion differ. NRDS within 0.1 r.l.u. of (0 0 −2) is dominated by low-energy acoustic phonons (∝ n/ω²), whereas the anomalous process emits the lowest optical phonons, so the observable is at best indirect. The ROI (Fig. S10C) was chosen by hand to avoid a Bragg shift visible at 0.15 and 0.6 ns (dipolar patterns in panels 2 and 4), and AFM-specific magnetostriction could broaden (0 0 −2) inhomogeneously and raise ROI intensity without a shift. The authors should add a PM reference just above T_N2 (~235–250 K) and an AF2 reference, estimate steady-state heating at 1 kHz and the deposited energy at each temperature, show radial/azimuthal and radial-versus-transverse profiles and fitted Bragg position versus delay in both phases, demonstrate insensitivity to ROI choice, give fit uncertainties for the 2 ns and 160 ps constants, and fit RXD, RDS and NRDS jointly with a coupled rate-equation model including magnon → phonon transfer, phonon redistribution and an energy-budget check.

8. **The polarization test does not distinguish transverse magnon fluctuations from longitudinal (Bragg-like) magnetic scattering.** The text takes the π/σ ratio of the upturn (Fig. 2G, ~6 at 4 ps) being "similar to the magnetic RXD profile" (Fig. S5B, ~3.1/0.5) as proof of magnon origin. Magnons are fluctuations transverse to the b-axis moments, so Eq. 1 generally predicts a different polarization factor from the ordered moment; as it stands the agreement arguably favours a Bragg-tail origin. Using the geometric tensor F (Eq. S37), the authors should compute the expected σ and π intensities from transverse S^xx and S^zz correlations and the Bragg ratio from S^yy, and compare both with Figs. 2G and S5B.

9. **The energy dependence of the RDS upturn follows the absorption maximum, not the magnetic RXD resonance (Fig. 2E).** The upturn peaks at ~931.7 eV, at the XAS maximum, whereas RXD peaks near 930 eV and dips at 933 eV from self-absorption, although magnon resonant scattering is governed by the same dipole matrix elements as the Bragg intensity. The authors should explain this, for example with a calculated resonance profile for the inelastic channel and self-absorption corrections for each geometry, and rule out a pump-induced change in absorption, fluorescence or self-absorption scaling with the local tail intensity, e.g. by time-resolved XAS in fluorescence yield at the same fluence.

12. **Contamination from the transient incommensurate (ICM) phase is not ruled out quantitatively.** The ICM peak at (0.506, 0, −0.483) differs from the detuned point (0.49, 0.01, −0.51) by only 0.016–0.027 r.l.u. per component, within the measured q range; Fig. S7 shows the ICM signal spread broadly on the detector, growing with time constants up to several hundred ps, and appearing at higher fluence, so the margin between 3 mJ/cm² and the ICM onset matters (especially with the probed layer near T_N1). The statement that no upturn is seen at the ICM position "within our experimental precision" (p. S7) should be backed by the actual trace and its uncertainty at 3 mJ/cm².

13. **Twin and antiferromagnetic domains, and their walls, are not quantified as a source of diffuse intensity.** Fig. S4 shows a dense set of twin stripes, at odds with "a minimal volume of twin domains"; magnetic domains are not characterised. Domain walls and finite domain size give quasi-elastic diffuse intensity near the magnetic peak that the energy-integrating APDs collect and that can change on photoexcitation. The authors should give a twin volume fraction, the X-ray footprint relative to domain size, where twin-domain magnetic reflections fall relative to the detuned positions, the pump-off diffuse intensity at the measured q, and an argument that pump-induced domain-wall dynamics do not contribute.

14. **Momentum-resolved data exist at only one temperature (190 K), one fluence (3 mJ/cm²) and one crystal, so the proposed scattering hierarchy is not tested through its predicted dependences.** Magnon–magnon rates scale with magnon density, so τ_RDS(q) should shorten with fluence; the anomalous magnon–phonon rate depends on the lowest optical phonon occupation, so the ~7 ns recovery should vary with temperature in a calculable way; the Bose factor in Eq. 2 makes the equilibrium RDS baseline temperature dependent. All data come from one floating-zone crystal at a single position. The authors should measure τ_RDS(q) at two or three fluences and tr-RDS plus long-delay RXD at one or two lower temperatures (e.g. 100–150 K, which also moves away from T_N1), compare with QBE predictions for the same conditions, and confirm the key momentum dependence (Fig. 3F) on a second crystal or spot or after thermal cycling, stating the spread.

15. **The claims of generality go beyond the evidence.** The abstract and conclusions state that the framework "transcends phenomenological models and generalizes across materials", "establishes design principles", applies to "any gapped magnetic insulators" (p. 9), and enables "attosecond-scale investigations" (p. 10), and that "strongly overlapping dispersions … exhibit faster recovery". The evidence comes from CuO at one temperature over |q| ≤ 0.08 r.l.u. along two directions, with an assumed magnon–phonon coupling (concern 5), and the dispersion-overlap rule is not tested even within CuO (e.g. by tuning phonon populations with temperature). These statements should be reframed as hypotheses and testable predictions, or supported with a second temperature or material that tests a prediction.
