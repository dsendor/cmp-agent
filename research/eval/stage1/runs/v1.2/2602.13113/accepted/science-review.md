A. Must address

1. **A hot, quasi-equilibrium spin system (critical fluctuations near T_N) is not separated from the claimed non-thermal magnon population, and there is no static calibration of diffuse intensity against magnon population.** All tr-RDS data are taken at 190 K, only ~23 K below T_N1 = 213 K, with the incommensurate phase just above (213-230 K) (Methods, Fig. S5). A rough estimate for 3 mJ/cm² absorbed within a ~450 nm penetration depth gives ~50-70 J/cm³; with a CuO heat capacity of ~3 J/(cm³·K) this is a near-surface rise of ~15-20 K once spins and lattice equilibrate, bringing the 18 nm probed layer to or near T_N1. Thermally populated critical spin fluctuations near T_N produce diffuse scattering around the magnetic Bragg peak that is resonant, π-enhanced and symmetric about q_RXD, rises as the order parameter falls and recovers as the order recovers; critical dynamics can also make relaxation slower at small q. The fluence dependence in Fig. 2f (onset with demagnetization, then a decrease at high fluence that the text itself attributes to approaching T_N) fits this picture too. The paper interprets I_RDS through Eqs. (1)-(2) and converts it to f_μ(q,t), but never shows the equilibrium diffuse intensity around (1/2 0 -1/2) versus temperature in the same geometry. Because "non-thermal magnons" and "quasi-thermalization fulfilling Bose-Einstein statistics" are central claims, the authors should: (i) measure static (pump-off) RDS q-profiles from ~150 K through T_N1 and the incommensurate phase, test that the tail scales with the thermal magnon population as Eq. (2) predicts, and use this to calibrate an effective magnon temperature at each delay; (ii) compare transient profiles (Fig. 3e,f) with equilibrium profiles at matched RXD suppression and show that the transient q-dependence differs from the equilibrium critical lineshape, including whether the state at 4-4.5 ps (Fig. 2f) is simply a hotter magnet; (iii) fit the extracted f_μ(q, t ≥ 2 ps) to a Bose-Einstein form with free temperature and chemical potential and report the result (a magnon-number-conserving process should give a non-zero chemical potential); (iv) move the temperature estimate from Supplementary Note 3 into the main text, giving spin and lattice temperatures of the probed layer at 4.5 ps and 1-10 ns, the lattice temperature at 3 mJ/cm², and whether any part of the excited layer crosses T_N1 into the incommensurate phase; and (v) if possible, repeat at a lower base temperature or fluence where heating stays well below T_N1.

B. Should address

2. **A transient change in absorption or in the resonant scattering factor is not ruled out as a contributor to the "RDS" upturn.** The upturn is strongest at ~931.5-932 eV, close to the XAS maximum and exactly where static RXD shows a self-absorption dip, whereas RXD peaks near ~930.5 eV (Fig. 2e). The authors attribute this to reduced self-absorption from a near-surface magnon density, but photoexcitation of a charge-transfer insulator (doublons/holons, heating) can shift, broaden or bleach the Cu L3 resonance and change the refractive index, which would alter the scattered intensity at fixed photon energy without any change in spin correlations. Away from q = 0, where the multiplicative RXD loss is small in absolute terms, such a change could look like an upturn. The amplitude profile A_RDS(q) (Fig. 3f) follows the Bragg-tail shape closely, as a q-independent multiplicative change would, and the π/σ dependence (Fig. 2g) does not discriminate because the Bragg tail has the same polarization dependence. The authors should show: (I_ON - I_OFF)/I_OFF versus q at fixed delays (e.g. 0.5, 1.5, 4 ps), which is q-dependent for magnon redistribution and q-independent for an absorption/form-factor change; time-resolved XAS (or fluorescence yield) at 3 mJ/cm², or the full energy dependence of RXD and RDS at several delays; a non-resonant, below-edge, or non-magnetic-position control (e.g. a charge Bragg tail at the same energy) in the same geometry; and a model of the expected energy dependence of a purely magnetic upturn including the depth profile, quantifying how much of the upturn could come from self-absorption change or resonance shift.

3. **The separation of the RDS upturn from the RXD tail depends on the fit model, and the small-|q| points that carry the linear 1/τ ∝ |q| law are the least constrained.** A_RDS and τ_RDS (Fig. 3a,c-f) come from two-exponential fits to each trace (Supplementary Note 2). Near q_RXD the tail dominates: the q = 0.010 trace drops to about -5 a.u. while the upturn is ~+0.2 a.u. (Fig. 3a inset); the Bragg top is saturated (Fig. 2c) and the central region is excluded (hatched). τ_RXD is ~50-100 fs, comparable to the 60 fs time resolution (Fig. 3d), so a misestimated fast component leaks into the slow amplitude; and τ_RDS reaches ~3-4 ps at small |q|, comparable to the 4 ps window of Fig. 3a. The points at |q| = 0.010-0.020 r.l.u. are only 2-4 times the ~5×10⁻³ r.l.u. resolution, and the positive/negative-q asymmetry is attributed to "a small error in the definition of the origin", an error that could be half the smallest |q|. The authors should: state whether fits are convolved with the instrument response; show residuals and amplitude-τ covariances for the inner points; test alternative decompositions, e.g. model the RXD contribution from the measured, unsaturated static Bragg lineshape scaled by the RXD time trace; extend the delay range at small |q| to several time constants; treat the q origin as a fit parameter, give its value and uncertainty, and show the linear 1/τ scaling survives; and state whether the line in Fig. 3i is a fit (through the origin or not) and what form the QBE predicts for 1/τ(q).

4. **Transient changes in Bragg-peak position or lineshape are not excluded, and the width analysis relies on saturated data.** The main text says the RXD width "remains nearly constant" (Fig. S4), which is used to exclude correlation-length changes, Kibble-Zurek coarsening and an incommensurate transition (Supplementary Note 4). But the peak top is saturated in the shown profiles (Fig. 2c), and a peak shift (from lattice expansion, strain or a move toward the incommensurate wavevector by a fraction of the resolution) or a lineshape change would produce asymmetric or rising diffuse intensity at detuned positions without changing the width. The authors should state how width and centre were extracted under saturation (attenuated data? Jungfrau detector?), show full transient q-scans through the peak at several delays with position, width and integrated intensity (extending Fig. S4), give upper bounds on transient width and position changes, and show quantitatively that the measured symmetry (Fig. 3f) excludes a shift.

5. **A transient incommensurate (AF2) phase or short-range AF2 correlations are not convincingly excluded in the main text.** A photo-induced collinear-to-spiral transition in CuO has been reported (ref. 23). The AF2 propagation vector (0.506, 0, -0.483) lies within a few hundredths of r.l.u. of the commensurate peak, the same scale as the Fig. 3 q range; the RDS detector at (0.49, 0.01, -0.51) and the Fig. 4d position (0.47, 0, -0.53) both deviate in h and l. Transient incommensurate satellites or short-range AF2 correlations would give a growing, resonant, π-enhanced diffuse signal consistent with Figs. 2e-g without magnon redistribution. The exclusion is deferred to Supplementary Note 4; the main text should explain why the measured q points and their time dependence rule out AF2 satellites, and show the RDS response at a lower fluence or base temperature where the estimated temperature stays well below T_N1 (see concern 1).

8. **Heat diffusion and lattice cooling are not excluded as causes of the ~7 ns recovery.** The paper attributes the ~7 ns RXD/RDS recovery (Fig. 4d) to an intrinsically inefficient anomalous magnon-phonon process, the basis of the "dispersion mismatch creates bottlenecks" claim. If magnons and lattice are near local equilibrium by ~1 ns, heat leaving the excited layer into the cold bulk (time ~d²/D, a few ns for d ~45 nm and D ~10⁻⁶ m²/s) would restore the RXD on a similar timescale regardless of the magnon-phonon coupling; the RXD also recovers only partially by 12 ns, and the residual could reflect lattice temperature and heat redistribution. The reviews disagree on the 400 nm pump penetration depth (~45 nm versus ~450-500 nm), which changes this estimate by about two orders of magnitude, so the authors should state it explicitly. Supplementary Note 11 treats the microsecond phonon timescale; the nanosecond component needs the same treatment. The authors should estimate the lattice-temperature evolution in the 18 nm probed layer from 0.1 to 20 ns using CuO thermal conductivity at 190 K and the actual excitation profile, show that diffusion would be clearly faster or slower than 7 ns (or model depth-resolved recovery with both diffusion and the QBE magnon-phonon channel and refit the coupling), and ideally test the fluence or probe-depth dependence (X-ray incidence angle or energy across the edge), since diffusion and an intrinsic bottleneck scale differently.

9. **The magnon-phonon coupling is derived from the recovery time, so the model's agreement with that timescale is not an independent test of the anomalous-process mechanism.** The effective coupling of ~59 µeV is "estimated from the magnon relaxation timescale" (p. 7), making the ~7 ns recovery an input rather than a prediction. Since this coupling is a headline number, the main Methods should also give the magnon-phonon QBE ingredients (phonon branches, coupling vertex) rather than only referring to Supplementary Note 10.

10. **The tr-NRDS evidence for phonon creation comes from very different conditions, and its timescale does not match the RXD/RDS recovery.** The claim rests on matching the tr-NRDS enhancement at (0 0 -2) (ESRF) with the RXD/RDS recovery (SwissFEL) in Fig. 4d. tr-NRDS probes ~1.5 µm, about three times the pump penetration (~450-500 nm at 88° grazing incidence), so most of its signal comes from weakly excited or unexcited material, while tr-RDS probes ~18 nm; the pump pulses are ps versus 35 fs long; repetition rates differ (1 kHz versus 100 Hz), so cumulative heating may differ; and a nominal "3 mJ cm⁻²" may correspond to very different absorbed energy densities. The NRDS enhancement appears "around 2 ns", whereas the RXD/RDS recovery constant is ~7 ns, and the NRDS data are sparse with large error bars. The PM reference temperature is not stated (the cryostream range is 190-300 K); at a much higher temperature the phonon population, heat capacity and diffuse background differ. The authors should report absorbed energy densities and steady-state heating at 1 kHz for both setups, give a depth-weighted estimate of the excited fraction seen by NRDS, state the fitted NRDS rise and decay constants with uncertainties and explain why 2 ns and 7 ns count as matching, give the exact AFM and PM temperatures (Fig. 4d, Fig. S11), ideally use a PM reference just above T_N2 (~235-240 K), and explain why the AFM/PM difference is not a temperature-dependent background effect.

13. **The magnon and phonon dispersions underlying the optical-magnon exclusion and the bottleneck argument are not shown.** The model uses a simplified orthorhombic cell with one Cu per layer (J_AFM = 120 meV, J_FM = -15 meV, J_out = -18 meV, D = 0.025 meV), said to reproduce the neutron dispersion (Fig. S1c). It is not shown that this cell has both the acoustic (~1.3 meV) and optical (~23.2 meV) magnon branches used to rule out optical-magnon contributions, nor how the 1.3 meV acoustic gap arises from D = 0.025 meV. The phase-space argument (Fig. 4a-c,e,f) depends on where acoustic magnon and lowest optical phonon dispersions cross; magnons quasi-thermalized near the ~1.3 meV gap would need phonons of only a few meV, yet the text names the "lowest-energy optical phonons" as partners in the two-magnon-to-one-phonon process (Fig. 4e).
