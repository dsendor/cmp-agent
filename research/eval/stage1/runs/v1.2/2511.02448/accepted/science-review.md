A. Must address

1. The PNO dxy orbiton dispersion (Fig. 5a,b) has not been shown to be intrinsic rather than a fitting or cross-section artifact. This matters because the "opposite dispersion" relative to CCO is the paper's main new result and the starting point for the orbital-superexchange analysis. The claimed dispersion is a centroid shift of about 62–90 meV in a peak whose width is about 0.4 eV (the FWHM used in the single-ion model, SM G), roughly 15–20% of the resolution-convolved linewidth. The peak sits between the 0.7 eV "RE" feature and the broad dxz/yz plus d3z2 tail (Fig. 2c), and Supplementary Fig. 8 shows that the fitted parameters of these overlapping components are strongly cross-correlated. Several things change along the cut and could each move the fitted centroid by tens of meV without real propagation: the 0.7 eV feature, which by the authors' account (p. 3, Fig. 1b) grows toward the zone edge; the incidence angle at fixed 2θ = 149.5°, which changes the polarization projections and hence the relative weight of dxz/yz and other components (Fig. 4); self-absorption, which differs between grazing incidence and grazing emission; L itself; and the relative weight of the spin-flip and non-spin-flip parts of the dd excitation, which differ in energy by an amount of order the exchange (tens of meV for J1 ≈ 46 meV). Fig. 4 and p. 6 already show that the polarization character of the 1.29 eV peak differs between ±H and from theory. The quoted error bars are fit confidence intervals that ignore the correlations among overlapping peak parameters, and the fit has three free hoppings plus an offset (t1 = 12.5 ± 3.8, t2 = 0.2 ± 4.8, t3 = 5.4 ± 1.7 meV) for a 60–90 meV dispersion. Fig. 5b, with vertical lines fixed at the minimum, is not enough to judge such a shift on a 0.4 eV-wide peak. The authors should: (i) compare the dxy position at +H and −H at the same |Q| (grazing incidence against grazing emission), for example with the spectra already taken at H = ±0.4 (Fig. 4a,b), since an intrinsic dispersion is even in Q and a cross-section artifact generally is not; (ii) show that the trend survives changes to the fit model — two versus three dd peaks (Fig. 7), RE peak free, fixed or removed, dxz/yz and d3z2 widths fixed or free, widths fixed across Q; (iii) report a model-independent first moment over a fixed window around 1.3 eV; (iv) simulate, with the single-ion cross sections of Fig. 4 including the sf/nsf energy offset, the apparent centroid shift that geometry alone produces along the measured path; (v) give the fitted dxy width and amplitude against Q next to Fig. 5a, show the dxy energy from σ-polarized maps if they exist, and if possible repeat one cut at a different scattering angle. The same tests on CCO would show whether the sign reversal between the two materials is robust. If only one sign of dispersion survives every test, the claim stands; otherwise it should be presented as tentative.

2. The claim of three-dimensional antiferromagnetic correlations in PNO "similar to" CCO, with comparable out-of-plane exchange (abstract; p. 5; SM F; Fig. 13; Conclusions), rests on a parameter that was not fitted. J⊥ = 5.9 meV is held fixed in the Fig. 3a fit. Fig. 13 shows that it is constrained only by the few points within 0.05–0.125 r.l.u. of the specular condition, where the magnon merges with the strong elastic line (SM D, Fig. 9), the phonon was removed by hand, and the error bars span roughly ±30–50 meV (about 75 meV near Q = 0.05). Even so, the abstract, main text ("J⊥/J1 of 8.5% for CCO and 13% for PNO", "comparable value of the spin excitation energy at in-plane zone center") and Conclusions ("clearly confers a certain degree of tridimensionality") treat J⊥ as measured. Other causes of an apparent gap at Γ are not excluded: an XY/easy-plane or easy-axis anisotropy gap (the authors cite Ref. 65 for this), a finite in-plane correlation length in a film without established long-range order (a spin-glass-like state is cited in the Introduction), and the bias from fitting a ~100 meV-wide damped mode with a single DHO next to a strong elastic tail. Because 2θ is fixed, L changes along every cut, yet the L value of each spectrum is not given, it is not stated whether the LSW model was evaluated at the actual (H, K, L) of each point, and no L dependence was measured at fixed in-plane Q. The authors should: tabulate or plot L for each Q (this also bears on the dd intensities); state whether the LSW fit uses the actual L; fit J⊥ freely, or with an anisotropy gap as an alternative, and report its confidence interval, its correlations with J1–J4 and a χ² comparison; and ideally measure the near-Γ magnon energy at two or more L for the same in-plane Q (for example H = K = 0.05–0.1 at different incidence angles or scattering angle), which directly tests J⊥ against anisotropy. Otherwise the three-dimensionality statement should be reduced to "consistent with J⊥ in the range 0–10 meV", and J⊥/J1 quoted as a range (0–22%) wherever it is used. The statement that the results "give a proof of the fully occurred topotactic reduction of the PNO samples and the absence of interstitial oxygen atoms" (p. 5) does not follow from a fixed J⊥ and should be removed or supported by structural data.

3. The magnon damping comparison (Fig. 3b; p. 4; abstract and Conclusions) does not support the claim that self-doping has "a much milder impact on spin order than chemical doping" and offers "a non-disruptive way to achieve superconductivity". Fig. 3b compares absolute damping: about 80–100 meV for PNO against about 200–500 meV for Bi2201. But the PNO magnon bandwidth is roughly half that of the cuprates (J1 = 46 against 82 meV); relative to the mode energy, PNO damping (about 90 meV at 150–190 meV, γ/ω ≈ 0.5–0.6) is close to that of underdoped Bi2201 at comparable momenta, so on that measure PNO looks like an underdoped cuprate rather than a nearly undamped system. Several other contributions to the PNO width are not separated from intrinsic damping: disorder and inhomogeneous or partial reduction in a 5 nm topotactically reduced, capped film; residual apical oxygen and the capping interface; the impurity phases the authors themselves invoke for the 0.7 eV peak; L integration; the unresolved phonon and multi-magnon/bimagnon content, which was left out of the PNO fit (SM B) but included for CCO; and the single-peak DHO fit near X, which the authors admit overestimates damping in CCO. The Bi2201 values (Ref. 54) come from a different sample family, experiment and fitting chain. Finally, the self-doping level of the measured film is not determined, so "self-doping" and "chemical doping" cannot be put on a common axis. The authors should: plot damping normalized by magnon energy or by J1 for all samples; fit PNO with a model that includes the phonon and a multi-magnon component and show how much width remains; fit PNO and Bi2201 with the same DHO definition and resolution treatment, or confirm that Ref. 54 used them; estimate the doped-hole concentration of the RIXS sample (for example from the Ni L3 XAS lineshape, the O K pre-edge, or Hall data in Ref. 42); discuss extrinsic sources of damping; and ideally compare with a non-superconducting or differently reduced PNO film. Without this, the claim should be restated in qualitative terms.

B. Should address

4. The quantitative agreement between orbiton theory and experiment (Table III: t1^orb 13.7 against 12.5 ± 3.8 meV) depends on chosen parameters, and the theory section has internal inconsistencies. (a) Sensitivity: t1^orb = (jHS − jLS)/2 · J̄1^orb with jHS = 1/(1 − 3J_H/U). With J_H = 1.2 eV and U = 6.5 eV, 3J_H/U = 0.55, close to a divergence; taking J_H = 1.0 eV (the CCO value) at the same U lowers (jHS − jLS)/2 from about 0.50 to about 0.34, a roughly one-third change before J̄1 changes at all. The CCO result (6.7 meV) uses J_H = 1.0 eV and U = 8 eV, so the contrast between the materials depends largely on these choices. J_H is argued from the atomic value via a nephelauxetic argument, the hoppings are set by hand to 80% of CCO "to account for the larger d-p splitting", and Δ = 4.4 eV comes from DFT; none is independently determined. (b) Source of U: the Methods say U was taken from La4Ni3O8 (Ref. 29), while SM H says "our estimate of the Mott energy U value is exclusively based on the orbital dispersion". If U was tuned to the dispersion, the Table III agreement is partly circular. The Methods also say both the hoppings and Δ are "taken from" Ref. 6 while being scaled by hand. (c) Arithmetic: SM I gives J̄1^orb = U·J1^orb/(U − 2J_H) ≈ 27 meV; with J1^orb = 21.6 meV, U = 6.5 eV and J_H = 1.2 eV that formula gives about 34 meV, while 27 meV corresponds to U/(U − J_H). Either the formula or the number is wrong, and t1^orb = 13.7 meV uses 27 meV. (d) Spin-exchange check: the same three-band parameters should reproduce the measured spin J1 in both compounds, and this is not shown. A crude estimate J ∝ t_pd⁴/Δ³ with the Table II values (0.8⁴ × (1.8/4.4)³) gives J_PNO/J_CCO ≈ 0.03, far below the measured ≈ 0.56 (46/82 meV), suggesting the parameter set may not describe PNO's superexchange. (e) Approximations: the free-orbiton treatment drops the orbiton–magnon coupling terms (SM Eqs. 15–19), whose prefactors ((jHS + jLS)/2 ≈ 1.7 and jHS ≈ 2.2) exceed that of the kept hopping (≈ 0.5), yet the main text (p. 8) argues that string-type magnon dressing strongly suppresses NN orbiton motion; the argument thus relies on the neglected terms. The model also assumes a long-range antiferromagnetic, ferro-orbital ground state although PNO is a self-doped metal without reported long-range order, and the fitted t3 = 5.4 meV (about half of t1) lies outside the model. The authors should state unambiguously how U was obtained; fix the J̄1 formula or number; compute the spin J1 for both compounds from the Table II parameters and compare with Fig. 3a; show t1^orb, J1^orb and J2^orb as functions of U, J_H, Δ and the hopping scaling over plausible ranges for both PNO and CCO, and whether the sign and NN dominance survive; estimate the renormalization of the coherent orbiton band by the neglected terms (for example with the self-consistent Born or linear-string treatment of Ref. 45), state the expected quasiparticle weight and relate the 0.4 eV dxy width to the incoherent part; explain why neglecting t3 does not undermine the comparison; and present the agreement as consistency rather than validation.

5. The CCO and PNO orbiton dispersions are fitted with different models, and the fitted parameters do not match the quoted bandwidths. CCO is fitted with a pure NNN term (Eq. 2, with no constant offset written), PNO with NN, NNN and third-neighbour terms (Eq. 1), so the "opposite dispersion" and "NN against NNN" contrast partly reflects the model choice; SM I itself predicts a finite NN hopping t1 = 6.7 meV for CCO. For PNO, Eq. 1 with the quoted t1, t2, t3 gives about 50 meV along Γ–(0.475,0) and about 100 meV toward (0.34,0.34), the reverse of the 90 and 62 meV quoted in Fig. 5a. The authors should fit both data sets with the same Eq. 1 form (t1 and t2 free for CCO, testing compatibility with 6.7 meV) and report parameters with covariances, check that the plotted curves correspond to the quoted parameters, and reconcile the bandwidths with the arrows in Fig. 5.

6. The measured PNO film is not characterized in the paper, although several conclusions depend on its state. Superconductivity in nominally undoped PrNiO2 (onset about 11 K, zero resistance below 4 K) is itself unusual and is the basis of the paper's framing, yet it is supported only by Ref. 42 ("the resulting samples show a complete superconducting behavior"); no ρ(T), XRD or XAS of the RIXS sample is shown, and it is not stated whether the same film was used for transport and RIXS. The broad resistive transition suggests inhomogeneity. No Ni L3 or O K XAS is shown, so a residual Ni²⁺ (incompletely reduced) fraction is not excluded; such a fraction would add dd weight near 1–2 eV and could produce the mixed polarization of the 1.29 eV peak and the unpolarized continuum (Fig. 4). The 0.7 eV peak is said to be stronger in samples with impurity-phase diffraction, yet no diffraction of this sample is shown, and the self-doping level is not estimated. The authors should show ρ(T) (including after the beamtime), XRD (c-axis parameter, absence of secondary phases) and Ni L3/O K XAS for the measured film, estimate its self-doped hole density, and briefly discuss inhomogeneity.

7. The "coexistence of superconductivity and sharp spin excitations" (pp. 2–4, abstract, Conclusions) was not measured. 8. The four-neighbour linear spin-wave fit for PNO (Fig. 3a) is under-constrained, and its comparison with the literature is not quantitative. Five couplings (J1–J4 plus J⊥) are fitted to a single dispersion along M–Γ–X–M that stops at H = 0.475, has about 45 meV of zone-boundary dispersion, large error bars near Γ, and a single-peak fit near X that the authors call approximate. J2–J4 are likely strongly correlated with each other and with J1, yet covariances are not reported, and the quoted uncertainties (J1 = 45.8 ± 0.3 meV, J4 = 8.0 ± 0.4 meV) look implausibly small for a ~100 meV-wide magnon. Along [H,H] the data peak near (0.2,0.2), not (0.25,0.25), and lie about 20 meV below the fit near (0.25,0.25) (Fig. 3a, Fig. 13); no J1–J4 Heisenberg model with this sign structure reproduces that maximum, and the authors link it to a possible incommensurate reflection or elastic artifact (p. 6). It is not stated whether the S = 1/2 quantum renormalization factor (Zc ≈ 1.18) is included, which affects J1 directly, and the sign convention for J2 (negative) is not defined. "In agreement with the literature [12, 24, 51]" needs numbers: Ref. 12 reported J1 ≈ 64 meV for NdNiO2 and Ref. 47 reports values for PrNiO2 films, mostly from J1–J2 models, so parameters from different Hamiltonians are being compared. The PNO/CCO comparison of J1 and J⊥/J1 depends on these choices. The authors should report the parameter covariance matrix and how the 95% intervals were obtained; show fits with fewer couplings (J1–J2, J1–J2–J3, or t/U with ring exchange) for both compounds with model-selection statistics; show how robust J1 is when [H,H] points beyond about 0.2 are excluded; state Zc and the J2 sign convention; tabulate literature J values with the model used for each; and express the PNO/CCO comparison also in model-independent quantities such as E(0.5,0), E(0.25,0.25) and their difference.

9. The interpretation of the mixed polarization character of the 1.29 eV dxy peak, and of the enhanced dxy intensity under σ polarization, as Pr 5d (or interstitial s) hybridization (Fig. 4, Fig. 12, p. 6) is one of several possibilities, and the polarimetric analysis lacks procedure and quantitative comparison. Alternatives not addressed include overlap of the broad dxz/yz tail and the 0.7 eV peak (both partly non-crossed), the unpolarized continuum the authors attribute to self-doping, a residual Ni²⁺ fraction (see concern 6), and the polarimeter efficiency correction and its uncertainty. The 0.7 eV feature that underpins the hybridization picture is itself of uncertain origin; the text acknowledges a possible extrinsic origin linked to impurity phases (p. 3), and no incident-energy dependence is shown. The extraction of the crossed and non-crossed components (polarimeter efficiency, multilayer reflectivity ratio, normalization) is not described, and the PNO components are noisy enough to need 15-point smoothing (Fig. 12). The comparison with Fig. 4(g–l) is by eye only, and the single-ion calculations use the experimental peak energies of Table I as input and per-peak widths chosen by hand to "reproduce the remaining peaks" (for example a 1.2 eV FWHM for dz² in PNO, and separate sf/nsf widths for dxz and dyz in CCO), so "well reproduced within a single-ion model" tests only relative polarization weights. The in-plane 45° spin direction is assumed for PNO, which has no established long-range order, and this choice affects the sf/nsf weights. The authors should describe the polarimeter calibration; fit the crossed and non-crossed spectra simultaneously with the same component set and report the crossed fraction of each peak (especially dxy) with uncertainties propagated from the calibration, against the expected fraction under each scenario; state which Fig. 4 comparisons are predictions and which depend on tuned widths; show how the PNO predictions change for other spin orientations or a spin-averaged paramagnetic case; provide RIXS at a few incident energies across the Ni L3 edge to distinguish a Ni-centred excitation from a feature with different resonance behaviour; and otherwise present the hybridization interpretation as one of several possibilities.

12. The charge-order test (SM A, Fig. 6) is limited, and it is used to support full topotactic reduction. It integrates the quasi-elastic signal (±0.1 eV) along [H,0] with π polarization only, at whatever (unspecified) L the fixed 2θ gives, in about 0.025 r.l.u. steps, at a single incident energy (852.48 eV, not necessarily where the reported order resonates), with the elastic tail rising at both ends. Charge-order peaks in infinite-layer nickelates have a specific L dependence and resonance, and the authors' own Ref. 50 reports that STO-capped films do not show the (1/3,0) order, so a null result says little about reduction. The authors should give the L reached near H = 1/3, state whether the incident energy matches the reported charge-order resonance, state the expected peak width and detection limit relative to the intensities in Refs. 49 and 57, check whether the rise toward 0.475 tracks the off-resonance or σ/π response expected for a structural or substrate reflection, and not use this null result as evidence of complete topotactic reduction.
