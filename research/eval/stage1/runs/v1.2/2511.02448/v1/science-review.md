A. Must address

1. **The dispersion law used to extract J^orb (Eq. 1) is the next-nearest-neighbour form, so it cannot support the nearest-neighbour conclusion for PrNiO2, and the fitted J^orb does not reproduce the bandwidths marked in Fig. 5.** Eq. (1), ε_k = 2J^orb cos(k_x)cos(k_y), is taken from Ref. 43, where it describes diagonal (NNN) orbiton hopping in CaCuO2 (cos(k_x+k_y) + cos(k_x−k_y) = 2cos k_x cos k_y). The same function is fitted to PrNiO2 to obtain J^orb = +14 ± 4 meV, and this fitted coefficient is then identified with the calculated NN coupling J^orb_NN = 14.5 meV (Table III; pp. 6–7). NN hopping on a square lattice gives a dispersion ∝ (cos k_x + cos k_y), with its extremum at M opposite to Γ, whereas the product form gives equal energies at Γ and M. Flipping the sign of the coefficient of cos k_x cos k_y does not turn an NNN process into an NN one: the two cases differ in the shape of the dispersion along [H,H] relative to [H,0], not in sign. The two forms predict clearly different bandwidth ratios along the measured paths: the NN form gives equal changes of 4J from Γ to (0.5,0) and from Γ to (0.25,0.25), with the energy still changing towards (0.34,0.34); the NNN form gives 4J along [H,0] but only about 1.4J from Γ to (0.34,0.34). The quoted numbers also do not match the fit: Eq. (1) with J^orb = 14 meV gives about 56 meV from Γ to (0.475,0) and about 20 meV from Γ to (0.34,0.34), whereas Fig. 5(a) marks 90 meV and 62 meV (the [H,H] branch off by a factor of about 3); for CaCuO2, J^orb = −11 meV gives 44 and 22 meV against 56 and 34 meV marked in Fig. 5(c). Eq. (1) also has no constant on-site term, although the data sit at about 1.3 and 1.65 eV, and the Fig. 5(a) caption calls the continuous line a "charge transfer model fitting" while the text says it is the Eq. (1) fit. The central claim of NN propagation in the nickelate against NNN in the cuprate, and the agreement with theory in Table III, are not meaningful until this is resolved. The authors should write the full dispersion, ε0 + 2J_NN(cos k_x + cos k_y) + 4J_NNN cos k_x cos k_y; fit the [H,0] and [H,H] branches of both compounds jointly with the NN, NNN and combined forms, with J_NN and J_NNN free; report parameters, uncertainties, their correlation and χ² for each; overlay all fitted curves on the data along both cuts; state which form is used for which compound and which line Fig. 5 shows; and restate the quoted bandwidths (62/90 meV and 34/56 meV) as differences between fitted points or of the fitted curve, with uncertainties, related explicitly to 4J^orb.

2. **The PrNiO2 d_xy dispersion (60–90 meV on a peak about 0.5 eV wide) has not been shown to be robust against fitting and geometry artefacts.** In PrNiO2 the d_xy feature (Fig. 2(c)) is a Gaussian several hundred meV wide that overlaps the 0.7 eV "RE peak", the d_xz/yz peak (at 1.93 eV, about 0.6 eV higher), the d_z² component and an unpolarized continuum or broad tail; its fitted position is sensitive to how these are constrained. At fixed 2θ = 149.5° the incidence angle changes continuously along each cut (grazing incidence on one side, grazing emission on the other), and so do L, self-absorption through the STO cap and film, and the polarization projection. Fig. 4 and the text on p. 6 show that the relative weights of d_xy, d_xz/yz and the continuum, and the polarization character of the 1.29 eV peak itself, change strongly with H and with geometry. With free widths and intensities, such momentum-dependent changes in the decomposition can shift a fitted centroid by tens of meV without any intrinsic dispersion. It is also notable that the maximum of the d_xy energy in Fig. 5(a) coincides with the specular point, where the elastic and low-energy intensity is largest (Fig. 1(e)). In CaCuO2 the d_xy peak is narrow and well separated (Figs. 2(d), 5(d)), so the issue is much less severe there, and the apparent sign contrast could therefore arise for instrumental rather than physical reasons. Fig. 5(b) shows the stack only with fixed vertical guide lines, whose meaning is not stated, and no shift is visible by eye. The authors should: state which fit parameters (widths, positions and amplitudes of neighbouring peaks, the RE-peak position and weight, continuum shape) were free, fixed or shared across Q; show that the dispersion survives alternative constraints (widths fixed versus free, the 0.7 eV peak included versus excluded, constrained versus free d_xz/yz positions, a different or free continuum model, and a model-independent first moment over a fixed window); compare the ±H branches, which are equivalent by symmetry but measured in different geometries, and show the σ-polarized cuts as an independent check; give the d_xy linewidth and its Q dependence; estimate, from the single-ion cross-sections, how large an apparent shift polarization and geometry factors produce for overlapping peaks of these widths; discuss geometry-dependent self-absorption at the Ni L3 edge; and overlay raw, normalized spectra at Γ and at the zone boundary so the shift can be seen without a fit.

3. **J⊥ and the claim of "three-dimensional antiferromagnetic order" rest on the magnon energy near Γ measured at an uncontrolled, varying L, and alternative explanations are not excluded.** The abstract and p. 4 conclude that both materials "support a three-dimensional antiferromagnetic order", and J⊥ = 5.9 ± 1.1 meV for PrNiO2 (Fig. 3(a)) is justified only by the 60–80 meV magnon energy near the in-plane zone centre "at non-integer L". All data were taken at fixed 2θ = 149.5°, so L changes along every in-plane cut (with c = 3.28 Å at 852.4 eV, from roughly 0.44 r.l.u. at Γ to roughly 0.18 r.l.u. at H = 0.475, similarly for CaCuO2), and the Methods state that "this is not an issue, since the in-plane Js are still dominating". That contradicts using these same data to extract J⊥, which is constrained essentially by a handful of points near Γ. It is not stated whether SpinW used the actual (H, K, L) of each point. Near Γ the magnon energies are comparable to the phonon (placed at about 80 meV in the text but fitted near 55–60 meV in Fig. 2(a)), to the 40 meV resolution, and to the tail of a very strong specular elastic line (Figs. 1(e), 2(a)), within about 1.5–2 resolution widths of it, and the Γ-point error bars in Fig. 3(a) are the largest of the dataset. A finite energy at Γ could also come from a single-ion or exchange anisotropy (easy-plane/XY) gap, which Ref. 59 raises and the text acknowledges is hard to separate before setting it aside; from an artefact of the elastic/phonon subtraction; from finite film thickness (15–20 unit cells); from the paramagnon character of a system without long-range order; or from Pr 4f–Ni coupling. None of these is ruled out. Moreover, the LSW model presupposes a specific 3D Néel order (stacking vector) that is not stated, no direct evidence of order is shown (no magnetic Bragg peak, no temperature dependence), and the introduction itself notes that infinite-layer nickelates show no long-range order and show spin-glass behaviour (refs. 13, 19, 20); a dispersion consistent with LSW does not establish long-range 3D order. The authors should report the L value of every spectrum in Fig. 3(a) and include it in the SpinW fit; show the Γ-point spectra, their decomposition and the sensitivity of J⊥ to those fits; compare fits with J⊥ = 0 plus an anisotropy gap against fits with J⊥ ≠ 0 and no anisotropy, with goodness of fit; ideally measure the magnon at fixed in-plane momentum near Γ (and at (0.5,0)) while varying L, by changing 2θ or the incident geometry, to show the energy follows the LSW J⊥ form rather than an L-independent gap; and, short of direct evidence of order, change "support a three-dimensional antiferromagnetic order" to "three-dimensional spin correlations" or "interlayer spin correlations" in the abstract and text.

4. **The NN/NNN interpretation depends on a magnetic string effect that is asserted, not computed, and that contradicts the paper's own magnetic conclusions.** For CaCuO2 the charge-transfer model in Table III gives a positive J^orb_NN = 22 meV that exceeds |J^orb_NNN| = 15 meV, yet the experiment is attributed to NNN propagation by invoking suppression of NN hopping by magnon dressing (the string effect), which is outside the model (no magnon coupling is included) and is not calculated. For PrNiO2 the same suppression is argued to be "absent or much weaker" because "the nickelate … does not present evidence of long-range AFM order" (p. 6). But the spin section reports a sharp, weakly damped magnon with J1 ≈ 46 meV, a sizeable fraction of the CaCuO2 value, and argues in the abstract for three-dimensional AFM order. The two statements cannot both support the conclusions. The string effect depends on local antiferromagnetic correlations over a few lattice spacings, on the timescale of orbiton hopping, not on static long-range order, and the authors' own data show strong short-range correlations. As it stands, the model favours NN in both compounds and the contrast rests on an untested assumption. The authors should give a quantitative estimate of the suppression of NN hopping by spin–orbital coupling for each compound, based on the measured J1 and the spin correlation length (for example the ratio J^orb_NN/J1 that controls the string effect in the Wohlfeld et al. framework, or orbiton–magnon coupling relative to J1), showing why it is effective in CaCuO2 but not in PrNiO2; otherwise present the NN/NNN interpretation as a hypothesis and make the abstract consistent with that.

5. **The magnon damping comparison with Bi2201 does not isolate self-doping and does not support "self-doping has a much milder impact on spin order than chemical doping".** On p. 4 the ~100 meV damping in PrNiO2, smaller than the ≳ 200 meV in UD15K Bi2201 (Fig. 3(b)), is said to demonstrate that self-doping is milder and "non-disruptive". The effective hole count from self-doping in this film is never estimated, so the two are compared at unknown relative doping. The comparison uses absolute damping between a system with J1 ≈ 46 meV and Bi2201 with J of order 150 meV: in units of the magnon energy or J1, PrNiO2's damping (about 100 meV against magnon energies of 100–200 meV) is not obviously smaller than underdoped Bi2201's (about 250 meV against roughly 300 meV). The Bi2201 values come from a different study (Ref. 49), with a different resolution and possibly a different line-shape and background model. The PrNiO2 damping itself depends on the fit model: no continuum, phonon overtones or multimagnon contribution is included for PrNiO2 (Fig. 2(a)), near Γ the magnon overlaps the phonon, and the paper itself notes that a single-peak fit overestimates damping near (0.5,0) in CaCuO2 because of the multimagnon continuum, so the flat ~100 meV may partly reflect the fit model. In a 5 nm topotactically reduced film, inhomogeneous broadening from partial reduction, residual or excess oxygen, stacking faults, c-axis defects or strain relaxation could also contribute and is not separated from intrinsic damping. The authors should normalize the damping (γ/ω(q) or γ/J1); refit the Bi2201 raw data (or a representative subset) with the same procedure and resolution convolution, or demonstrate equivalence; show how sensitive the PrNiO2 damping is to adding a continuum; estimate the self-doped hole density of the measured film (Ni L3 or O K pre-edge XAS, or Hall data) and place it on the Bi2201 damping-versus-doping plot; compare with Sr-doped infinite-layer nickelates (Ref. 12 and the authors' Ref. 46), NdNiO2/LaNiO2 literature or a differently reduced reference film; or otherwise rephrase the conclusion as a hypothesis.

6. **The charge-transfer model parameters for PrNiO2 are partly tuned, so the agreement of J^orb_NN = 14.5 meV with the measured 14 ± 4 meV is not independent support.** In Table II the PrNiO2 hoppings are set to 80% of the CaCuO2 values, and Δ = 8.00 eV was "chosen sufficiently larger than U also providing reasonable results" (p. 7), which is effectively tuning against the outcome; literature estimates of Δ for infinite-layer nickelates are commonly a few eV, substantially below 8 eV. U = 3.2 eV for Ni (from Refs. 5 and 25) is far below the 8 eV used for Cu and the 5–7 eV usually assumed for nickelates, and those references give effective one-band U values, not the on-site multiplet Hubbard U a multiband charge-transfer model requires. The authors should list all parameters with their sources and justify the PrNiO2 set against literature estimates; show J^orb_NN and J^orb_NNN as functions of Δ (e.g. 3–10 eV), U (e.g. 3–7 eV), t_pd and the hopping scaling, and state whether the sign of the NN–NNN competition survives across that range; check that the on-site parameters (ε_xy = 0.70 eV, ε_πσ = −1.00 eV) are consistent with the measured dd energies in Table I; and give the model equations and the definition of the orbital superexchange in the Supplement rather than only by reference to Ref. 43.

7. **The LSW exchange constants are weakly constrained, their uncertainties are not credible as stated, and they are not compared quantitatively with the literature.** Five parameters (J1–J4, J⊥) are fitted to a single dispersion per compound along one path, which for PrNiO2 stops at H = 0.475 and does not reach X, yet Fig. 3(a) quotes J1 = 45.8 ± 0.3 meV and similarly tight values for the others, despite large data error bars. J1, J2 and J3 are expected to be strongly correlated when fitted only along M–Γ–X–M, and systematic errors (L variation, elastic/phonon subtraction, the quantum renormalization factor Z_c, which the paper does not say whether it applied) are not included. The PrNiO2 [H,H] maximum lies at (0.2,0.2) rather than (0.25,0.25), with elastic intensity rising beyond the zone boundary (p. 5, Fig. 1(e)); it is not clear whether the fit was forced through it, and if this reflects stripe-like or incommensurate order, a Néel-based J1–J4 model may absorb that physics into J2–J4 and bias them. "In agreement with the literature [12, 24, 46]" needs numbers: Ref. 12 reported about 64 meV for NdNiO2 with a 2D model and a different parameter set, and the difference from 46 meV should be stated and explained (rare-earth or strain dependence, inclusion of J⊥, different longer-range terms). 8. **The concluding statements go well beyond the data.** The summary (pp. 7–8) asserts that the lower Tc in nickelates "is mainly due to the overall smaller energy of spin fluctuations"; that the Mott–Hubbard character "might imply … smaller electron-phonon interaction and the lack of charge density waves"; that IL nickelates "are indeed mimicking the essential physics of cuprates, including the mechanisms for superconducting pairing"; and that they "are indeed unconventional superconductors very similar to cuprates". None of these is tested by the measurements, which concern undoped, normal-state (20 K, above Tc ≈ 4 K and the ~11 K onset) one-magnon and dd excitations, with no superconducting-state or doping-dependent data. The "lack of charge density waves" also contradicts reports of charge order in infinite-layer nickelates, including the authors' own Ref. 45. These statements should be removed or clearly labelled as speculation or open questions with appropriate citations, and the abstract checked for the same issue.
