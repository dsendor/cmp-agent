A. Must address

1. The polarization dependence cannot distinguish the proposed magnetic-dipole emission from non-magnetic second-order emission, and no measurement does so. In point group -3 an in-plane axial vector (M_x, M_y) and the in-plane response to E⊗E both belong to E_g (Table S1). At the (0001) surface the symmetry drops to point group 3, where an in-plane polar vector (a surface optical-rectification polarization or shift/rectification current, J_x ∝ a(E_x² − E_y²) + b(2E_xE_y)) belongs to E. Because point group 3 has no mirrors, this gives exactly the same normal-incidence form |cos(2φ − 2δ)| with a non-universal δ (Eq. 3 and Eq. IV.64 have the same form). Bulk electric-quadrupole or magnetic-dipole optical rectification, photon drag (proportional to the photon wavevector), anisotropic photocurrents, and electric-quadrupole radiation from the very intra-cell quadrupoles invoked in SI Fig. S6b produce the same angular pattern. The fourfold pattern with non-universal δ in Figs. 2d,e is therefore expected for any of these mechanisms and is not specific to M_y. The two arguments on p. 2 for excluding a surface contribution are not decisive. (i) That surface emission "would not track magnetic order" is undercut by the paper's own data: the fourfold pattern is fully present at 200 K, about three times Tc, with an amplitude about 85% of the maximum and comparable to or larger than at 7 K (Figs. 2e,f). Surface or bulk rectification can also depend on temperature through band-gap, absorption and carrier-lifetime changes, which Cr2Ge2Te6 shows across Tc through magneto-elastic and band effects. (ii) A sublinear, saturating fluence dependence is generic for photocurrent and rectification emitters in absorbing semiconductors (absorption saturation, carrier screening, band filling; see concern 7). Since the identification of the radiating channel rests on symmetry that does not separate the candidates, the authors should add measurements that do: (a) emission against in-plane and out-of-plane applied magnetic field, including field reversal, at 7 K and above Tc, at both incidence angles, using the magnet of the OptiCool cryostat they already have (the model predicts a specific dependence on H_dc, Eq. V.33); (b) emission in transmission against reflection from a thin exfoliated flake, since a sheet electric-dipole current and a sheet magnetic dipole radiate with opposite relative sign forward and backward; (c) dependence on sample thickness, on the crystal face, or on surface condition (fresh against aged cleave, capped against uncapped), or a change of pump photon energy to vary the penetration depth; (d) detection of both THz polarization components, since an in-plane magnetic dipole should give an E_y ∝ M_x ∝ sin(2φ − 2δ) channel in quadrature with the measured E_x channel (this constrains the tensor form but not the multipole order); (e) a non-magnetic or isostructural reference and a reference emitter such as ZnTe measured under identical conditions, which also fixes the absolute polarity and the detection-chain response.

2. The claimed "manipulation of ferromagnetism" and the change of the Cr moments are not demonstrated. The only observable is far-field THz emission; no measurement shows ΔM, its direction, or its relation to the ferromagnetic order. Because the signal persists at nearly full strength at 80 K and 200 K (Figs. 2e,f), most of the effect, even within the authors' picture, is a light-induced magnetization of a paramagnet. The itinerant nonequilibrium spin density would also itself radiate as a magnetic dipole without involving the Cr moments at all. Statements such as the field "pull[ing] the magnetization completely into the plane" (p. 4–5, Fig. 4d) are model outputs, not observations. The authors should add a direct magnetic probe of the pumped state with the same pump-polarization dependence (time-resolved Kerr or Faraday rotation or ellipticity, or time-resolved magnetic linear dichroism), showing δM_y(t) ∝ cos(2φ − 2δ) with the same δ as the THz emission. This would also give an absolute scale for the Edelstein–Zeeman field (concern 5). A comparison with isostructural CrSiTe3 (Tc ≈ 33 K) would test whether any temperature feature moves with Tc, and the THz amplitude should be plotted together with M(T) and χ(T) from Figs. S1/S2. Otherwise the title and abstract should be narrowed.

3. The temperature dependence argues against, rather than for, ferromagnetism-mediated emission, and it does not match the model or the text. In Fig. 2f the signal grows from about 0.4 to about 0.85 between 5 and 175 K, and in Fig. 2e the 200 K pattern is the largest. The text says the signal "decreases gradually with cooling and then undergoes a more rapid reduction upon crossing Tc" (p. 2), but within the error bars (about ±0.1–0.15, origin not given) there is no clear feature at Tc: the amplitude is roughly flat from about 40 K to above Tc, and the steep drop is below about 30–40 K, well inside the ordered phase. The petal orientation is identical at 7, 80 and 200 K (Fig. 2e), so the ordered moment, which lowers the magnetic point group from 3' to 3 (SI p. S-17), leaves no trace; magnetization-odd contributions allowed below Tc should be discussed and bounded. In the model, the low-fluence in-plane susceptibility χ_∥ is constant below Tc, peaks near Tc and falls as 1/(T − Tc/(1+κ)) above it (Eqs. V.20, V.23; Fig. S7b, where η_z diverges at Tc). By Curie-Weiss it should be about ten times smaller at 200 K than at 80 K ((80 − 67)/(200 − 67)), yet the measured amplitude is not smaller. A rise with temperature appears only at I0 = 5 Tc0 (Figs. S8b, S9 right), a deep-saturation regime, whereas the temperature series uses 100 µJ/cm², which lies in the near-linear part of the fluence curve in Fig. 4c. The authors must either show, with an explicit map from fluence to I0/Tc0, that 100 µJ/cm² is in the model's high-fluence regime, or reconcile the trend otherwise. They should add the model curve at the same fluence to Fig. 2f, define the plotted quantity and its error bars, take finer polarization maps across Tc (not just 7, 80 and 200 K), explain why the change is concentrated far below Tc, and extend the temperature dependence to room temperature, where a Cr paramagnet should respond weakly.

4. The 45° interpretation relies on a net single-domain out-of-plane magnetization that a soft ferromagnet should not have at zero field, and it compares static magnetization with what is a pump-induced signal. Fig. S2 shows a soft ferromagnet with vanishing coercivity and no remanence, so at zero field the 1.9 mm (×√2) pump spot should cover many up and down domains with net ⟨M_z⟩ ≈ 0. The change δM_z caused by tilting toward +y is odd in the sign of M_z and cancels between domains; only the even δM_y survives. The "frozen easy-axis component M_z" competing with M_y (p. 6) that explains the polarity reversal and the rotated two-fold pattern (Figs. 3b,c and 4d,e; "|M_y − M_z|") would then be absent. The paper does not say whether a field was applied in the 45° OptiCool measurements. In addition, THz emission is set by the pump-induced change ΔM(t) or its time derivative, not by the static M(I0). Fig. 4d plots the full static |M_z|, |M_y| and |M_y − M_z| with M_z starting at 1 at zero fluence, so the minimum of the green curve comes from subtracting a large equilibrium M_z that does not itself radiate. Recomputed with ΔM_y and ΔM_z = M_z(I0) − M_z(0), which have opposite signs, and with the geometric weights of concern 11, the destructive interference and the minimum may not survive. The authors should state the field and magnetic history of the measurement; repeat the 45° fluence series with the sample saturated at +B_z and at −B_z and show that the M_z-derived part and the pattern rotation follow the sign of M_z; repeat the 45° series above Tc, where the sign reversal should vanish if M_z competition is essential; and recompute Fig. 4d using ΔM with correct weights.

5. A static equilibrium model is used to describe sub-picosecond dynamics. The model minimizes a free energy, or solves the self-consistent Eq. V.9, for a static field H^EZ at each fluence (Eq. 4; Fig. 4; Figs. S7–S9) and identifies the emitted amplitude with the resulting equilibrium M. The radiated far field depends on d²M/dt², and H^EZ exists only during the coherence window of a 160 fs pulse and the photocarrier lifetime, while the emitted pulses are single-cycle transients of about 0.5–1 ps (Figs. 2c, 3b,d). Local Cr moments respond through precession and damping, with magnon, anisotropy and FMR frequencies in Cr2Ge2Te6 in the GHz to sub-THz range, and through longitudinal dynamics; neither reaches the adiabatic equilibrium assumed here. In particular, pulling the magnetization completely into the plane (p. 5) needs a coherent rotation that a 100–200 fs torque cannot complete. The model also gives no account of the waveform or spectrum. The authors should solve a dynamical model (for example LLG, Landau–Lifshitz–Bloch or a Bloch equation driven by H^EZ(t) with the experimental pulse envelope), derive the emitted waveform and spectrum, compare them with Figs. 2c and 3d (including the roughly 1 ps duration and 0.3–1.5 THz content), and show that the polarization, fluence and waveform trends survive; or else argue explicitly why the adiabatic limit applies and why the amplitude would track a static M(I0).

6. Steady-state and transient heating are not quantified, although the paper claims a non-thermal mechanism and draws conclusions from behaviour relative to Tc ≈ 67 K. The normal-incidence data use 50 kHz (25 kHz after chopping) and up to 330 mW average power on a 1.9 mm spot in an exchange-gas cryostat at a nominal 7 K (SI Sec. II), where the thermal conductivity of van der Waals crystals is low; the 45° data use up to 90 mW at 3 kHz in a different cryostat. Steady-state heating could raise the local temperature by several to tens of kelvin. Per pulse, an absorbed fluence of about 0.3 mJ/cm² deposited within an optical penetration depth of a few tens of nm gives an energy density of order tens of J/cm³, which literature heat-capacity values suggest is comparable to or larger than the enthalpy needed to heat Cr2Ge2Te6 from 7 K to above Tc. Heating would blur the Tc feature in Fig. 2f, could itself produce the saturation in Fig. 4c through loss of magnetization and the non-monotonic behaviour in Fig. 4e, and means the spin temperature is not the 7 K (T = 0.104 Tc0, Fig. S9) assumed in the model. The authors should give the absorbed fluence and penetration depth at 1.2 eV; estimate or measure the steady-state and per-pulse temperature rise (for example from the shift of a Tc-related feature with average power at fixed fluence, by varying the repetition rate or chopping); state the effective temperature for Figs. 2f and 4; show that the low-fluence pattern is unchanged after high-fluence exposure (no cumulative damage or surface change); and explain what, beyond the polarization dependence, supports the "non-thermal" claim in the abstract and conclusion.

7. There is no quantitative estimate that the proposed mechanism can produce the measured field, and the measured field itself is quoted inconsistently. The main text says "several V/cm" (p. 2), Fig. 2c peaks at about 0.8 V/cm, Fig. 3b at about 0.4 V/cm, and SI Sec. II.C says "on the order of 0.1 V/cm". 9. The fluence-driven rotation and polarity reversal at 45° (Figs. 3b,c, 4e) have simpler two-source explanations that are not excluded. A two-fold pattern that rotates by 90° and passes through a minimum follows from interference between a polarization-independent term A and the fourfold term B·cos(2φ − 2δ) whenever A and B have different signs and scale differently with fluence. Candidates for A include photo-Dember or a surface-field current along z (which radiates p-polarized light at oblique incidence), ultrafast demagnetization of M_z (polarization-independent and nonlinear in fluence), or a term linear in fluence such as surface rectification or a photocurrent added to a saturating term. The main text dismisses photo-Dember as "largely isotropic", but an isotropic term is exactly what this interference needs. The spectrum also changes with fluence (Fig. 3d): the peak moves from about 0.4 THz at 0.1 mJ/cm² to about 1 THz at 0.75 mJ/cm² and the waveform becomes more oscillatory. In the model both M_y and M_z are driven by the same H^EZ(t), so only their relative amplitude should change and the spectrum should stay fixed; a shift points to two sources with different time responses. Fig. 3c shows only two fluences with sparse points, and the minimum in Fig. 4e rests on about three points below 0.4 mJ/cm². The authors should take signed polarization maps at several fluences through the minimum (about 0.1–0.4 mJ/cm²) with repeat runs; fit them with A(F) + B(F)·cos(2φ − 2δ) and with the full oblique-incidence Eq. 3 including the E_z terms; decompose the waveforms into a fluence-dependent sum of two fixed temporal components; and show that the fitted coefficients evolve as the M_y/M_z competition predicts rather than as a sum of independent contributions.

11. Fresnel factors, refraction and radiation projection at 45° are not included. At oblique incidence the transmitted pump intensity differs between s- and p-polarization, so rotating φ also modulates the absorbed fluence. Eq. V.35 uses the external angle ψ, but with n ≈ 3–4 at 1.2 eV the internal angle is about 12°, so the internal E_z is reduced to about sin(12°). 13. The microscopic theory is a generic toy model, not connected quantitatively to Cr2Ge2Te6, and does not treat the ferromagnetic phase. The SI Hamiltonian (Eq. III.3, Fig. S5) is a two-site, uniaxial Dirac/Rashba model with C_n symmetry about z, opposite-sign SOC on inversion partners stacked along z, and unspecified t'1, t'2 and α̃. It shows that χ^(2) ≠ 0 but gives neither its magnitude, sign, nor δ for the real compound, whose 1.2 eV transitions involve Te-p and Cr-d bands with C3 Cr sites in honeycomb layers. The text says intraband dipole-current contributions involve Fermi-surface terms, which an insulator at low temperature lacks. The hidden-spin-texture argument relies on Kramers degeneracy, which is lifted below Tc where bands are exchange-split; there, polarization-dependent absorption by spin-polarized bands, photoinduced magnetic anisotropy and the inverse Cotton–Mouton effect all give polarization-dependent torques from linearly polarized light. J_sd, the photocarrier density and the coherence lifetime (regularized by δ⁺ → 0) set the size of H^EZ but none is estimated. The authors should estimate χ^(2) and H^EZ for the real band structure (for example DFT with SOC, as in Refs. 6–8, or a tight-binding model fitted to it), including the ratio χ_z,xx/χ_x,∥ that the 45° interpretation needs, or present the theory explicitly as a symmetry-allowed proof of principle with magnitude constrained by experiment. They should also discuss how the Edelstein–Zeeman field differs, experimentally and in symmetry, from the established mechanisms above, and explain why the dynamic intra-cell electric quadrupoles of SI Sec. IV.B.4 do not themselves radiate comparably.

16. The polarization purity of the pump inside the cryostat is not verified. The half-wave plate sits before the cryostat window, and strain-induced birefringence in the window at low temperature can add φ-dependent ellipticity. The authors should measure the polarization state after the window, or show with a quarter-wave-plate test that the emission does not depend on helicity.
