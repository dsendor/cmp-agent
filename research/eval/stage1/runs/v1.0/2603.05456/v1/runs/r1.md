Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper reports polarization-dependent THz emission from bulk Cr2Ge2Te6 under 1.2 eV excitation. The authors attribute it to magnetic-dipole radiation from a magnetization driven by a resonant, second-order nonlinear Edelstein effect, which they call an "Edelstein–Zeeman field". The supporting arguments are the hidden spin texture of locally non-centrosymmetric Cr sites, a point-group-3 symmetry analysis and a mean-field Heisenberg model. If established, a non-thermal, polarization-programmable effective field on local moments in a centrosymmetric magnet would interest the ultrafast magnetism and nonlinear-response communities. The theory (an SI derivation of an interband spin response at second order from the dyads v⊗A and A⊗A) is the most developed part. The experiment, as presented, does not yet tie the emission to magnetism. Every observable shown could also come from non-magnetic second-order rectification with the same symmetry.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The idea is timely and general. A rectified, polarization-controlled effective field acting on local moments in globally centrosymmetric but locally non-centrosymmetric crystals ties hidden spin polarization to optical control of magnetism.
- The symmetry analysis for point group 3 (Eqs. 2–3, SI Eqs. IV.58–IV.66, Table S1) is clear. It correctly predicts a cos(2φ−2δ) emission pattern whose petals are not locked to a crystal axis. The data in Figs. 2d,e follow this form.
- The SI microscopic derivation shows explicitly that the first-order spin response cancels over the Brillouin zone while the second-order dipole-current and quadrupole terms survive. This is a useful pedagogical result.
- The data set covers three control knobs (polarization, fluence, temperature) and two geometries (normal and 45° incidence). At 45° there is a clear fluence-driven change of the polarization pattern (Fig. 3c).

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The polarization dependence cannot tell magnetic-dipole emission from electric-type rectification, and no measurement discriminates between them. In point group 3, an in-plane polar vector (P_x, P_y) and an in-plane axial vector (M_x, M_y) both transform as Eg. So any second-order in-plane source has the same normal-incidence form: surface electric-dipole rectification (C3 surface), bulk electric-quadrupole or magnetic-dipole optical rectification, or an anisotropic photocurrent. That form is S ∝ cos(2φ−2δ), with δ material-dependent and not locked to a mirror, because 3 has no mirrors. The fourfold |S| pattern with a non-universal δ (Figs. 2d,e; Eq. 3) is therefore expected for any of these mechanisms. It is not specific to M_y. The two arguments used to exclude a surface contribution (p. 2) are also not decisive:
   - (i) A surface or bulk rectification can be temperature dependent through the band structure, carrier lifetimes and absorption, which change across Tc in Cr2Ge2Te6.
   - (ii) A sublinear fluence dependence is generic once carrier screening, absorption saturation or heating set in (see concern 7).

   The authors have a magneto-optic cryostat (OptiCool) and should use it. They should:
   - (a) measure the emission while applying an in-plane and an out-of-plane magnetic field, and reverse the field, at 7 K and above Tc. The magnetic-dipole interpretation predicts specific changes, for example of the M_z-related term at 45° and of the susceptibility-controlled amplitude near Tc.
   - (b) measure the transient magnetization directly with time-resolved Kerr or Faraday rotation as a function of pump polarization φ, and show δM_y(t) ∝ cos(2φ−2δ) with the same δ.
   - (c) give a thickness or penetration-depth test (several flake thicknesses, or a different pump photon energy) to separate surface from bulk.
   - (d) measure a reference emitter (for example ZnTe or a non-magnetic centrosymmetric crystal) under identical conditions to fix the absolute polarity and the detection-chain response.

2. The temperature dependence argues against, rather than for, a ferromagnetism-mediated emission. In Fig. 2f the signal grows monotonically from 7 K to 180 K, and in Fig. 2e the 200 K pattern is the largest. Within the error bars (about ±0.1–0.15) Fig. 2f shows no clear feature at Tc. The petal orientation is identical at 7, 80 and 200 K (Fig. 2e). This means the ordered moment, which lowers the magnetic point group from 3' to 3 (SI p. S-17), leaves no trace on the response. Magnetization-odd contributions allowed below Tc should be discussed and bounded.

   The model does not reproduce this trend in the regime where the data were taken. At low fluence the in-plane susceptibility χ_∥ is largest at and below Tc and falls as 1/(T − Tc/(1+κ)) above it (Eq. V.20; Fig. S7b). The increase with temperature is obtained only at I0 = 5 Tc0 (Fig. S8b, Fig. S9 right), a deep-saturation regime. The temperature series uses 100 µJ/cm², which in Fig. 4c lies in the near-linear part of the fluence curve. The authors must:
   - show that 100 µJ/cm² corresponds to the high-fluence regime of the model, with an explicit map from fluence to I0/Tc0, or
   - reconcile the trend in some other way, and
   - extend the temperature dependence to room temperature, where a Cr paramagnet should respond weakly.

3. There is no quantitative estimate that the claimed mechanism can produce the measured field, and the measured field itself is quoted inconsistently. The main text says "several V/cm" (p. 2), Fig. 2c shows a peak of about 0.8 V/cm, and SI Sec. II.C says "on the order of 0.1 V/cm". The authors should settle the calibrated value, including detector response and collection efficiency. They should then estimate the transient δM (in µB/Cr) and its rise time needed to radiate it through a magnetic dipole, given the excited volume (spot size and penetration depth).

   That δM should be compared with what the NLEE can supply. The model needs H^EZ of order I0 = 5 Tc0 (about 30 meV, i.e. effective fields of order 100 T) to give the saturation and temperature trends. The authors should estimate J_sd⟨S⟩ from the photoexcited carrier density at 0.1 mJ/cm² and show that such fields are plausible. Without this the mechanism stays qualitative, and the magnitude may instead point to an electric-dipole source (compare concern 1).

4. A static equilibrium model is used to describe sub-picosecond dynamics. The modelling minimizes a free energy, or solves the self-consistent equation V.9, for a static field H^EZ (Fig. 4, Figs. S7–S9). The radiated far field depends on d²M/dt², and H^EZ exists only during the coherence window of a 160 fs pulse. Local Cr moments respond through precession, with magnon and FMR frequencies in Cr2Ge2Te6 in the GHz to sub-THz range, and through longitudinal dynamics. Neither reaches the adiabatic static equilibrium assumed here. In particular, "pulling the magnetization completely into the plane" (p. 5) needs a coherent rotation that a 100–200 fs torque cannot finish.

   The authors should:
   - give a dynamical model (for example LLG or Landau–Lifshitz–Bloch with the transient H^EZ(t));
   - derive the emitted waveform and spectrum from it;
   - compare it with the waveforms in Figs. 2c and 3d, including their roughly 1 ps duration and the 0.3–1.5 THz content.

   They should also explain why the amplitude would track a static M(I0) at all.

5. The 45° interpretation rests on a single-domain M_z, which the sample's own characterization makes unlikely at zero field. Fig. S2 shows a soft ferromagnet with vanishing coercivity and no remanence. In zero applied field the sample should therefore break into up and down domains with net ⟨M_z⟩ ≈ 0. The magnetization change δM_z caused by tilting toward +y is odd in the sign of M_z, so it cancels between domains. Only the even δM_y survives. The competition between M_y and M_z that explains the polarity reversal and the rotated two-fold pattern (Figs. 3b,c, 4d,e; "|M_y − M_z|") would then be absent.

   The paper does not say whether a field was applied in the 45° (OptiCool) measurements. The authors should state the field and magnetic history. They should repeat the fluence series at 45° with the sample saturated at +B_z and at −B_z: the M_z-derived part should reverse sign. A conventional alternative must also be addressed. At 45° incidence, ultrafast demagnetization of M_z radiates, is polarization-independent, and grows nonlinearly with fluence. Interference between this term and a polarization-dependent term could explain Fig. 3c without an Edelstein–Zeeman field.

B. Should address

6. The comparison between model and data is qualitative, and the free parameters are not stated. In Figs. 4b,d the fluence axis is "I0 ∝ |E0|²" in arbitrary units, and the mapping to mJ/cm² used to overlay Figs. 4c,e is not given. The gray curve in Fig. 4c is not identified (model or fit?). For Figs. 4d,e the 45° signal is modelled as |M_y − M_z| with equal weights. The weights should follow from the geometry: refraction (at 1.2 eV n ≈ 3–4, so the internal angle is about 12°), the emission direction and the far-field projection n̂ × M. The authors should:
   - list all parameters (κ, χ_x,∥, χ_z,xx, J_sd, the fluence scale) and say which are fitted;
   - give the projection weights;
   - show the model curves on the same axes as the data.

   The non-monotonic minimum in Fig. 4e rests on about three points below 0.4 mJ/cm² and needs denser sampling with repeat runs.

7. The saturating fluence dependence (Fig. 4c) is taken as evidence for magnetic saturation, but it has standard non-magnetic explanations. These include absorption or band-filling saturation at the resonant 1.2 eV transition, screening of the THz-generating field by photocarriers, and cumulative heating. The authors should:
   - measure the absorbed fluence (pump transmission or reflection) against incident fluence;
   - measure the fluence dependence of a reference quantity, for example the transient reflectivity amplitude;
   - show that the emission saturates at a different fluence or with a different temperature dependence than those quantities.

8. Heating and the effective sample temperature are not addressed. The normal-incidence data use 50 kHz (25 kHz after chopping) and up to 330 mW average power on a 1.9 mm spot in a He-exchange cryostat. This can raise the steady-state temperature by several kelvin or more at 7 K, where the thermal conductivity of van der Waals crystals is low. The 45° data use 3 kHz in a different cryostat. Since the claims depend on behaviour relative to Tc ≈ 67 K, the authors should:
   - estimate or measure the steady-state and per-pulse temperature rise, for example from the shift of a Tc-related feature with average power at fixed fluence (vary the repetition rate);
   - state the effective temperature for Figs. 2f and 4.

9. The emission spectrum changes with fluence at 45° (Fig. 3d). The spectral peak moves from about 0.4 THz at 0.1 mJ/cm² to about 1 THz at 0.75 mJ/cm², and the waveform becomes more oscillatory. In the model both M_y and M_z are driven by the same H^EZ(t), so only their relative amplitude changes with fluence and the spectrum should stay fixed. A shift of the spectral content points to two sources with different time responses (for example a slow demagnetization or photocurrent term and a faster one). The authors should analyse the waveform as a fluence-dependent sum of two fixed temporal components and test whether that decomposition is consistent with their picture.

10. The emission sign is never shown, and δ is not tested against the crystal axes. All polar plots show |S_THz|max or peak magnitudes. Eq. 3 and Eq. IV.64 predict a sign change between adjacent lobes, and p. 3 claims a "reversal in polarity", but only signed amplitudes can show this. The authors should:
    - plot signed peak amplitudes (or the projection onto a fixed waveform) against φ at both incidences;
    - rotate the sample azimuthally at fixed pump polarization. The pattern should rotate with the crystal, which excludes systematic effects from the half-wave plate, cryostat-window birefringence or the detection polarizer;
    - report the a-axis orientation and how it was determined.

    The fitted δ should also be quoted with an uncertainty. The SI uses δ_∥ ≈ 50° (p. S-20), while the main text and Fig. 2f inset put the maximum near φ = 60°.

11. Fresnel factors and refraction at 45° are not included. At oblique incidence the transmitted pump intensity differs between s- and p-polarization, so rotating φ also modulates the absorbed fluence. Eq. V.35 uses the external angle ψ, but the internal E_z is reduced by refraction to about sin(12°). The authors should:
    - include the polarization-dependent transmission and the internal field components when they model Fig. 3c;
    - estimate how large the "out-of-plane" contributions (χ_x,yz, χ_x,xz, χ_z,zz) can really be, given a small internal E_z.

12. The microscopic theory is a generic toy model and does not treat the ferromagnetic phase. The SI Hamiltonian (Eq. III.3) is a two-site Rashba model with unspecified t'1, t'2 and α̃. It is not Cr2Ge2Te6 at 1.2 eV, and no magnitude of χ^(2) is computed. The hidden-spin-texture argument also relies on Kramers degeneracy, which is lifted below Tc, where the bands are exchange-split. There, polarization-dependent absorption by spin-polarized bands, photoinduced magnetic anisotropy and the inverse Cotton–Mouton effect all give polarization-dependent torques on M from linearly polarized light. The authors should:
    - discuss how the Edelstein–Zeeman field differs from these established mechanisms, experimentally and in its symmetry;
    - estimate χ^(2) for the real band structure (for example from DFT with spin–orbit coupling), or at least with parameters taken from Cr2Ge2Te6 band calculations.

13. The number of samples and reproducibility are not reported. The paper does not say how many crystals were measured, whether the normal- and 45°-incidence data come from the same crystal, or whether the pattern, δ and fluence behaviour reproduce across samples and cooldowns. The surface condition (cleaved or as-grown, air exposure) also matters for the surface-contribution question and should be stated. Please give this information and show the key polarization and fluence data on a second crystal.

C. Minor

14. Figure cross-references in the Landau section are shifted. "The agreement between theory and experiment in Figs. 4a and 4b" should cite Figs. 4b and 4c. "Fig. 4c and Fig. 4d" for 45° incidence should be Figs. 4d and 4e.

15. The fluence calibration needs checking (SI Sec. II.B).
    - With f = 3 kHz and the √2 area factor, Eq. II.2 gives about 0.0083·P, not 0.017·P. The quoted constant implies f = 1.5 kHz after chopping, so the text and the formula disagree.
    - Using the 1/e² diameter in a flat-top formula underestimates the peak fluence of a Gaussian beam by a factor of 2. Please state which convention is used.
    - The absorbed fluence, after reflection losses, should also be given.

16. Tc is given as about 66 K (p. 2), 67 K (Fig. 2f) and about 67 K (SI). Fig. 2f's y-axis reads "Spectral Weight", while the text describes |S_THz|max. Please make these consistent and define "spectral weight" (the integration range).

17. The SI magnetization data need corrections.
    - The Fig. S2 caption reads "2 K to 300 14K".
    - The text calls M(H) "nearly linear". The 2 K curve is a sharp step that saturates below about 0.3 T.
    - The saturation moment (about 3.2 µB/f.u.) suggests normalization per CrGeTe3 rather than per Cr2Ge2Te6. Please state the formula unit.
    - The measurement field for Fig. S1 is not given.

18. On p. 3, the two waveforms in Fig. 3b are said to have "comparable amplitudes". Fig. 3c, however, scales the 0.1 mJ/cm² data by ×3, and the Fig. 3b traces differ by a factor of about 2–3. Please make the description match the figures.

19. The Fig. 4 caption for panel a is garbled ("above the Curie temperature Tc and in the presence of the planar Edelstein–Zeeman field"). The four free-energy panels should each be described.

20. The SI THz-field estimate (Eq. II.3) uses the probe angular frequency ω where most treatments use 2π/λ with the full balanced-detection relation. Please give the measured ΔI/I and the resulting field explicitly, so the numbers in concern 3 can be checked.

21. The data are "available upon request". For a claim of this kind, depositing the raw waveforms and polarization scans would help.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add one magnetic-field-dependent data set. Measure the emission against B_z and B_in-plane, with field reversal, at 7 K and 100 K, at both incidences. This is the most direct way to show that the emission couples to M, and it uses the authors' existing magneto-optic cryostat.
2. Add time-resolved Kerr or Faraday rotation against pump polarization, to measure δM(t) directly. Use it to calibrate the magnitude and timescale of the Edelstein–Zeeman field, which also addresses concerns 3 and 4.
3. Replace the static free-energy minimization with an LLG or LLB simulation driven by H^EZ(t) with the pulse envelope. Map fluence to I0 explicitly and compare the simulated far-field waveforms and spectra directly with Figs. 2c and 3d.
4. Add an estimate of the NLEE magnitude for Cr2Ge2Te6 at 1.2 eV, from DFT or a tight-binding model fitted to its bands, and convert it to an effective field in tesla at 0.1 mJ/cm². This would make the proposed mechanism a quantitative prediction.
