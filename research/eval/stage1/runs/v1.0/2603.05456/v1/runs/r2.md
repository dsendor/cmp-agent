Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports time-domain THz emission from bulk Cr2Ge2Te6 (CGT), a centrosymmetric van der Waals ferromagnet, pumped at 1.2 eV. It attributes the emission to magnetic dipole radiation from Cr moments driven by a second-order "Edelstein-Zeeman" field, which it says comes from a resonant nonlinear Edelstein effect enabled by local non-centrosymmetry (hidden spin texture). Optical control of magnetic order through an even-order, polarization-tunable response in a centrosymmetric magnet would be of broad interest, and the supplementary theory (a two-sublattice Dirac model, a symmetry reduction in point group 3-bar and a mean-field Heisenberg model) is a useful framework. The central difficulty is that the data establish a fourfold, polarization-dependent THz emission, but they do not yet establish that this emission is magnetic dipole radiation, or that it reflects manipulation of the ferromagnetic order.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- A clean, reproducible fourfold dependence of the emitted THz amplitude on pump polarization at normal incidence, at 7, 80 and 200 K and at two fluences (Fig. 2d,e), with petals not locked to the crystal axes. This agrees with the 3-bar symmetry analysis (Eq. 3, Eqs. IV.62–IV.66), which permits a non-universal phase δ.
- A striking fluence-driven sign reversal and 90° rotation of the twofold pattern at 45° incidence (Fig. 3b–d), with a minimum in spectral weight near 0.2 mJ/cm² (Fig. 4e).
- A complete symmetry classification of the second-order magnetoelectric tensor in 3-bar, and a microscopic demonstration (Sec. IV.B) that the interband quadrupolar (A⊗A) and dipole-current (v⊗A) channels give a spin density that survives Brillouin-zone integration in a globally centrosymmetric, locally non-centrosymmetric lattice.
- The proposed mechanism is generic and timely, connecting hidden spin polarization, nonlinear Edelstein physics and ultrafast magnetism.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The polarization dependence does not single out a bulk magnetic dipole source, and the arguments against surface electric dipole emission are not decisive. On p. 2 the authors exclude surface second-order rectification on two grounds: it (i) would not track the magnetic ordering and (ii) would scale linearly with fluence. But the (0001) surface of a 3-bar crystal has point group 3. There, an in-plane polar vector (a shift or rectification current J_x, J_y) belongs to the E representation, just as M_x, M_y does in the bulk. So a surface current J_x ∝ a(E_x² − E_y²) + b(2E_xE_y) gives exactly the same |cos(2φ − 2δ)| pattern with a non-universal δ (Eq. 3 and Eq. IV.64 have the same form). Bulk electric-quadrupole rectification and photon drag share this pattern as well.
   - Argument (i) is undercut by the paper's own data. The fourfold pattern is fully present at 200 K, roughly three times Tc, with amplitude comparable to or larger than at 7 K (Fig. 2e, 2f).
   - Argument (ii) does not hold either. Saturating fluence dependence is common for surface photocurrents and rectification in absorbing semiconductors (absorption saturation, carrier screening, band filling).
   As it stands, the central identification of the radiating channel rests on symmetry that does not distinguish the candidates. The authors should add at least one measurement that does:
   - Compare emission in transmission and reflection from a thin exfoliated flake. A sheet electric dipole current and a sheet magnetic dipole radiate with opposite relative sign in the forward and backward directions.
   - Measure dependence on sample thickness, or on the surface (fresh cleave against aged surface, capped against uncapped).
   - Detect both THz polarization components. At normal incidence, an in-plane magnetic dipole M ⊥ k radiates E ⊥ M, so the predicted E_y ∝ M_x ∝ sin(2φ − 2δ) channel should appear in quadrature with the measured E_x channel. This should be checked directly, although it constrains only the tensor form and not the multipole order.

2. The link to ferromagnetism and to the localized Cr³⁺ moments is not demonstrated. The title and abstract claim "manipulation of ferromagnetism", but no measurement probes the Cr magnetization.
   - The temperature data argue against a strong role for magnetic order. The signal is largest well above Tc (Fig. 2f rises from about 0.4 to about 0.85 between 5 and 175 K). The text calls the drop "more rapid" upon crossing Tc, but within the error bars it is not evident. There is no feature tied to the susceptibility, whereas the paper's own Fig. S7b predicts η_z diverging at Tc.
   - In the authors' framework, the paramagnetic response at 200 K should follow the Curie-Weiss susceptibility (Eq. V.20). That susceptibility is about ten times smaller at 200 K than at 80 K ((80 − 67)/(200 − 67)), yet the measured amplitude is not smaller. The paper reconciles this only by moving to a strongly saturated regime, I0 = 5 Tc0 (Fig. S8, S9), with no evidence that the experiment is in that regime.
   - The itinerant nonequilibrium spin density would itself radiate as a magnetic dipole, with no need to involve the Cr moments.
   The authors should do the following:
   - Use the magneto-optic cryostat they already have to measure the emission against an applied field, in-plane and along c, including field reversal below Tc. The model predicts a specific dependence on H_dc (Eq. V.33).
   - Add a direct probe of the local moments: time-resolved MOKE or Faraday rotation with the same pump polarizations, showing a fourfold φ-dependent change in magnetization.
   - Compare with isostructural CrSiTe3 (Tc ≈ 33 K), to test whether any temperature feature moves with Tc.
   - Plot the THz amplitude together with M(T) and χ(T) from Fig. S1/S2.

3. The 45°-incidence interpretation assumes a single-domain sample and an adiabatic response, and it ignores a simpler explanation. The account of Fig. 3 and Fig. 4d,e has a "frozen easy-axis component M_z" competing with a light-induced M_y.
   - Domains: Fig. S2 shows zero coercivity and no hysteresis, so at zero field the bulk is multidomain. Over the 1.9 mm (×√2) pump spot, the net M_z should then average close to zero. The paper does not state whether any field was applied.
   - Adiabatic response: the model minimizes an equilibrium free energy (Eq. 4, Eq. V.9) at each fluence. The radiated field, however, is a single-cycle transient of about 0.5 ps (Fig. 2c, 3b), set by the pump and carrier lifetime. The paper does not justify why Cr moments, with GHz-scale anisotropy and magnon dynamics, would follow a quasi-static minimum on that timescale.
   - Simpler explanation: a twofold pattern at oblique incidence that rotates by 90° and passes through a minimum follows from interference between a polarization-independent term A (for example photo-Dember or a surface-field current along z, which radiates p-polarized light at oblique incidence) and the fourfold term B·cos(2φ − 2δ). The same result follows whenever A and B scale differently with fluence. The main text dismisses photo-Dember as "largely isotropic", but an isotropic term is exactly what this interference needs. The marked change in spectral content between 0.1 and 0.75 mJ/cm² (peak shifting from about 0.4 to about 1 THz, Fig. 3d) also suggests two physically distinct sources with different dynamics, not two projections of one magnetization.
   The authors should:
   - Fit the 45° polar data to A(F) + B(F)·cos(2φ − 2δ) and report how A and B depend on fluence.
   - Repeat at 45° above Tc. If M_z competition is essential, the sign reversal should vanish there.
   - Repeat under an applied field that saturates M_z in each sign.
   - Model M(t) dynamically (for example with LLG including H_EZ(t)) instead of by static minimization.

B. Should address

4. There is no quantitative estimate of the Edelstein-Zeeman field or of the magnetization change. Neither the experiment nor the theory gives a magnitude. The simulations use I0 = 0.005 Tc0 and 5 Tc0, and χ_x,∥ = χ_z,xx = 0.5 (Tc0)⁻¹ (pp. S-20, S-21). The fluence axes in Fig. 4b,d are dimensionless, and the mapping to mJ/cm² is not given. The authors should:
   - Estimate from the measured E_THz the ΔM (in μ_B per Cr) needed to radiate it as a magnetic dipole, given the absorption depth at 1.2 eV, pulse duration and collection optics.
   - Estimate the H_EZ that this requires (in tesla), and compare it with J_sd times a plausible photoinduced spin density at the stated fluences.
   - Calibrate the theory's fluence axis against experiment.
   If H_EZ must be comparable to the exchange scale (I0 ~ 5 Tc0, i.e. tens of meV), that should be stated and justified.

5. The comparison between model and data is qualitative, and parameters are chosen per figure.
   - Fig. 4b and 4c are compared only for "rapid growth then saturation". This shape is generic to any saturable process (the grey curve in Fig. 4c is not defined).
   - The parameters behind Fig. 4b,d are not stated. Fig. 4b shows |M_y| saturating near 0.95, while Fig. S9 (same normal incidence, κ = 0.2) saturates near 0.67. Fig. 4d shows M_z falling to about 0.25.
   - δ∥ ≈ 50° is taken from the data (p. S-20) rather than predicted. κ = 0.2 is not tied to the measured anisotropy of CGT.
   The authors should list all fixed and free parameters for every simulated curve, fit the normal- and oblique-incidence fluence data jointly with one parameter set, and show residuals.

6. Heating and the effective sample temperature are not addressed, although the paper claims a non-thermal mechanism and makes statements about the temperature dependence near Tc. At normal incidence, average powers reach 330 mW at 25 kHz on a 1.9 mm spot (p. S-3), at a base temperature of 7 K, in a closed-cycle or exchange-gas cryostat. Steady-state and per-pulse heating could raise the local temperature by tens of kelvin. That would blur the Tc feature in Fig. 2f and could itself produce the fluence saturation in Fig. 4c through loss of magnetization. The authors should:
   - Estimate the per-pulse and steady-state temperature rise.
   - Check the THz amplitude against repetition rate at fixed fluence.
   - Explain what supports the claim that the mechanism is non-thermal ("non-thermal" in the abstract and conclusion), beyond the polarization dependence.

7. Other causes of the fluence saturation are not excluded. Saturation in Fig. 4c is presented as the signature of Heisenberg magnetization near saturation (p. 6). Saturable absorption at a near-resonant 1.2 eV transition, or Coulomb screening of photocarriers, would give the same curve. The authors should measure pump transmission or reflection against fluence, or carrier density by optical-pump/THz-probe, and plot the THz amplitude against absorbed fluence or carrier density.

8. The polar plots discard sign, and the oblique-incidence plots ignore Fresnel coefficients. Fig. 2d,e and 3c plot |S_THz|max. The model predicts a sign change between adjacent lobes (cos(2φ − 2δ)), which is a stronger test than the magnitude pattern; Fig. 2c hints at this, but only two angles are shown. Please plot the signed peak amplitude, or the projection onto a reference waveform, against φ. At 45° incidence, the s- and p-components of the pump transmit differently, and the internal field has an E_z component that depends on the refractive index at 1.2 eV. This distorts the φ-dependence, and the Eq. V.35 parametrization (incidence angle ψ) appears to use the external angle without refraction. The analysis of Fig. 3c should include the internal fields.

9. Sample count, reproducibility and comparisons across setups need stating. The manuscript does not say how many crystals were measured, their thickness, or whether the 45° sign reversal and the fourfold pattern were reproduced on more than one sample or cleave. The normal- and oblique-incidence data come from different cryostats (Janis and OptiCool), different repetition rates (50 kHz and 3 kHz) and possibly different samples, yet they are compared within one model. How error bars in Fig. 2f, 4c and 4e were obtained is not stated, and the Fig. 2f error bars are large compared with the claimed Tc feature. The authors should report these details and show the key results on at least a second sample.

10. The resonance claim is untested. The mechanism is described as "resonant" interband nonlinear Edelstein physics at a 1.2 eV direct gap at Γ (p. 2; Eq. IV.21). No dependence on photon energy is shown, and CGT's optical absorption at 1.2 eV is not characterized. Even a two-wavelength comparison (for example 1.2 eV against 2.4 eV, or OPA tuning), with the THz amplitude normalized to absorbed fluence, would test whether the response follows the proposed interband resonance.

11. The microscopic model is generic rather than specific to CGT. Sec. III–IV uses a two-site, uniaxial Rashba model with C_n symmetry about z. This shows that a nonzero second-order spin density is possible, but it does not show that it is sizable in CGT. There the Cr sites have C3 symmetry within honeycomb layers, and the relevant states near 1.2 eV involve Te p and Cr d manifolds. The text also says the intraband dipole-current contributions involve Fermi-surface terms, which an insulator at low temperature lacks. The authors should either estimate the χ(2) components for CGT (for example from DFT with SOC, as in Refs. 6–8), or present the theory explicitly as a symmetry-allowed proof of principle, with the magnitude constrained by experiment.

C. Minor

12. The THz amplitude is quoted inconsistently. The main text says "several V/cm" (p. 2), Fig. 2c shows a peak of about 0.8 V/cm, and Sec. II.C says "on the order of 0.1 V/cm". Please reconcile. Please also check r41 for CdTe at 1030 nm; about 4.5 pm/V is commonly quoted, not 7 pm/V.

13. The fluence calibration needs checking. For oblique incidence, Eq. II.2 states f = 3 kHz, but the prefactor 0.017 corresponds to f = 1.5 kHz, i.e. after chopping (P/(π√2(d/2)²f) with f = 3 kHz gives about 0.0083 mJ/cm² per mW). Both formulas use the mean fluence over a 1/e² disk, not the Gaussian peak, which is a factor of 2 larger. They also quote incident, not absorbed, fluence. Please state which fluence is used throughout.

14. Tc is given as about 66 K (p. 2), 67 K (Fig. 2f) and about 67 K (Fig. S1). The Fig. 2f axis says "Spectral Weight" while the text refers to |S_THz|max. Please unify.

15. On p. 3, "point group 3m (D3d)" should read 3-bar m (D3d).

16. The Fig. S2 caption reads "from 2 K to 300 14K".

17. In Fig. 3b, "comparable amplitudes but opposite polarity" is hard to judge. The two waveforms differ in shape and timing as well as sign, and Fig. 3c rescales the low-fluence data by ×3. Please quantify polarity, for example by the sign of the projection onto a common reference waveform.

18. Figure details:
   - The legend of Fig. 4b lists three curves but shows one.
   - The grey curve in Fig. 4c is undefined.
   - The temperature and φ for Fig. 4c and 4e are not stated.
   - Fig. 2d uses a ×2 scaling for the low-fluence data; please state the absolute ratio.

19. Typographical: "Kramer's" should be "Kramers'"; "we find can write" (p. S-9); "it shown to be" (p. S-21). The data availability statement ("upon request") could be strengthened by depositing the waveforms.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add measurements that separate magnetic dipole from electric dipole emission and tie the signal to the Cr moments: emission in transmission against reflection on a thin flake, both THz polarization components, a sweep and reversal of the applied field in the OptiCool magneto-optic cryostat, and a time-resolved Kerr or Faraday measurement with the same pump polarizations.
2. Decompose the 45° data into isotropic and fourfold parts against fluence and temperature, including above Tc. Use this decomposition to test the M_y–M_z competition picture against an interference between photo-Dember and anisotropic emission.
3. Make the theory quantitative: map I0 to mJ/cm², estimate the required ΔM and H_EZ in physical units, and replace the static free-energy minimization with a dynamical (LLG-type) simulation that predicts the THz waveform.
4. Report sample statistics, heating estimates and absorbed-fluence normalization, and consider a CrSiTe3 comparison to test whether any temperature feature follows Tc.
