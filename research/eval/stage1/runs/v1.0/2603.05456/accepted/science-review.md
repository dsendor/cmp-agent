A. Must address

1. **No evidence that the ordered Cr moments, rather than the photoexcited carriers, are the radiating magnetization.** The title, abstract and Discussion claim "manipulation of ferromagnetism" and "femtosecond-to-picosecond manipulation of magnetic order". However, the only observable is far-field THz emission during roughly the first 2 ps: a single-cycle transient with spectral weight at about 0.3–1.5 THz (Fig. 3d) and no feature at the Cr2Ge2Te6 magnon or FMR frequency, which is in the GHz range. The paper itself calls the magnetic degrees of freedom "slower". A transient spin or orbital density of the photocarriers (the nonlinear Edelstein δM of Eq. 1 itself) would radiate as a magnetic dipole with the same φ dependence and without any rotation of the Cr sublattice. The emission pattern and amplitude are also essentially unchanged across Tc (concern 4), so the data do not show that the Cr moments respond on this timescale at all. The authors should separate "carrier magnetization" from "Cr magnetization driven by H^EZ" with a direct probe of the ordered moment at the same fluences and pump polarizations: time-resolved MOKE or Faraday rotation (which should be feasible in the existing setup), at least a static Kerr measurement before and after pumping, or the XMCD they mention only as future work. The probe should show a change in the ordered moment that outlasts the pulse. At minimum they should estimate the Cr response time and the induced δM (concern 9). Without such evidence, the headline claim should be narrowed to THz emission from a light-induced nonequilibrium (nonlinear Edelstein) magnetization, with ferromagnetic involvement inferred only from the 45° data.

2. **No magnetic field is applied and the domain state is not characterized, yet the magnetic origin and the 45° interpretation depend on them.** The 45° explanation (pages 4–6, Figs. 3b–c, 4d–e) assumes a single-domain, out-of-plane equilibrium M_z that competes with the light-induced M_y. Bulk Cr2Ge2Te6 is a soft ferromagnet; in zero field below Tc it forms stripe or bubble domains with net M_z ≈ 0 over a beam spot of tens to hundreds of micrometres, so the M_z contribution to E_THz ∝ (M × n), and the interference that produces the polarity reversal, would largely cancel. The Methods mention a magneto-optic cryostat, but no field-dependent data are shown and the field history (zero-field-cooled or field-cooled) is not stated. Without field-reversal data, the link between the emission and the ferromagnetic order rests only on a modest kink in Fig. 2f. The authors should state the magnetic history and: (i) repeat the 45° fluence series at low and high fluence under ±B along c and after field-cooling in ±B_z, showing that the M_z-derived component reverses sign with the field while the light-set M_y component does not; (ii) show how the field shifts the fluence at which the pattern reorients (Figs. 3d, 4e), which the Landau model ties directly to r0, rz and g; (iii) measure the normal-incidence signal under in-plane and out-of-plane fields; all below and above Tc. This is the most direct test of the two-component magnetic picture.

3. **The polarization dependence does not single out magnetic-dipole emission, and surface electric-dipole, photon-drag and electric-quadrupole channels are not excluded experimentally.** Pages 2–3 and Eq. (3) give |M_y| ∝ |cos(2φ − 2δ)| at normal incidence. An in-plane polar response with point group 3 at the surface has exactly the same form, including surface optical rectification and surface shift or injection currents, P_x ∝ a(E_x² − E_y²) + b(2E_xE_y) with an arbitrary phase; the authors acknowledge on page 3 that "there is no mirror symmetry on the surface ... to lock the surface dipole emission to the crystal axes". In the bulk, linear photon drag (in-plane current ∝ q_z E⊗E, allowed in trigonal symmetry) and bulk electric-quadrupole rectification (same E⊗E structure; Fig. 1 itself invokes quadrupole transitions) are allowed in centrosymmetric crystals and give the same response. The arguments against a surface origin are weak: surface depletion fields, carrier densities and surface χ(2) of a semiconductor are also temperature dependent, Fig. 2f shows a smooth crossover rather than an order-parameter-like onset, most of the signal persists well above Tc (Fig. 2e at 200 K), and saturation with fluence is generic (concern 10). At 1.2 eV the penetration depth is likely only tens of nm, so "bulk" and "surface" are not cleanly separated. Decisive tests should be added: (a) rotating the sample azimuth at fixed pump polarization, compared with rotating the polarization at fixed sample, to relate the lobes to the crystal axes and report δ; (b) detecting both orthogonal THz polarization components (only the component along a is measured, page 2), since Eq. (2) also predicts an in-plane M_x through the M± terms, point group -3 fixes how the full in-plane vector rotates with φ and with a definite phase relation, and the sign of E_THz should alternate between adjacent lobes; this would also show that the response follows bulk -3 symmetry rather than strain, step edges or a particular termination; (c) front versus back illumination, or reflection versus transmission, which separates mechanisms odd in the light wavevector (photon drag) from those even in it (E⊗E → M), and under which magnetic-dipole and electric-dipole radiation flip sign differently; (d) thickness dependence (the thickness is not stated) and excitation-wavelength dependence; (e) incidence-angle dependence, which differs between magnetic-dipole, quadrupole and surface-dipole radiation; (f) a nonmagnetic isostructural or same-point-group layered reference crystal under identical conditions.

4. **The temperature dependence argues as much against magnetic control as for it.** Figure 2e–f and page 2 report that the emission "increases with temperature": the spectral weight is largest in the paramagnetic phase, roughly flat from ~60–70 K to 200 K, about twice its 7 K value near 150 K, and falls only below ~50 K. Ordering therefore suppresses rather than produces the emission, and the bulk of the signal is a paramagnetic-phase nonlinear response. In the mean-field model of Eq. (4) with r_z ∝ T − Tc, the in-plane magnetization induced by a fixed H^EZ should follow the paramagnetic susceptibility, peaking near Tc and falling roughly as 1/(T − Tc) above it, crossing over below Tc to the transverse susceptibility set by the anisotropy; the data show no such feature. The claim that the emission "tracks the magnetic ordering" (page 3) and the "more rapid reduction upon crossing Tc" are hard to see within the error bars, and the explanation of the weak temperature dependence is qualitative. The authors should compute the temperature dependence predicted by their model with stated parameters and overlay it on Fig. 2f; extend the data above 200 K, e.g. to room temperature; measure the fluence dependence at several temperatures above and below Tc, since the model predicts a saturation fluence that tracks r0(T); consider temperature-dependent non-magnetic factors (absorption edge, carrier lifetime, THz absorption); explain how a mechanism that needs a magnetic susceptibility gives its largest signal where the susceptibility is smallest; and, if the high-temperature signal is non-magnetic, show how it is subtracted from the low-temperature signal. The title claim should be framed in light of the effect existing without ferromagnetic order.

5. **Heating and ultrafast demagnetization are not ruled out as the origin of the fluence-driven reversal, although "non-thermal" is a headline claim.** The 45° reversal and 90° rotation occur between 0.1 and 0.75 mJ/cm² at 7 K (Figs. 3b–d), and fluences reach 1.5 mJ/cm². With above-gap 1.2 eV photons absorbed within tens to ~100 nm, 0.4–0.75 mJ/cm² deposits tens of J/cm³; at base temperatures below Tc = 66 K, where the heat capacity of Cr2Ge2Te6 is small, this could transiently heat the excited region above Tc within the electron-phonon time, and cumulative heating depends on the repetition rate, which is not given. Quenching of M_z or a heating-induced anisotropy change would suppress the M_z-related contribution and leave the in-plane one, giving the phenomenology of Fig. 4d. Alternatively, demagnetization of M_z (which radiates into the detected p-polarization at 45°) adds a polarization-independent offset A to a cos 2φ term from M_y; as A changes weight or sign with fluence the twofold pattern rotates by 90° and passes through a minimum, as in Figs. 3c–d and 4e, without any Edelstein-generated M_z. Heating could also explain the saturation at normal incidence (Fig. 4c). The authors should report the repetition rate, spot size and absorbed energy density; estimate the transient and steady-state lattice and spin temperatures at each fluence relative to Tc; decompose the 45° data at each fluence into isotropic and cos 2φ / sin 2φ parts; show the 45° fluence series above Tc (e.g. 80 K), where a reversal due to competition with ordered M_z must vanish; measure a temperature series across Tc at fixed fluence at 45° to test whether the low-fluence (M_z-dominated) component follows the order parameter; and show that the crossover fluence in Fig. 4e does not shift with base temperature or repetition rate as heating would predict.

6. **Inverse Cotton-Mouton and photo-induced anisotropy are not ruled out as the coupling to the ordered moment.** Below Tc, a response δM ∝ M E E (inverse Cotton-Mouton) is allowed for linear polarization in a magnetized crystal, as is a photo-induced change of the magnetic anisotropy. Either would give polarization-dependent emission that changes at Tc. The introduction dismisses these channels on symmetry grounds, but in the ordered phase they are not symmetry-forbidden. The authors should state the specific evidence for a time-reversal-even, interband-resonant α(2) (Edelstein) origin of the ordered-phase response as opposed to these M-linear channels, and test it: field reversal (concern 2), under which the inverse Cotton-Mouton term reverses with M, and pump photon-energy dependence across the direct gap (concern 13).

B. Should address

7. **A static free-energy minimization is compared with a radiated field set by the time derivative of M.** E_THz ∝ ∂²M/∂t² (or ∂M/∂t in the near field), not the static M obtained by minimizing Eq. (4) under a constant H^EZ. The model has no timescale, damping or equation of motion, so it cannot predict a waveform, and it assumes the Cr magnetization follows H^EZ adiabatically on a sub-picosecond timescale, which is not justified for precessional dynamics in a weakly anisotropic magnet. The waveform and spectrum change markedly with fluence (Fig. 3d; the spectral peak moves from ~0.5 THz to ~1 THz), which the static picture cannot capture, and the spectral-weight comparison in Figs. 4c and 4e implicitly assumes the spectral shape is fixed. The authors should model the dynamics (e.g. LLG or a time-dependent Landau or mean-field equation driven by a pulsed H^EZ(t) with the 160 fs pump envelope and the carrier lifetime) and show that it reproduces the waveform and spectrum, or explicitly justify why the spectral weight should be proportional to the static |M_y| or |M_y − M_z|.

8. **The "quantitative" agreement claimed in the abstract is not demonstrated.** The abstract says the polarization, fluence and temperature dependences are "quantitatively captured". However, Figs. 4b and 4d plot the model in arbitrary units against a dimensionless I0 ∝ |E0|², while Figs. 4c and 4e plot data against fluence in mJ/cm²; the mapping between them, the values of r_z, r0, u_z, g, J and χ(2), which are fitted, and whether the anisotropy was fixed independently from magnetometry are not given in the main text. The grey curve in Fig. 4c is not identified as model or empirical fit. Fig. 4e has six points, one (at 1.5 mJ/cm²) with a large error bar, so the minimum near 0.2 mJ/cm² rests on few points. No model curve is shown for the temperature dependence (Fig. 2f) or the polarization patterns. In addition, if "spectral weight" integrates |E(ω)|² it is quadratic in the emitted field, while the model quantity |M_y| is linear in it. The authors should define spectral weight and compare quantities that scale the same way, list the parameters with values and sources and the number fitted relative to the number of data points, overlay the model on Figs. 4c, 4e and 2f in physical units, and either fit normal-incidence fluence, 45° fluence and temperature data jointly with one set of material parameters (anisotropy, Ms, Tc from magnetometry) or soften "quantitatively".

9. **The magnitude of the effect is not estimated.** The paper does not estimate the δM (in μB per Cr or as a fraction of Ms) needed to produce the measured several-V/cm field via the magnetic-dipole radiation formula, given the saturation magnetization and absorption depth; the size of H^EZ implied by the observed saturation, and whether it suffices to rotate M off the easy axis against the anisotropy field; or a microscopic estimate of the nonlinear Edelstein susceptibility J·χ(2) (Eq. 5) for Cr2Ge2Te6 at 1.2 eV from the band structure. Order-of-magnitude numbers would show whether the Cr moments can plausibly be rotated "completely into the plane" at sub-mJ/cm² fluence (as Figs. 4a and 4d and the text suggest), and whether the carrier spin density alone could account for the signal (concern 1).

10. **The saturation at normal incidence has simpler explanations, so it is not evidence against a χ(2) mechanism.** Page 2, point (ii), reads the sublinear growth in Fig. 4c as Zeeman-like saturation of M_y and argues that surface rectification would scale linearly with fluence. But absorption saturation, Pauli blocking or band filling, two-photon absorption, photocarrier screening and a fluence-dependent carrier lifetime all make second-order emission (including surface rectification and photo-Dember emission) saturate at mJ/cm² fluences. The authors should measure the fluence dependence of absorbed power or transient reflectivity over the same range, and ideally compare with the saturation of a nonmagnetic reference. If the THz amplitude saturates while the excited carrier density stays linear, that supports the magnetic interpretation.

11. **The superposition test in Fig. 3d is under-specified.** The 0.4 mJ/cm² trace is compared with E_0.1 + E_0.75 apparently with unit weights. Under the model both the M_y and M_z contributions change with fluence, so an unweighted sum of the end-point traces has no obvious reason to reproduce the intermediate one, and the agreement may be partly coincidental; also, the spectral-weight minimum is near 0.2 mJ/cm² (Fig. 4e), not 0.4. The authors should state whether any scaling, time shift or normalization was applied, give a residual or goodness-of-fit measure, show the same test at other intermediate fluences (0.15, 0.2 mJ/cm²), and ideally decompose all fluence traces globally (SVD or a fit to two fixed basis waveforms with fluence-dependent weights), showing that two components explain the series and that the extracted weights follow the model M_y(I0) and M_z(I0) of Fig. 4d.

12. **Conventional mechanisms at 45° incidence need fuller discussion.** Page 4 dismisses photo-Dember and surface-field emission as "largely isotropic with respect to pump polarization". At 45° incidence, however, s- and p-polarized light are absorbed differently through the Fresnel coefficients, so any z-directed surface current acquires a twofold dependence on φ. Competition between such a term, which saturates with carrier density, and a polarization-dependent rectification term could also produce a polarity reversal and a 90° rotation with fluence. The authors should state explicitly what φ means at 45° relative to the plane of incidence and the crystal axes, and how much E_z the pump carries; measure the polarization dependence of the absorbed fraction at 45°; estimate the photo-Dember or surface-field contribution; and show that their two-component decomposition is not reproduced by these terms.

13. **The interband-resonant (Edelstein) character and the excitation conditions are asserted, not measured or documented.** The pump is described as "near-resonant with the direct gap at Γ", but only one photon energy (1.2 eV) is used, and the relevant interband transitions, the absorption coefficient and the probed depth are not documented. A near-surface excitation also makes the assumption of bulk point group -3 throughout the excited volume less safe. The authors should document the transitions and absorption depth at 1.2 eV, and measure the pump photon-energy dependence across the direct gap, or at least compare with sub-gap excitation, to test that the response comes from near-resonant interband coherence rather than a generic off-resonant magnetic-dipole rectification allowed in any centrosymmetric crystal.

14. **Residual ellipticity and helicity-dependent channels are not controlled.** Residual pump ellipticity from the half-wave plate, especially at 45° incidence, could add an inverse-Faraday M_z contribution whose sign depends on φ and could mimic part of the twofold pattern. The authors should report the measured polarization purity and compare linear with circular pump polarization, which separates helicity-dependent inverse-Faraday, polarization-independent thermal and linear-polarization Edelstein channels.

15. **Samples, characterization and reproducibility are not documented.** The paper does not state how many crystals were measured, their thickness, surface preparation (cleaved? exposure to air? 16. **The error bars do not reflect measurement uncertainty.** In Figs. 2f, 4c and 4e the error bars are the spread from varying the Fourier-transform window (2–3.5 ps in Fig. 2f; 3–4 ps in Fig. 4). This captures sensitivity to the analysis choice, not shot-to-shot, scan-to-scan, spot-to-spot variation, laser-power fluctuation or drift.
