Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper reports THz emission from the centrosymmetric van der Waals ferromagnetic semiconductor Cr2Ge2Te6 under 1.2 eV excitation with linearly polarized light. It attributes the emission to magnetic dipole radiation from a magnetization generated at second order by a resonant nonlinear Edelstein effect, which the authors describe as an "Edelstein-Zeeman field" acting on the Cr moments. The evidence is a fourfold pump-polarization dependence at normal incidence, a temperature dependence with a kink at Tc, and a fluence-driven 90° rotation and polarity reversal of the two-fold pattern at 45° incidence, all interpreted with a Landau model. If the mechanism is established, it would be a new, symmetry-allowed and non-thermal route to optical control of magnetic order with linearly polarized light, and would interest the ultrafast magnetism and nonlinear-response communities. As presented, the THz data alone do not yet single out this mechanism over several standard alternatives.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The experimental observation is clear and of good quality: a large (several V/cm) THz emission from a nominally centrosymmetric crystal, a clean fourfold |cos(2φ − 2δ)| pump-polarization dependence (Fig. 2d,e), and a striking fluence-driven polarity reversal and 90° pattern rotation at 45° incidence (Fig. 3b–d).
- The symmetry analysis of the allowed axial-vector response in point group -3 (Eqs. 2–3) is clear and gives testable polarization selection rules.
- The superposition test in Fig. 3d (E0.1 + E0.75 reproducing the 0.4 mJ/cm² waveform) is a neat way to show that the 45° signal comes from two components of opposite polarity.
- The proposed concept (time-reversal-breaking nonequilibrium magnetic density from interband coherence in locally non-centrosymmetric crystals) is general and could prompt work on other hidden-spin-texture materials.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. No direct test of the magnetic (axial-vector) origin of the emission. The paper never applies an external magnetic field, although Methods says a magneto-optic cryostat was used. The central claim is that the THz field is magnetic dipole radiation from a light-induced change in M that couples to the ferromagnetic order (Fig. 1b, Eq. 4). The decisive checks are missing: (i) reverse the out-of-plane magnetization below Tc with a field along c, and show that the Mz-derived component at 45° incidence (Fig. 3b,c) changes sign while the My-derived component does not; (ii) show how the field changes the fluence at which the pattern reorients (Fig. 3d, 4e), which the Landau model ties directly to the anisotropy terms r0, rz and g; (iii) show how an in-plane field changes the normal-incidence signal. Without field-reversal data, the link between the emission and the ferromagnetic order rests only on a modest kink in Fig. 2f. The authors should add field-dependent and field-reversed emission at 45° and at normal incidence, below and above Tc.

2. Surface electric-dipole and bulk electric-quadrupole contributions are not excluded experimentally. The argument against a surface electric-dipole origin (p. 3) is that a surface χ(2) would not track the magnetic order and would scale linearly with fluence. But the authors themselves note that the surface has no mirror symmetry to lock the pattern to the crystal axes, so the polarization dependence alone cannot separate surface from bulk. Moreover, most of the signal is present well above Tc (Fig. 2e at 200 K; Fig. 2f shows the spectral weight is largest in the paramagnetic phase). Saturating fluence dependence (Fig. 4c) is generic for interband absorption: absorption saturation, carrier screening and band filling all saturate. Bulk electric-quadrupole rectification is allowed in centrosymmetric crystals and has the same E⊗E structure; Fig. 1 itself invokes quadrupole transitions. Standard discriminating tests should be added:
   - emission from both faces, or after flipping the sample;
   - thickness dependence (Methods does not even state the thickness);
   - azimuthal rotation of the sample at fixed pump polarization, compared with rotation of the polarization at fixed sample;
   - emission with the detection axis rotated, or full vector-resolved detection, to check the M × n geometry;
   - dependence on incidence angle, which differs between magnetic-dipole, quadrupole and surface-dipole radiation.

3. The time scale of the "ferromagnetic" response is not reconciled with the THz bandwidth. The emitted transients are single-cycle with spectral weight at 0.5–1.5 THz (Fig. 3d). The uniform ferromagnetic mode of Cr2Ge2Te6 is in the GHz range, and the paper itself calls the magnetic degrees of freedom "slower". The Landau model is static: it minimizes F(M) and gives no time scale, damping or equation of motion. It therefore cannot predict a waveform, which for magnetic dipole radiation is set by d²M/dt². It is unclear whether the emission reflects a reorientation of the ordered Cr moments, as the title and abstract claim ("manipulation of ferromagnetism"), or only the ~ps transient itinerant spin density, which would radiate whether or not it couples to the order. The authors should (a) give a dynamical model (for example LLG with the Edelstein-Zeeman field as a pulse) that reproduces the waveform and spectrum, and (b) provide independent evidence of a change in the ordered moment that outlasts the pulse, such as time-resolved Kerr or Faraday rotation at the same fluences and polarizations. Time-resolved XMCD is mentioned only as future work, but MOKE should be feasible in the existing setup.

4. Thermal and demagnetization mechanisms can mimic the 45° reorientation. At 45° incidence, Mz radiates into the detected p-polarization. Ultrafast demagnetization of Mz, or heating of the spot towards Tc, gives a polarization-independent contribution. Adding such an isotropic offset A to a cos 2φ term from My produces a two-fold pattern. As fluence grows and the relative sign or weight of A changes, the pattern rotates by 90° and passes through a minimum, which is what Fig. 3c,d and 4e show. This reading does not need an Edelstein-generated Mz. The fluences reach 1.5 mJ/cm² with above-gap 1.2 eV photons, and neither the pulse heating nor the steady-state heating is estimated; the repetition rate, spot size and absorption depth are not given. The authors should:
   - decompose the 45° data at each fluence into isotropic and cos 2φ / sin 2φ parts;
   - compare linear with circular polarization, which separates helicity-dependent inverse-Faraday, polarization-independent thermal and linear-polarization Edelstein channels;
   - estimate the transient and accumulated lattice and spin temperature at each fluence relative to Tc.

5. Inverse Cotton-Mouton and photo-induced anisotropy are not ruled out as the coupling to the ordered moment. Below Tc, a response δM ∝ M E E (inverse Cotton-Mouton) is allowed with linear polarization in a magnetized crystal, as is a photo-induced change of the magnetic anisotropy. Either would give polarization-dependent emission that changes at Tc. The paper dismisses these channels in the introduction on symmetry grounds, but in the ordered phase they are not symmetry-forbidden. The specific evidence for an Edelstein (time-reversal-even α(2), interband-resonant) origin of the ordered-phase response, as opposed to these M-linear channels, needs to be stated and tested. Two tests are field reversal (concern 1: the inverse Cotton-Mouton term reverses with M) and pump photon-energy dependence across the direct gap, since the interpretation relies on near-resonant interband coherence.

B. Should address

6. The "quantitative" agreement with the Landau model is not substantiated. The abstract says the polarization, fluence and temperature dependences are "quantitatively captured". However, Fig. 4b,d plot normalized M against fluence in arbitrary units, and the paper does not say which parameters (r0, rz, uz, g, J, χ(2)) are fixed, which are fitted, or how simulated fluence maps onto mJ/cm². The grey curve in Fig. 4c is not identified as a fit or a simulation. Fig. 4e has six points, one of which (at 1.5 mJ/cm²) has a large error bar, and the minimum near 0.2 mJ/cm² rests on only a few points. No modeled temperature dependence is compared with Fig. 2f. The authors should list the parameters with their values and sources, overlay the model on Fig. 4c,e and Fig. 2f in physical units, and either fit the normal-incidence and 45° fluence data with one shared parameter set or soften "quantitatively".

7. The temperature dependence argues as much against a magnetic origin as for one. In Fig. 2f the signal is largest above Tc, flat from ~70 to 200 K, and roughly halves on cooling through Tc. Ordering therefore suppresses rather than produces the emission, and the bulk of the signal is a paramagnetic-phase nonlinear response. The text's account of the crossover (a "weak" temperature dependence because the Edelstein-Zeeman field breaks time-reversal symmetry) is qualitative. The authors should (a) show the fluence dependence at several temperatures above and below Tc, since the model predicts a saturation fluence that tracks r0(T); (b) consider temperature-dependent non-magnetic factors such as the absorption edge, carrier lifetime and THz-range absorption; and (c) frame the title claim of manipulating ferromagnetism in light of the fact that the effect exists without the ferromagnetic order.

8. The interband-resonant (Edelstein) character is asserted, not measured. Only one photon energy (1.2 eV) is used. Pump photon-energy dependence, or at least a comparison with sub-gap excitation, would test the claim that the response comes from near-resonant interband coherence at Γ, as opposed to a generic off-resonant magnetic-dipole rectification allowed in any centrosymmetric crystal.

9. Sample statistics and characterization are missing. The paper does not say how many crystals were measured, the thickness or surface preparation (cleaving, exposure to air; Cr2Ge2Te6 surfaces degrade), whether the fourfold pattern and the 45° reversal were reproduced on more than one sample or spot, or how Tc was determined; the text gives 66 K and Fig. 2f gives 67 K. No magnetization or transport characterization of the crystals is shown. Please add these, with sample-to-sample spread for the key observations in Fig. 3.

10. The error bars do not represent measurement uncertainty. In Figs. 2f, 4c and 4e the error bars are the spread from varying the Fourier-transform window (2–3.5 ps or 3–4 ps). This captures sensitivity to the analysis choice, not repeatability, noise or drift. Repeated scans, or noise estimates from pre-time-zero data, are needed, especially for the non-monotonic dip in Fig. 4e and the kink at Tc in Fig. 2f.

11. The superposition test in Fig. 3d needs clarification. Under the model, both the My and Mz contributions change with fluence. Why, then, should the unscaled sum E0.1 + E0.75 reproduce the 0.4 mJ/cm² trace? Please state whether any scaling was applied, give a residual or goodness-of-fit measure, and show the same test at other intermediate fluences (0.15, 0.2). Otherwise the agreement may be coincidental.

12. Experimental details needed to judge heating and calibration are missing. Methods omits the repetition rate, pump spot size and how fluence was defined, the probe fluence, the CdTe thickness and the resulting detection bandwidth, the purge conditions, and the sample thickness relative to the 1.2 eV absorption depth and the THz wavelength. It also does not describe how the emitted-field amplitude in V/cm was calibrated, given the detector response function and Fresnel factors. These are needed for concerns 2 and 4 and to compare the "several V/cm" magnitude with known emitters.

13. No estimate of the size of the induced magnetization or Edelstein-Zeeman field. The paper should estimate δM, in μB per Cr or as a fraction of Ms, from the measured field amplitude via the magnetic-dipole radiation formula. It should then compare this with a microscopic estimate of J·χ(2) (Eq. 5) from the band structure. That check would show whether the proposed mechanism is large enough to account for the signal and to rotate the ordered moment off the z-axis, as Fig. 4d claims.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add an external-field series in the existing magneto-optic cryostat, with field reversal at 45° and normal incidence below and above Tc. This is the single most decisive addition for the magnetic-dipole and ferromagnetic-coupling claims.
2. Complement THz emission with time-resolved MOKE or Faraday rotation at the same fluences and polarizations. This would show whether the ordered moment actually reorients and on what time scale, and would tie the static Landau picture to the measured dynamics.
3. Separate mechanisms by symmetry: compare linear and circular polarization, decompose the 45° patterns into isotropic and 2φ harmonics, measure both sample faces and several thicknesses, and add pump photon-energy dependence across the direct gap.
4. Give the Landau and mean-field model with explicit parameters and use one parameter set to fit the normal-incidence fluence, 45° fluence and temperature data in physical units, together with an order-of-magnitude estimate of δM and H_EZ.
