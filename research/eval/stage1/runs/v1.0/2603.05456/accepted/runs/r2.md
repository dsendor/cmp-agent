Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors report THz emission from the centrosymmetric van der Waals ferromagnet Cr2Ge2Te6 under 1.2 eV excitation near resonance. At normal incidence the emission amplitude varies fourfold with pump polarization. At 45° incidence a twofold pattern rotates by 90° and reverses polarity as fluence increases. The authors attribute the emission to magnetic-dipole radiation from the Cr magnetization, driven by a light-induced "Edelstein-Zeeman field" (a second-order nonlinear Edelstein effect), and they model it with a Landau free energy. Linearly polarized, non-thermal optical control of magnetic order in a centrosymmetric material would be an important and broadly interesting result. As presented, however, the link from the THz signal to manipulation of the ferromagnetic order rests mostly on symmetry and on trends with temperature and fluence that other mechanisms could also produce.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The idea is attractive and timely: interband nonlinear Edelstein response treated as an effective field acting on slower localized moments. It connects hidden spin polarization in centrosymmetric crystals to ultrafast magnetism.
- The data set is clean and covers several control parameters. It includes polarization-resolved emission at two incidence angles, several fluences and temperatures from 7 K to 200 K, and both time-domain waveforms and spectra (Figs. 2, 3).
- The 45° data are striking: a fluence-driven polarity reversal, a 90° rotation of the twofold pattern, and a minimum in spectral weight at intermediate fluence (Figs. 3b–d, 4e). These are hard to explain with a single term that scales monotonically.
- The symmetry analysis for point group -3 (Eqs. 1–3) is clear. A Landau-type model gives an intuitive picture of competition between in-plane and out-of-plane magnetization (Fig. 4a).

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The polarization dependence does not single out a magnetic-dipole source. Pages 2–3 and Eq. (3) show |My| ∝ |cos(2φ − 2δ)| at normal incidence. An in-plane polar response with point group 3 at the surface has exactly the same form. That includes surface optical rectification and surface shift or injection currents, giving Px ∝ a(Ex² − Ey²) + b(2ExEy) with an arbitrary phase. The authors acknowledge this on page 3: "there is no mirror symmetry on the surface ... to lock the surface dipole emission to the crystal axes".

In the bulk, second-order mechanisms that are allowed in centrosymmetric crystals and do not involve magnetism also give a cos(2φ − 2δ) in-plane response. Examples are linear photon drag (in-plane current ∝ q_z E⊗E, allowed in trigonal symmetry) and electric-quadrupole radiation. The magnetic-dipole interpretation therefore rests only on the trends with temperature and fluence, which concerns 2 and 5 address. The authors should add measurements that separate these channels. Useful ones are:
- dependence on an external magnetic field, using the magneto-optic cryostat listed in the Methods;
- illumination from the front and the back, or reflection against transmission geometry, which separates mechanisms that are odd in the light wavevector (photon drag) from those even in it (E⊗E → M);
- dependence on sample thickness, which separates bulk from surface emission;
- a nonmagnetic, isostructural or same-point-group layered reference measured under identical conditions.

2. The temperature dependence argues against magnetic control, not for it. Figure 2e–f and page 2 report that the emission "increases with temperature" and is largest at 200 K, far into the paramagnetic phase. The spectral weight near 150 K is about twice its value at 7 K. In the model of Eq. (4), with r_z ∝ T − Tc, the in-plane magnetization induced by a fixed H^EZ above Tc scales with the paramagnetic susceptibility. That susceptibility should peak near Tc and fall as roughly 1/(T − Tc) at higher temperature. The data show the opposite trend. The text also says the emission "tracks the magnetic ordering" (page 3), but the claimed "more rapid reduction upon crossing Tc" in Fig. 2f is hard to see given the error bars.

The authors should:
- compute the temperature dependence predicted by their mean-field model and overlay it on Fig. 2f;
- extend the data above 200 K, for example to room temperature;
- explain how a mechanism that needs a magnetic susceptibility gives its largest signal where the susceptibility is smallest.

If the 200 K signal comes from a non-magnetic channel, they need to show how they subtract it from the 7 K signal.

3. The paper does not show that the Cr ferromagnetic moments themselves are manipulated. The title, abstract and Discussion claim "optical control of ferromagnetism" and "femtosecond-to-picosecond manipulation of magnetic order". However, the only observable is far-field THz emission during roughly the first 2 ps. The emission is essentially a single-cycle transient with a spectrum of 0.3–1.5 THz (Fig. 3d). It shows no feature at the CGT magnon or FMR frequency, which is in the GHz range at these fields.

The nonequilibrium spin density of the photoexcited carriers could radiate as a magnetic dipole on its own, without any rotation of the Cr moments. Separately, the model in Eq. (4) finds the static minimum of the free energy. That assumes the Cr magnetization follows H^EZ adiabatically on a sub-picosecond timescale, which is not justified for precessional dynamics in a weakly anisotropic magnet.

The authors should:
- solve the dynamics, for example Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert or time-dependent mean-field equations, with H^EZ(t) set by the 160 fs pump and the carrier lifetime;
- estimate the H^EZ magnitude needed to rotate M away from the easy axis against the anisotropy field of CGT;
- estimate the ΔM needed to produce the measured several-V/cm field, given the CGT saturation magnetization and the absorption depth;
- add a direct probe of the Cr magnetization, such as time-resolved polarization-dependent MOKE or Faraday rotation pump-probe, or at least a static Kerr measurement before and after pumping.

Without such a probe, the claim should be narrowed to observation of light-induced nonequilibrium magnetization.

4. The magnetic domain state and the applied field are not specified, yet the 45° interpretation depends on them. The 45° explanation (pages 4–6, Fig. 4d) assumes a single-domain, out-of-plane equilibrium Mz that competes with the light-induced My. Bulk Cr2Ge2Te6 is a soft ferromagnet. In zero field it forms stripe or bubble domains with net Mz ≈ 0 over a beam spot of tens to hundreds of micrometres. In that case the Mz contribution, and the interference that produces the polarity reversal, would average to near zero.

The Methods do not say whether a field was applied or whether the sample was field-cooled. The authors should state the field history. They should then repeat the 45° fluence series after field-cooling in +B_z and in −B_z. The prediction is clear: the Mz-derived component should reverse sign with the field, while the light-set My component should not. This is the most direct test of the two-component picture and would greatly strengthen the paper.

5. The paper does not rule out heating, although "non-thermal" is a headline claim. At 0.4–0.75 mJ/cm² with a 1.2 eV pump, absorption within roughly 100 nm deposits tens of J/cm³. At base temperatures below Tc = 66 K, where the lattice heat capacity of CGT is small, this could transiently heat the excited region above Tc within the electron-phonon time. Average heating depends on the repetition rate, which is not given. The fluence-driven 90° rotation and polarity reversal at 45° (Fig. 3), and the saturation at normal incidence (Fig. 4c), could then reflect transient or cumulative heating, including quenching of Mz (ultrafast demagnetization) or a heating-induced change in anisotropy, rather than an increasing H^EZ.

The authors should:
- report the repetition rate, spot size and absorbed fluence;
- estimate the transient and steady-state temperature rise;
- show that the crossover fluence in Fig. 4e does not shift with base temperature or repetition rate in the way a heating mechanism would predict.

B. Should address

6. The superposition test at 0.4 mJ/cm² (Fig. 3d, second panel) is not a strong test. The waveforms at 0.1 and 0.75 mJ/cm² are added with unit weights and compared with the 0.4 mJ/cm² trace. If the signal is a sum of two components with fluence-dependent weights, an unweighted sum of the two endpoint traces should not generally reproduce the intermediate one. The agreement may therefore be partly coincidental. Also, the spectral-weight minimum is near 0.2 mJ/cm² (Fig. 4e), not 0.4.

The authors should decompose all fluences globally, for example with SVD or a fit to two fixed basis waveforms with free weights. They should then show that two components explain the full series, and plot the extracted weights against the model My(I0) and Mz(I0) from Fig. 4d.

7. The 45° results need a fuller discussion of conventional mechanisms. Page 4 dismisses photo-Dember and surface-field emission as "largely isotropic with respect to pump polarization". At 45° incidence, however, s- and p-polarized light are absorbed differently through the Fresnel coefficients. Any z-directed surface current would therefore acquire a twofold dependence on φ. Competition between such a term, which saturates with carrier density, and a polarization-dependent rectification term could also produce a polarity reversal and a rotation of the pattern with fluence.

At 45°, the meaning of φ relative to the plane of incidence and to the crystal axes, and how much Ez the pump carries, need to be stated explicitly. The authors should:
- measure the polarization dependence of the absorbed fraction at 45°;
- estimate the photo-Dember or surface-field contribution;
- show that their two-component decomposition is not reproduced by these terms.

8. The model–data comparison is qualitative, yet the abstract says it is "quantitatively captured". Figures 4b and 4d give the model in arbitrary units against I0 ∝ |E0|², and the main text gives no parameter values (r_z, r0, u_z, g, J, χ(2)). The paper also does not say which parameters were fitted, how I0 maps onto the measured fluence, or whether the anisotropy was fixed independently from magnetometry.

Figure 4c compares a saturating curve with the "spectral weight". If spectral weight is an integral of |E(ω)|², it is quadratic in the emitted field, while the model quantity |My| is linear in it. The authors should define spectral weight, compare quantities that scale in the same way, list the parameters and their sources, and state how many parameters are fitted relative to the number of data points. They should also either temper the word "quantitatively" or show a joint fit across fluence, temperature and incidence angle with fixed material parameters.

9. Only one component of the emitted field is detected, the one along a (page 2). Equation (2) also predicts an in-plane Mx (M± terms), which would radiate in the orthogonal polarization. Point group -3 makes a definite prediction for how the full in-plane vector rotates with φ. The authors should measure both orthogonal THz polarizations, and the dependence on sample azimuth at fixed pump polarization, to test the full tensor of Eq. (2). That would fix δ and show that the response follows bulk -3 symmetry rather than strain, step edges or a particular surface termination.

10. The saturation argument against surface rectification is incomplete. Page 2, point (ii), argues that the saturation in Fig. 4c is incompatible with a χ(2) mechanism, whose amplitude would scale as F. But saturable absorption, screening by dense photocarriers, two-photon absorption and band filling all make second-order emission saturate at mJ/cm² fluences. The authors should measure reflectivity or absorption against fluence, and ideally compare with the saturation behaviour of a nonmagnetic reference, before using saturation as evidence for a magnetic mechanism.

11. Sample statistics and reproducibility are not reported. The paper does not state how many crystals were measured, their thickness, whether they were freshly cleaved, how the a-axis was determined (Laue or another method), or whether the fourfold pattern and the 45° reversal appear in more than one sample and spot. The error bars in Figs. 2f, 4c and 4e come only from varying the Fourier-transform window. They do not include repeated scans or fluctuations in laser power. The authors should report these details and give error bars that reflect repeatability.

C. Minor

12. The Methods omit the repetition rate, spot size, sample thickness, whether emission is collected in transmission or reflection at normal incidence, the applied field, the thickness of the CdTe detector crystal and the detection bandwidth, and whether the THz path was purged. The Fourier-transform windows differ between Fig. 2f (2–3.5 ps) and Fig. 4 (3–4 ps). Please justify the choice or unify the windows.

13. Tc is given as about 66 K in the text and as 67 K in Fig. 2f. Please make these consistent and state how Tc was determined for these crystals, for example by magnetometry.

14. The polar plots in Figs. 2d–e and 3c show |S_THz|max, which hides sign changes. Sign is central to the arguments for interference and for cos(2φ − 2δ). Please plot signed amplitudes, or the projection onto a fixed reference waveform. Please also explain how the comparable φ = 0 amplitudes in Fig. 3b fit with the 3× scaling of the low-fluence data in Fig. 3c.

- Please define δ explicitly in terms of α_xxy and α_xxx.
- "Cr2Si2Te6" on page 3 should be CrSiTe3, as in Ref. 35.
- References 32–33 contain "\mmfnt".

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Do a field-cooled ±B_z series at 45°, plus an in-plane field series at normal incidence. A component that reverses with magnetization, together with one set by the light polarization, would be the clearest evidence for the two-component magnetic picture.
2. Add a direct, time-resolved probe of the Cr magnetization (polarization-dependent pump–probe MOKE or Faraday rotation). Pair it with a dynamical model, such as LLG with H^EZ(t), that predicts the THz waveform rather than only its peak amplitude.
3. Measure a nonmagnetic, centrosymmetric layered reference with the same point group, and CGT samples of different thickness. Front and back illumination under identical conditions would separate bulk magnetic-dipole emission from surface rectification and photon drag.
4. Provide a microscopic estimate of the H^EZ magnitude per unit fluence, and use it in a joint fit of the dependences on fluence, temperature and incidence angle with fixed material parameters (anisotropy, Ms, Tc from magnetometry). That would justify the word "quantitatively" in the abstract.
