Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors FIB-structure exfoliated Sr2RuO4 flakes into micron-scale rings. They report that Josephson junctions form spontaneously in the ring arms, and they attribute these junctions to pinned superconducting domain walls. In these devices they report violation of Ic+(H) = Ic-(-H), a zero-field supercurrent diode effect, a narrowed central lobe in the SQUID pattern (about 0.73 Phi0), current-switchable bistable Ic states under in-plane field, and half-integer Shapiro steps. If the Ic+(H) ≠ Ic-(-H) violation is shown to be intrinsic, this would be a transport-based, device-level signature of TRSB in unstrained Sr2RuO4 and a new kind of Josephson element. The domain-wall Josephson idea extends the authors' earlier npj Quantum Materials work; the new elements are the time-reversal test and the in-plane-field phenomenology.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The TRSB test in the Ic(H) pattern is the right observable. It is applied to more than one device (Rings 1, 2 and 3) and at several temperatures (Ring 2 at 400, 800 and 1000 mK).
- The extraction procedure for Ic (S2) is documented, and it is validated on a conventional MoGe/Ag SNS SQUID that shows no TRSB within error (Fig. S9) and is well fitted by an RCSJ model (Fig. S11b).
- Ring 2 was re-milled in steps (disk, ring with island, ring, single arm; Fig. S2). This is unusually informative: it shows directly how geometry controls the Josephson response.
- The phenomenology is rich and, if robust, interesting: a zero-field diode that keeps its sign over a full period, a narrow central lobe that does not depend on sweep direction, and Ic switching by a training current whose polarity reverses with the in-plane field.
- Devices keep Tc of about 1.3 to 1.5 K and RRR above 100 after FIB processing, which supports sample quality.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. No in-situ null control or training test for the TRSB signal (Sec. IV, Fig. 2b,c, Fig. S6). The only reference showing Ic+(H) = Ic-(-H) is a MoGe/Ag SQUID (Figs. S7 to S9). It is a different material with different Ic, RN, field scale and temperature, and the paper does not say whether it was measured in the same cryostat, magnet and wiring. Several extrinsic mechanisms can produce ΔIc of a few µA in a 100 µA device:
   - a DC offset in the current source;
   - Abrikosov vortices trapped in the wide Sr2RuO4 banks or the Au/Ti leads near one arm, which add a local phase offset that a global field shift cannot remove;
   - hysteresis of the superconducting magnet between the +H and -H halves of the sweep;
   - slow thermal drift, which the authors themselves cite to explain the residual diodicity in Fig. S9.

   In Ring 2 (Fig. 2c), ΔIc is a nearly field-independent offset of about -5 µA, with jumps at the lobe minima. A field-independent offset is the signature a current offset or a vortex-induced phase offset would give. The authors already have natural null devices from the same crystal: the disk stage of Ring 2 (Fig. S2a), the single-arm stage (Fig. S2d), Ring 4, and their 3K-phase rings. All could be measured with the identical protocol. Requested:
   - (a) The ΔIc analysis of Fig. 2b,c applied to at least one Sr2RuO4 device in the same setup where TRSB should be absent, such as the disk stage, or the same ring above the TRSB onset as in Fig. S6a.
   - (b) Ic+(H) and Ic-(-H) taken with interleaved polarity at each field point, and with the current leads swapped. Swapping the leads reverses any instrument offset but not an intrinsic diode.
   - (c) Repeated cooldowns, zero-field-cooled with the remanent field nulled, and field-cooled in +Hz versus -Hz. Cooling picks the domain chirality at random, so if the signal is intrinsic, the sign of the zero-field diode and of ΔIc should vary between cooldowns or follow the cooling-field sign, as it does in Kerr training. A sign that never changes across cooldowns would point to an extrinsic origin.

2. The error analysis does not cover the dominant uncertainties, and the field-offset correction is ambiguous (S2, Eq. 1, Fig. 2 error bars).
   - The parametric bootstrap propagates only voltage noise. It ignores the stochastic spread of switching currents, which is the main uncertainty in Ic. In Sr2RuO4 junctions in particular, telegraph noise near Ic is well documented (Refs. 31 to 33, discussed by the authors).
   - The paper does not say how many IV sweeps enter each point, or whether the error bars in Fig. 2 reflect run-to-run reproducibility.
   - As written, Eq. 1 minimizes [Ic+(H+H0) − Ic−(H+H0)]², which is the diode asymmetry at the same field, not the time-reversal asymmetry Ic+(H) versus Ic-(-H). It is unclear which quantity was actually minimized, and how this interacts with a genuine zero-field diode.
   - A SQUID built from two φ0-junctions with different anomalous phases shows a pattern shifted in field. Any shift-based remanent-field correction will partly absorb exactly this intrinsic signature, while leaving extrinsic distortions in place.

   Requested:
   - Give the fitted H0 for each dataset, with an independent remanent-field calibration (for example a Hall sensor or a reference superconductor in the same mount).
   - State the sign convention in Eq. 1.
   - Show switching-current histograms at a few field points, and recompute the significance of ΔIc using the measured switching distribution width.
   - Show ΔIc with and without the H0 correction.

3. The claim that the junctions are intrinsic domain walls, rather than extrinsic weak links, rests on indirect arguments (Sec. III; Figs. 1, 2a; Fig. S3).
   - The text says "Shapiro steps can only originate from Josephson coupling, we can rule out Little-Parks oscillations or kinematic vortices". This is too strong. Phase-slip centres and phase-slip lines in narrow superconducting strips also lock to microwaves and produce voltage steps at hf/2e.
   - The single-arm stage of Ring 2 shows Shapiro steps and a Gaussian Ic(B) envelope (Figs. S3, S4). Any FIB-defined constriction, or any Ga-implanted or amorphised region, could do the same. Fig. S3 is presented as confirming "the presence of a chiral domain wall", but it does not discriminate.
   - The IcRN products are very small: about 80 µA × 19 mΩ ≈ 1.5 µV for Ring 1, against Δ/e ≈ 0.2 to 0.3 mV. The authors should discuss what this implies about the weak-link structure.
   - Both the TRSB claim and the order-parameter conclusion depend on the junctions being intrinsic Sr2RuO4 domain walls. Extrinsic weak links containing magnetic damage or trapped flux could also break time-reversal symmetry.

   Requested: evidence that localises the junctions and ties them to the domain structure. Options include:
   - devices with deliberately different neck widths, or with the arms milled asymmetrically, to show where the phase drop sits;
   - a quantitative comparison with the TDGL predictions of Ref. 38;
   - a statement of how many nominally identical rings did and did not show junctions;
   - the 3K-phase ring data (cited as showing no SQUID oscillations) shown in the supplement as a fabrication-matched null.

B. Should address

4. "Unstrained" and "strain-free" are asserted, not measured (Abstract; Sec. III; Sec. VII). The flakes are 0.25 to 0.7 µm thick, sit on SrTiO3, and are capped with SiOx or Au. FIB milling leaves a damaged rim of up to about 10 nm. Differential thermal contraction can impose inhomogeneous strain of order 0.1%. Tc of 1.3 to 1.5 K does not exclude this, because Tc rises only gently at small strain, and the 3K phase requires dislocations or large strain. The Discussion rejects the strain-texture scenario (Refs. 55, 56) on the grounds that the crystals are "ultra-pure and strain-free". That scenario is precisely the leading alternative explanation for TRSB at domain walls. Requested: an estimate of the thermal-mismatch strain for this stack, Raman or strain-sensitive data if available, and a statement that the discriminating argument against Refs. 55 and 56 rests mainly on domain-wall mobility.

5. The narrow central lobe and the toy model (Fig. 2a; S3; Fig. S10).
   - The model is admittedly heuristic. Eq. 3 assumes a linear coupling α(Ψ+ − Ψ−)B without derivation. Eq. 4 is an ad hoc sigmoid.
   - The fit has at least three free parameters (φm, a, Φint^max) on top of the RCSJ/envelope parameters. The fit quality in Fig. S10 is modest: outer lobes are overshot and lobe widths are mismatched (fit 0.91 versus data around 0.97).
   - The fitted Φint^max = 0.24 Phi0 (Ring 1) implies a sizeable spontaneous flux from a single-chirality ring. This should be compared with the null results of scanning SQUID and Hall searches for chiral edge currents in Sr2RuO4, which place upper bounds orders of magnitude below chiral p-wave estimates.
   - Alternative explanations for a narrowed central lobe should be excluded: field-dependent flux focusing as vortices enter the wide banks, and vortex entry at |H| of about 2 mT.

   Requested:
   - the number of free parameters and their posteriors;
   - an order-of-magnitude estimate of the domain flux compared with the edge-current bounds;
   - Ic(H) taken after zero-field cooling for first-sweep and return-sweep, over a wider field range, to look for vortex-entry signatures.

   The S10 caption also refers to a panel (d), Ring 2 at 400 mK, that is not present.

6. The bistable Ic states have conventional explanations that are not excluded (Sec. V; Fig. 3; Figs. S13, S16).
   - With an in-plane field, Josephson-like vortices between RuO2 layers, or Abrikosov vortices in the thin flake, feel a Lorentz force whose sign depends on the product of current sign and field sign. That is exactly the observed rule: the roles of the training currents swap when Hx reverses.
   - Field misalignment adds an out-of-plane component (1° of 20 mT ≈ 0.35 mT, a sizeable fraction of the about 4.7 mT period). If βL is not small, the ring can host several fluxoid states that a large bias current can switch between.

   Requested:
   - βL (from L_kinetic and the geometric inductance) for each ring;
   - the out-of-plane alignment accuracy and how it was set;
   - Ic(Hz) patterns of the high-Ic and low-Ic states: a relative shift by a fluxoid would identify a fluxoid-state change;
   - the temperature dependence of the switching thresholds.

   The strong sample-to-sample variation of the optimal field angle (20° versus 55°) and the irregular map in Fig. S16b also deserve discussion in terms of what pins the domain configuration.

7. The half-integer Shapiro steps are not tied uniquely to a modified current-phase relation (Sec. VI; Fig. 4; Figs. S14, S15).
   - Half-integer steps arise conventionally in two-junction SQUIDs with finite inductance near half-flux bias, at Ic minima of a Fraunhofer-like pattern (as the authors note for 4.5 and 9.9 mT), and under strong RF drive with heating.
   - The steps change after resetting the field (Fig. S14): steps are missing, and the half-step appears on only one polarity. Reproducibility is therefore limited.

   Requested:
   - Step widths versus RF power (Bessel-type maps) for integer and half-integer steps, compared with RCSJ simulations of an inductive SQUID with sinusoidal CPR at the corresponding flux.
   - The device temperature at 20 dBm drive.

   The power map in Fig. 1d is shown only as a histogram, with no comparison to the expected Bessel dependence.

8. Sample statistics and temperature dependence of the TRSB signal (Sec. III; Fig. S1; Fig. S6).
   - The text says features are "universally observed in a large selection of samples". However, TRSB data appear for three rings, and for Ring 3 only at a single temperature (1100 mK).
   - Ring 3 shows no significant TRSB at 1200 mK, described as "very close to Tc", yet Fig. S1 lists its Tc as 1.5 K.
   - The onset temperature of TRSB relative to Tc is a key discriminator. µSR and Kerr find TRSB onset at Tc in unstrained samples, whereas strain-split scenarios predict a lower onset.

   Requested:
   - the number of devices fabricated, how many showed junctions, and how many were tested for and showed TRSB;
   - the ΔIc amplitude (and zero-field diode efficiency) versus T/Tc for each ring, with the onset compared with the resistive Tc of the same device.

9. The order-parameter conclusion goes beyond the data (Sec. VII; Conclusion). A transport TRSB signal plus mobile domains is consistent with any two-component TRSB state with degenerate domains, including non-chiral combinations such as s+id or d+ig. Only the toy model and the narrow central lobe invoke chirality and domain flux specifically. The statement that Eg pairing "is favored based on the experiments" should be softened, or supported with an observable that separates chiral from non-chiral domains (for example the sign relation between field-cooling direction and diode sign requested in concern 1c). The authors acknowledge, but do not resolve, the conflict with the absence of a second heat-capacity anomaly.

With Fig. S1 dimensions this gives Φ0/A ≈ 5.4 mT for Ring 1, but the outer lobes labelled 1.00 Phi0 span only about 4.7 mT. 4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Make the cooldown-and-training experiment the centrepiece of Sec. IV. Zero-field-cool and field-cool in ±Hz over several thermal cycles, and show how the sign of the zero-field diode and of ΔIc is distributed. Random or trainable signs would be far more convincing than any single pattern.
2. Use the Ring 2 milling sequence as a built-in control. Show Ic+(H) versus Ic-(-H) and the zero-field diode for the disk, island and single-arm stages, measured with the same protocol as Fig. 2c.
3. Replace the ad hoc sigmoid toy model with the TDGL simulations already used in Ref. 38. Include the bias current and an in-plane field, so that the central-lobe width, the zero-field diode and the bistability can be compared with data quantitatively, with no geometry-free parameters.
4. Collect the device statistics (fabricated, showing junctions, tested for TRSB, showing TRSB) and the T/Tc dependence of ΔIc for every ring in one summary figure.
