Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors use low-temperature STM/STS on cleaved Ta2Pd3Te5 to study the in-gap states that form around individual surface Te vacancies. They interpret these states as the excitonic analogue of impurity-induced pair-breaking (Yu-Shiba-Rusinov-like) states in superconductors. The impurity problem in an excitonic insulator was posed theoretically in 1967 and has had little experimental study, so an atomic-scale spectroscopic fingerprint of local excitonic depairing would be of broad interest. It would also give independent evidence that the gap in Ta2Pd3Te5 is excitonic, which is still debated. The novelty rests on whether the in-gap states can be distinguished from ordinary donor/acceptor-like defect states in a narrow-gap semiconductor. As submitted, the paper does not make that distinction. A closely related study (ref. 41) appeared during review.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- High-quality atomic-resolution data. Decorated-tip imaging lets the authors assign defects to specific Te chains (Te1 to Te4), and the defect-resolved statistics of the in-gap pair separation 2δ (Fig. 2d) are carefully presented.
- Several independent handles on the same states: tip-approach dependence (Fig. 2e, S4, S5), temperature dependence (Fig. 2g), spatial mapping showing that the Ee and Eh profiles coincide (Fig. 3g,h), dimer hybridization (Fig. S10, S11), and the dependence on the local electronic environment (Fig. 4).
- A mean-field two-band impurity model is used to discuss the trends, and the authors set out clearly the expected differences between excitonic and superconducting pair breaking (particle-hole asymmetry, orbital character, impurity selectivity).
- A timely topic, given recent transport, ARPES and STS claims of an excitonic insulator in Ta2Pd3Te5 (refs. 6, 21-23).

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. No discrimination from conventional defect states in a semiconductor.
   - Where: Figs. 2-4 and the Conclusion.
   - What is missing: Every feature used as evidence for pair breaking also appears in an ordinary semiconductor or band insulator with a vacancy that has donor/acceptor or charge-transition levels. These features are (i) a pair of in-gap peaks, (ii) energies that depend on the defect site, (iii) peaks that shift with tip-sample distance, (iv) Ee and Eh profiles with the same spatial shape (they can be two charge-transition levels of one localized orbital), and (v) bonding/antibonding splitting in dimers. The Conclusion states that the results "confirm that the probed insulating gap … arises from the excitonic pairing". That inverts the logic: the pair-breaking interpretation assumes an excitonic gap, and the data do not exclude a non-excitonic gap with defect levels.
   - What to do: Identify at least one observable that the excitonic scenario predicts and a single-particle defect-level scenario does not, and test it. Options:
     - A DFT supercell calculation of the Te vacancy (with the appropriate surface and band structure, no excitonic order) to show whether the in-gap levels at the observed energies arise without any excitonic order parameter.
     - A demonstration that the in-gap states disappear, or merge into the continuum, as the excitonic gap closes on warming toward the transition temperature. This requires temperatures well above 20 K; see concern 3.
     - A check of the predicted relation between the bound-state decay length and the excitonic coherence length ħv/Δ, set against the measured widths of 0.6 nm and 2.5 nm.
   - Without such a test, the headline claim in the title is a hypothesis, not a finding.

2. The reported "subgap" energies are inconsistent with the stated gap.
   - Where: Fig. 1d, Fig. 2c,d and Fig. 3d-f.
   - What is wrong:
     - The text gives the hole-like gap onset at about -35 mV and Δ = 85 meV (Fig. 1d). Yet the Eh state of VTe2 is mapped at -45 meV (Fig. 3f) and appears near -45 mV in Fig. 2c, which is outside the stated hole edge.
     - The histograms in Fig. 2d give a most probable 2δ of 104 meV for VTe3, with tails to about 120 meV. These separations exceed the 85 meV gap.
   - Why it matters: Bound states of a pair-breaking impurity must lie inside the local gap. If 2δ > Δ, then either the gap definition is wrong, or the local gap near the defect is different, or the peaks are not true in-gap bound states.
   - What to do: Define the gap edges quantitatively (criterion, threshold, spatial averaging). Report the local gap measured just outside each defect. Plot Ee and Eh, not only 2δ, against these edges for every defect in Fig. S3.

3. The temperature dependence is too narrow to support the pair-breaking claim.
   - Where: Fig. 2g.
   - What is missing: Data stop at 20 K, while the excitonic order in Ta2Pd3Te5 is reported to persist to roughly 10² K (refs. 5, 6). Over 1.6-20 K the excitonic gap should be essentially temperature independent. The authors nonetheless invoke "the thermal suppression of excitonic gap" and say the bound states "decay faster" than Fermi-function convolution.
   - Why it matters: The extra broadening could come from a phonon-assisted or inelastic lifetime of a localized defect level, a temperature-dependent tip or junction, or thermal drift. None of these needs pair breaking.
   - What to do:
     - Extend the measurements to temperatures where the gap itself evolves.
     - Show the gap edges of a defect-free region at the same temperatures, alongside the impurity spectra.
     - Quantify the extra broadening, with stated lock-in modulation amplitude and instrumental resolution.
     - Explain why the peaks "shift toward the gap center", which is not obvious in Fig. 2g.

4. Tip-induced band bending and tip gating are not considered.
   - Where: Fig. 2e, Fig. 4e and the abstract ("continuously tunable by tip electric field").
   - What is missing: The tip-approach series runs to 8 nA at 0.1 V, and Fig. 4e runs to 90 nA (junction resistance about 1 MΩ). In a material with a narrow gap and low carrier density, tip-induced band bending, defect ionization (charging) and tip gating are the standard explanations for peaks that shift with set-point and "trigger" at high current. The modelling instead treats the tip as a change of the impurity scattering potentials J1 and J2. It then finds that |J1| and |J2| must decrease on approach, which the authors themselves call "unexpected", and resolves this by positing a tip potential of opposite sign. This is an ad hoc fit.
   - What to do:
     - Run set-point series at several bias set-points of opposite polarity, so that tip-sample distance and tip field are separated.
     - Look for charging rings in dI/dV maps around the vacancies.
     - Estimate the band bending from the work-function difference, gap and carrier density.
     - Show that the observed shifts cannot be reproduced by an electrostatic shift of a localized level.
   - The threshold behaviour at ≳25 nA in Fig. 4e is especially suggestive of a charging transition and needs the same analysis.

B. Should address

5. The theory parameters do not match experiment, and the comparison is only qualitative.
   - Where: Fig. 2f, Fig. 4d,f and Supplementary Sec. II.
   - What is wrong: The simulations use Δ0 = 35 meV (Fig. 2f), 45 and 18 meV (Fig. 4d) and 30 meV (Fig. 4f). The chemical potential is μ = 12.25 meV, and J1 and J2 differ in sign and magnitude between figures (e.g., J1 = -15 meV versus +20 meV in Fig. 4d). None of these Δ0 values matches the measured gap of about 85 meV.
   - Why it matters: With J1, J2, Δ0 and μ all free for each panel, agreement with lineshapes is weak evidence.
   - What to do:
     - State which parameters are fixed by independent data (band structure from DFT or ARPES, gap from STS) and which are fitted.
     - Constrain Δ0 and μ from the measured clean-region spectrum.
     - Show that one parameter set, varying only the quantity claimed to change (tip potential, or local Δ), reproduces the full series.
     - State whether the model is one-dimensional, its band dispersions, and whether it is spin-singlet. The mapping of a Te vacancy onto intra-band and inter-band potentials should be justified microscopically.

6. Defect identification rests on topography alone.
   - Where: Figs. 2a,b, S1 and S2.
   - What is missing: Assigning the defects as surface Te vacancies on chains Te1 to Te4 relies on apparent morphology in decorated-tip images.
   - Why it matters: The paper's argument that 2δ depends on "defect configuration", and hence on coupling strength, hinges on these assignments.
   - What to do: Support the assignment with simulated STM images (DFT) of each vacancy type, and possibly of competing candidates (antisites, Pd or Ta vacancies, adsorbates). Explain why no Te5 vacancies are observed.

7. The statistical claim that defect types have distinct 2δ is not quantified.
   - Where: Fig. 2d.
   - What is missing: The histograms overlap substantially; VTe4 and VTe2 in particular differ by only 7 meV in 2δm, with widths of order 20 meV.
   - What to do:
     - Report the number of defects per type, the number of samples, cleaves and tips, and the Gaussian widths with uncertainties.
     - Apply a statistical test.
     - Address whether tip condition or set-point differences between datasets contribute to the spread, since concern 4 shows the peak energies depend on set-point. Spectra in Fig. S3 should be at a common set-point, or corrected for it.

8. The type-I/type-II regions and the "secondary pair breaking" interpretation are under-supported.
   - Where: Fig. 4a-d and Fig. S12.
   - What is missing:
     - The classification uses a single "characteristic normal-state" peak energy near +120 mV (Fig. 4b) whose origin is not identified.
     - The inference that type-II is more electron-doped, with stronger e-h imbalance, is not tested by any other measure, such as the local gap edges or the band-onset positions.
     - It is unclear whether the gap center actually lies below EF: in Fig. 1d, edges at about -35 and +50 mV place the center above EF.
     - The tanh model with x_c = 80 b0 (about 30 nm) and a local gap reduced at the impurity is a phenomenological input.
   - What to do:
     - Give gap-edge positions for both region types.
     - Show how many defects of each type were measured in each region.
     - Demonstrate a correlation between local doping and the lower-energy states across a field of view, for example by spatial maps of the characteristic-state energy alongside impurity-state energies.

9. Evidence on reproducibility across samples is missing.
   - Where: the whole paper.
   - What is missing: The paper does not say how many crystals, cleaves and tips the key observations (Figs. 2e, 2g, 3, 4e) come from.
   - What to do: State these numbers. Show that the tip-approach and temperature trends hold on more than one defect of each type and on more than one sample (Fig. S5 partly addresses this for tip approach).

10. The dimer evidence rests on single examples.
    - Where: Fig. S10, S11.
    - What is missing: Hybridization is shown for one dimer at 1.3 nm, and its absence for one at about 2 nm.
    - What to do: Show the splitting against separation and orientation (along b versus c) for several dimers. Compare it with the anisotropic decay lengths of Fig. 3g,h, which would make this a quantitative consistency test of the bound-state picture.

C. Minor

11. Describe the normalization used in Fig. 2e and Fig. 4e ("normalized impurity spectra"), since changing It at fixed Vb changes the set-point normalization.
12. State the lock-in modulation amplitude and the energy resolution in the main text. These matter for the linewidth arguments in Fig. 2e,g.
13. Fig. 1d is described as showing the gap from about -35 to +50 mV, but defining Δ = 85 meV from a hole "onset" and an electron "peak" mixes criteria. Use one consistent definition.
14. Fig. 3g,h: give the extraction method and uncertainties for the widths of 0.6 nm and 2.5 nm, and state whether they are tip-convolved.
15. Discuss the relation to ref. 41 (Kwon et al., YSR states of an exciton condensate) beyond a "Note added": what is the same, and what differs?
16. Wording:
    - "formulism" should be "formalism".
    - "predicated" should be "predicted" (p. 5).
    - "Yu-Yhiba-Rusinov" in ref. 37 should be "Yu-Shiba-Rusinov".
    - The abstract's "tip electric field" should be reconciled with the main text's treatment of the tip as a scattering potential.
17. The discussion of impurity selectivity (p. 5-6) says both magnetic and nonmagnetic impurities break excitonic pairs. Only nonmagnetic vacancies are studied, so state this as a theoretical expectation, not an observation.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Perform DFT calculations of the Te vacancy in a non-excitonic Ta2Pd3Te5 slab, and compare the in-gap level energies and orbital shapes with Fig. 2c and Fig. 3. Doing so would directly test the conventional-defect alternative.
2. Extend the impurity and clean-region spectroscopy to temperatures approaching the reported excitonic transition. Show that the in-gap states track the closing of the excitonic gap.
3. Separate electrostatic tip effects from scattering-potential effects: use bias set-points of both polarities, check for charging rings, and estimate tip-induced band bending.
4. Refit all simulations with a single, experimentally constrained parameter set (Δ0, μ and band parameters from the clean-region spectrum and ARPES). Report fitted and fixed parameters explicitly.
