Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports STM/STS of surface Te vacancies on cleaved Ta2Pd3Te5, a leading candidate excitonic insulator (EI). Each vacancy type produces a pair of in-gap resonances. Their energies depend on the vacancy configuration and shift with tunnelling current, and their spatial profiles are anisotropic. The authors interpret these as excitonic pair-breaking states, the EI analogue of Yu-Shiba-Rusinov/impurity bound states in superconductors. A second, lower-energy pair of states in electron-rich regions is attributed to a locally suppressed order parameter. Atomic-scale impurity spectroscopy that can single out excitonic order would be valuable to the field, since the excitonic nature of the gap in Ta2Pd3Te5 and related compounds is still debated. As presented, however, the evidence shows defect-bound in-gap states in a gapped quasi-1D material. It does not yet show that these states are specific to an excitonic condensate. A closely related study (Kwon et al., arXiv:2512.23724, ref. 41) also needs to be positioned more explicitly.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- High-quality atomic-resolution imaging with a decorated tip, which resolves the five surface Te chains (Te1 to Te5) and lets individual vacancies be assigned to specific chains (Fig. 1c, Fig. 2a,b).
- A statistical survey of many vacancies, with 2δ histograms that separate by chain site (Fig. 2d, Fig. S3), rather than reliance on a single defect.
- Rich phenomenology: a tip-current dependence (Fig. 2e, Fig. S5), a temperature dependence (Fig. 2g), anisotropic spatial maps with spectral-weight reversal outside the gap (Fig. 3d to f), dimer splitting (Figs. S10, S11) and a correlation with local electronic inhomogeneity (Fig. 4). Together these form a useful experimental dataset on defects in Ta2Pd3Te5.
- The question is timely, and a positive identification would be a new spectroscopic tool for EIs.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The mean-field model cannot tell an excitonic condensate from an ordinary hybridization (band) insulator, so it cannot support the pair-breaking assignment, and the concluding claim is circular.
   - In the two-band model of Supplementary Sec. II, Δ0 is a fixed parameter (Δ0 = 35 meV in Fig. 2f; 45 and 18 meV in Fig. 4d; a prescribed Δ(x) in Fig. 4f). With a fixed, non-self-consistent Δ, the mean-field EI Hamiltonian is formally identical to a non-interacting two-band insulator with interband hybridization Δ. A potential scatterer with intra-band (J1) and inter-band (J2) terms produces bound states in the gap of such a band insulator just as well.
   - So the reproduction of an asymmetric pair of in-gap peaks (Fig. 2f, Figs. S6 to S8) is not evidence that the gap is excitonic.
   - The Conclusion's statement that "the results in turn confirm that the probed insulating gap at impurity-free region arises from the excitonic pairing" therefore assumes what it sets out to show.
   - What the authors should do:
     - Identify, and test, an observable that differs between the two cases. One candidate is a self-consistent calculation in which the order parameter is locally suppressed around the vacancy, giving characteristic gap-edge depression and healing length, with a comparison of those features to the measured gap edges near the defect. Another is a dependence on the coupling sign or on the order-parameter phase that a fixed hybridization lacks.
     - Show explicitly what the same model gives for a band insulator with the same gap.
     - Failing this, reword the claims as "consistent with" rather than "demonstrate/confirm".

2. A conventional semiconductor defect level combined with tip-induced band bending or charging has not been ruled out, and several observations point towards it.
   - Te vacancies in a narrow-gap semiconductor are generically expected to create donor or acceptor levels. In STM, such a level often appears as resonances at both bias polarities: one from resonant tunnelling through the level, one from tip-induced ionisation or charging in a double-barrier geometry.
   - Their energies shift with tip height and setpoint because the tip-induced band bending changes with tip height.
   - This is exactly the behaviour in Fig. 2e: the peaks shift towards the gap edges and change intensity ratio as It rises at fixed Vb = 0.1 V.
   - The authors themselves call the required decrease of |J1,2| with tip approach "unexpected" (p. 4). The abstract also says the states are "tunable by tip electric field", which is the language of band bending, not of pair breaking.
   - The lower-energy states that appear only above about 25 nA (Fig. 4e) fit this picture too.
   - What the authors should do:
     - Separate the effects of field and tunnelling rate. Measure spectra at fixed tip height with different setpoint biases, and at fixed current with different setpoint biases.
     - Show whether the gap edges and coherence-like peak of the defect-free region shift by the same amount under the same It sequence; this is the band-bending reference.
     - Look for charging rings or bias-dependent contrast in dI/dV maps around vacancies, and give the extrapolated peak energies as It → 0.
     - Compare against a tip-induced band-bending estimate using the known gap, carrier density and tip work function.

3. The temperature dependence does not connect the subgap states to the excitonic order.
   - Fig. 2g covers only 1.6 to 20 K. The excitonic gap is about 85 to 100 meV, and the reported transition is well above 100 K, so over this range the order parameter should be essentially constant.
   - Yet the text says the accelerated decay of the subgap peaks occurs "alongside the thermal suppression of excitonic gap". No defect-free spectra at 20 K are shown to demonstrate any gap suppression.
   - The faster-than-thermal decay is attributed loosely to "local suppression of the excitonic gap by tip potential and/or the quantum pairing poison" (refs. 32, 33 concern Majorana/Andreev quasiparticle poisoning). That explanation is not specific to an EI. It would equally follow from phonon-assisted broadening of a localized defect level or from thermally activated charge fluctuations of a donor level.
   - What the authors should do:
     - Show the defect-free gap at each temperature in Fig. 2g.
     - Ideally, extend the measurement to temperatures where the gap is measurably reduced, and track the subgap energies against the local gap. A pair-breaking state should follow Δ(T), whereas a defect level need not.
     - At minimum, quantify the extra broadening and compare it with lock-in plus thermal broadening (see concern 14).

B. Should address

4. The "locked" spatial profiles of Ee and Eh are not a distinctive signature, and their analysis is under-specified.
   - On p. 5 the authors argue that because the dI/dV linecuts at 10 mV and −45 mV collapse onto the same curve (Fig. 3g,h), Ee and Eh are "locked as expected for pair-breaking subgap states". For impurity states in superconductors (ref. 19), however, the particle (u) and hole (v) components generically have different spatial profiles and different oscillation phases. A single localized defect level seen at two bias polarities (concern 2) would also give identical profiles.
   - It is not stated whether the curves in Fig. 3g,h were normalized before overlaying them. Fig. 3g also shows clear differences in the tails (beyond ±0.3 nm).
   - What the authors should do:
     - Show the calculated u and v (Ee and Eh) profiles from the model (Fig. S8) on the same axes as the data, and state the normalization.
     - Explain why identical profiles are expected here, when they are not in the superconducting analogue.

5. The model parameters are tuned panel by panel and do not match the measured gap, so the comparison is only qualitative.
   - The measured gap is Δ ≈ 85 meV (p. 2), but the simulation uses a "zero-temperature excitonic gap Δ0 = 35 meV" (Fig. 2f).
   - Fig. 4d uses Δ0 = 45 and 18 meV, Fig. 4f uses Δ0 = 30 meV, and μ = 12.25 meV.
   - J1 changes sign between Fig. 4d's higher-energy (J1 = −15 meV) and lower-energy (J1 = +20 meV) cases. This sign change sets the intensity asymmetry (Fig. S6) but has no physical justification.
   - The bands are not shown to represent Ta2Pd3Te5's quasi-1D, multi-orbital, spin-orbit-coupled band structure, including its proposed Dirac/topological character (refs. 6, 22).
   - What the authors should do:
     - Explain how Δ0 relates to the measured gap.
     - Fix the band parameters from ARPES/DFT.
     - Fit all vacancy types with one consistent parameter set, reporting which parameters are free.
     - Justify the J1 sign change physically.

6. The Te-vacancy identification rests on topography alone.
   - Vacancies are identified from topography with a cluster-decorated tip (Fig. 2a,b, Fig. S2), and appear as "elongated protrusions", which is unusual for a missing chalcogen atom.
   - No DFT-simulated STM images, formation-energy considerations or controlled defect creation/annealing are presented. Nor do the authors explain why no vacancies are seen on Te5.
   - Since the configuration dependence of 2δ (Fig. 2d) is central to the "coupling-tunable" argument, the chemical identity and site assignment matter.
   - What the authors should do: add simulated topographs for VTe1 to VTe4, including the defect-induced LDOS, and discuss other candidates such as antisites, substitutions and subsurface defects.

7. The configuration dependence of 2δ is not specific to pair breaking, and the measurement conditions behind the histograms are not given.
   - The text argues that the vacancy-dependent 2δ (Fig. 2d) "highlights their origin from the e–h pair-breaking states". Bound-state energies of any defect depend on its local configuration.
   - The four distributions also overlap strongly (for example VTe2 and VTe4).
   - Because peak energies depend on It (Fig. 2e), the histograms are meaningful only at a fixed setpoint and a comparable tip.
   - What the authors should do: state the setpoint, the number of vacancies per type, the number of tips and cleaves, and whether the histograms are corrected for setpoint.

8. The type-I/type-II classification and the high-current "triggered" lower-energy states need firmer support.
   - The regions are defined by a modest shift of a single normal-state feature near +120 to +130 meV (Fig. 4b, black and red arrows). Its identification as a "characteristic normal-state peak" whose energy measures e–h imbalance is not justified.
   - The lower-energy states require It ≳ 25 nA at 0.1 V (junction resistance ≲ 4 MΩ, up to 90 nA in Fig. 4e). At such currents, tip–sample forces, local heating and tip-induced band bending are substantial.
   - The Δ(x) = Δ0 tanh(x/xc) profile with xc = 80 b0 (about 30 nm) in Fig. 4f is put in by hand. It forces the gap to zero at the impurity, and is not derived.
   - What the authors should do:
     - Show the defect-free gap magnitude in type-I versus type-II regions; if the order parameter is locally reduced, the gap should be smaller.
     - Show the It dependence of a defect-free type-II spectrum up to 90 nA.
     - Justify the Δ(x) profile and its length scale, or derive it self-consistently.

9. The dimer result is generic.
   - A splitting into bonding/antibonding-like states for a 1.3 nm pair but not a 2 nm pair (Figs. S10, S11) is expected for any overlapping localized states (cf. ref. 36, Mn in GaAs). It therefore does not specifically probe "quantum pair-breaking states".
   - The text does not say along which crystal axis the dimer is separated. This matters given the 0.6 nm versus 2.5 nm anisotropy of Fig. 3g,h. It also does not say how many dimers were measured.
   - What the authors should do: give the orientation and statistics, and compare the measured splitting with a two-impurity calculation in the same model.

10. Several claims go beyond the data.
   - The abstract and introduction frame the work as revealing "how macroscopic coherence emerges from locally collapsed pairing" and "the critical behavior across excitonic condensation", but no data near the excitonic transition are presented.
   - Page 5–6 asserts that in EIs "both magnetic and nonmagnetic impurities show the pair-breaking effects, regardless of whether the pairing is spin-singlet or -triplet". Only nonmagnetic Te vacancies were measured.
   - What the authors should do: temper these statements, or present supporting evidence for them.

C. Minor

11. Gap definition.
   - The gap Δ = 85 meV is built from an electron-side peak (about 50 meV) and a hole-side "onset" (about −35 meV), and is called "under-estimated" compared with ARPES (about 100 meV at 20 K).
   - What the authors should do: use one consistent definition, give its uncertainty, and discuss surface versus bulk gaps.

12. Inconsistent VTe2 energies across figures.
   - Fig. 3 assigns Ee = 10 meV and Eh = −45 meV to VTe2, so 2δ ≈ 55 meV.
   - Fig. 2d gives a most probable 2δm = 69 meV for VTe2, and the VTe2 spectra in Fig. 2c,e show peaks at somewhat different energies.
   - What the authors should do: state the setpoint for each, and say whether these are the same defect.

13. Missing experimental parameters in the main text or captions.
   - Lock-in modulation amplitude, setpoints for every spectrum and map (Fig. 3d to f), the normalization used in Fig. 2e and Fig. 4e, and the number of samples, cleaves and tips are missing.
   - The tip-decoration procedure and its effect on spectra should be described, including whether spectra were taken with a normal or a decorated tip.

14. Thermal convolution in Fig. 2g.
   - The thermal convolution includes only the Fermi function.
   - What the authors should do: include the lock-in broadening, and state the effective electron temperature at 1.6 K.

15. Fig. 4a wording and labelling.
   - The caption says the dashed curve outlines regions with "relatively stronger e–h balance", whereas the text discusses imbalance. Please make these consistent and label type-I/type-II on the map itself.
   - The claim in the Fig. 4e caption that the lower-energy Ee "has merged into the Ee branch of higher-energy subgap states" should be supported by a fit.

16. Language and references.
   - Typos and wording: "formulism" (formalism), "predicated" (predicted), "Yu-Yhiba-Rusinov" (ref. 37), "those … tends", "a specific orbital".
   - Refs. 32 and 33 (Majorana quasiparticle poisoning) are not appropriate support for broadening in an EI.

17. Relation to the related study.
   - The "Note added" cites Kwon et al. (ref. 41). A short discussion of agreements and differences, for example in defect types, energies and interpretation, would help readers.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a discriminating test between excitonic pair breaking and an ordinary defect level in a hybridization gap. Candidates are a self-consistent order-parameter calculation that predicts local gap-edge suppression around the vacancy, together with a measurement of that suppression in the gap edges near the defect.
2. Carry out a full setpoint and tip-height study, including defect-free reference spectra under identical conditions, to quantify and remove tip-induced band bending before interpreting the It dependence.
3. Extend the temperature dependence to where the excitonic gap measurably changes, and plot subgap energies against the simultaneously measured local gap.
4. Fit all defect types and both regions with a single, physically constrained parameter set tied to the real band structure, and support the vacancy assignment with DFT-simulated STM images.
