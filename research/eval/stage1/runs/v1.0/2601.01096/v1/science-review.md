A. Must address

1. Nothing in the paper tells excitonic pair breaking apart from ordinary defect states in a hybridization-gapped or band insulator, so the Conclusion's argument is circular. The evidence offered for pair breaking appears in Figs. 2-4 and Supplementary Sec. II. It consists of a pair of in-gap peaks with electron-hole mixed character, energies that depend on the defect site, peaks that shift with tip-sample distance, a shared spatial envelope for Ee and Eh (Fig. 3g,h) and bonding/antibonding splitting in dimers (Figs. S10, S11). A non-interacting two-band insulator with a static hybridization of the same size would show every one of these. So would a narrow-gap semiconductor whose vacancy has donor/acceptor or charge-transition levels; there, Ee and Eh can be two charge-transition levels of one localized orbital. In the mean-field model of Supplementary Sec. II, Δ0 is a fixed, non-self-consistent parameter: Δ0 = 35 meV in Fig. 2f, 45 and 18 meV in Fig. 4d, and an imposed tanh profile Δ(x) in Fig. 4f. With Δ fixed, the EI Hamiltonian is formally identical to a two-band insulator with interband hybridization Δ. A potential scatterer with intra-band (J1) and inter-band (J2) terms produces bound states in the gap of such an insulator just as well. Reproducing an asymmetric pair of in-gap peaks (Fig. 2f, Figs. S6-S8) is therefore not evidence that the gap is excitonic. Whether the Ta2Pd3Te5 gap is excitonic or structural/phonon-driven is exactly the open question in refs. 6 and 21-23. Defect-bound states in a topological insulator (Ta2Pd3Te5 has been discussed as a quantum spin Hall and topological excitonic insulator, refs. 22, 25) are a further alternative. The Conclusion states that the results "confirm that the probed insulating gap at impurity-free region arises from the excitonic pairing". That assumes what it sets out to show, because the pair-breaking interpretation already presupposes an excitonic gap. The authors should identify at least one observable that the excitonic scenario predicts and a single-particle defect-level scenario does not, and test it. Options:
   - a self-consistent calculation in which the order parameter is suppressed around the vacancy, predicting a gap-edge depression and healing length that can be compared with dI/dV maps of the local gap edges near the defect;
   - a dependence on the coupling sign, the order-parameter phase or the electron-hole imbalance that a fixed hybridization lacks;
   - an explicit calculation in the same model for a band insulator with the same gap, showing which features it fails to reproduce;
   - a DFT supercell calculation of the Te vacancy in a non-excitonic Ta2Pd3Te5 slab, to see whether in-gap levels at the observed energies and with the observed orbital shapes arise without any excitonic order parameter;
   - the evolution of the in-gap states and the host gap on warming toward the excitonic transition, showing that the states track the closing gap (see concern 4);
   - a check of the predicted relation between the bound-state decay length and the excitonic coherence length ħv/Δ, against the measured widths of 0.6 nm and 2.5 nm.
   Without such a test, the title, abstract and conclusion should present pair breaking as one consistent interpretation ("consistent with"), not as a demonstration or confirmation.

2. The "subgap" states lie at or beyond the authors' own gap edges. In Fig. 1d the gap is defined as Δ ≈ 85 meV, from a hole-side onset near −35 mV to an electron-side coherence peak near +50 mV. Several numbers conflict with this. The most probable separation for VTe3 is 2δm = 104 meV, and the histograms extend to about 120 meV (Fig. 2d), more than the full gap. For VTe2, Eh is mapped at −45 meV (Fig. 3f, 3g,h) and appears near −45 mV in Fig. 2c, below the −35 mV hole onset. In Fig. 2c the defect spectra also show a hard, nearly zero-conductance region between the two resonances, with no remnant of the host's +50 mV peak or hole-side onset beyond them. That lineshape looks more like a locally shifted or renormalized gap with enhanced edges than like bound states inside an intact gap. Pair-breaking (YSR-type) states must sit at |E| < Δ, with the host coherence features still visible outside them, and this is the central premise of the title. If 2δ > Δ, then the gap definition is wrong, or the local gap near the defect differs, or the peaks are not true in-gap bound states. The authors should:
   - define the gap edges quantitatively (criterion, threshold, spatial averaging);
   - overlay defect and defect-free spectra on the same absolute scale over a wide bias range (±200 mV);
   - report the local gap measured just outside each defect, taking the reference spectrum next to each defect if local doping or gap inhomogeneity (Fig. 4) matters;
   - plot Ee and Eh, not only 2δ, against these edges for every defect in Fig. S3, showing that both resonances lie inside the local gap;
   - explain how a 104 meV separation fits into an 85 meV gap, or revise the gap definition.

3. Tip-induced band bending (TIBB), tip gating, voltage division and defect charging have not been ruled out as the origin of the "tunability", and several observations point towards them. The abstract says the energies are "continuously tunable by tip electric field", which is the language of band bending, not pair breaking. Fig. 2e changes the setpoint current at fixed Vb = 0.1 V up to 8 nA, and Fig. 4e up to 90 nA (junction resistance about 1 MΩ). This mainly changes tip height, and with it the local field, the TIBB, possible tip-induced charging of the defect, local heating and the mechanical tip-sample interaction. A Te vacancy in a narrow-gap, low-carrier-density semiconductor is generically expected to create a donor or acceptor level. In STM such a level often appears as resonances at both bias polarities: one from resonant tunnelling through the level, the other from tip-induced ionization or charging in a double-barrier (vacuum plus depletion) geometry. Both shift and broaden with tip height and setpoint, which is exactly the behaviour in Fig. 2e, where the peaks move towards the gap edges and change intensity ratio as It rises. The model instead treats the tip as a change of J1 and J2. It then requires |J1,2| to decrease on approach, which the authors call "unexpected" (p. 4) and resolve post hoc with a tip potential of opposite sign. The threshold appearance of the lower-energy states above about 25 nA (Fig. 4e) is especially suggestive of a charging transition. The authors should:
   - separate field and distance effects by measuring at fixed current with different setpoint biases (including both polarities) and at fixed tip height with different setpoint biases;
   - give I(z) curves or the apparent barrier height so that It can be converted into tip height;
   - show whether the gap edges and coherence-like peak of a defect-free region shift by the same amount under the same It sequence, as the band-bending reference (under TIBB they should; under a purely local pair-breaking mechanism they should not);
   - look for charging rings or bias- and height-dependent contrast in dI/dV maps around vacancies;
   - give the peak energies extrapolated to It → 0;
   - estimate the TIBB from the known gap, carrier density and tip work function, and show that the observed shifts cannot be reproduced by an electrostatic shift of a localized level;
   - analyse the ≈25 nA threshold in Fig. 4e as a possible charging event;
   - rephrase "tunable by tip electric field" to match what these controls show, and reconcile it with the main text's treatment of the tip as a scattering potential.

B. Should address

4. The temperature dependence (Fig. 2g) does not connect the subgap states to the excitonic order. The data cover only 1.6-20 K. The excitonic gap is about 85-100 meV and the reported order persists to roughly 10² K (refs. 5, 6), so over this range the order parameter should be essentially constant. Yet the text says the accelerated decay of the subgap peaks occurs "alongside the thermal suppression of excitonic gap", and no defect-free spectra at 20 K are shown to demonstrate any suppression. The faster-than-Fermi decay is attributed to "local suppression of the excitonic gap by tip potential and/or the quantum pairing poison". That explanation is not specific to an EI. The extra broadening could equally come from phonon-assisted or inelastic broadening of a localized defect level, thermally activated charge fluctuations of a donor level, a temperature-dependent tip or junction state or setpoint, thermal drift, or a tip temperature different from the sample temperature. The authors should show the defect-free gap edges at each temperature, measured with the same tip, alongside the impurity spectra. They should plot the fitted peak widths and energies against T with uncertainties, and quantify the extra broadening against lock-in, instrumental and thermal broadening (see concern 17). Ideally they should extend the measurements to temperatures where the gap is measurably reduced and track the subgap energies against the simultaneously measured local gap: a pair-breaking state should follow Δ(T), whereas a defect level need not. The statement that the states "shift toward the gap center" should be quantified, since it is not obvious in Fig. 2g.

5. The model parameters are tuned panel by panel and are not tied to the measured gap or band structure, so the comparison with the data is only qualitative. The measured gap is Δ ≈ 85 meV (p. 2), but the simulations use a "zero-temperature excitonic gap" Δ0 = 35 meV and μ = 12.25 meV (Fig. 2f), Δ0 = 45 and 18 meV (Fig. 4d) and Δ0 = 30 meV with x_c = 80 b0 ≈ 30 nm (Fig. 4f). J1 and J2 differ in sign and magnitude between figures. In particular, J1 changes from −15 meV for the higher-energy states to +20 meV for the lower-energy states in Fig. 4d. That sign change sets the intensity asymmetry (Fig. S6) but has no physical justification. With Δ0, μ, J1 and J2 free for each panel, agreement with peak positions and lineshapes is weak evidence. Nor is the model shown to represent Ta2Pd3Te5's quasi-1D, multi-orbital, spin-orbit-coupled band structure, including its proposed Dirac/topological character (refs. 6, 22). The authors should:
   - state which parameters are fixed by independent data (band structure from DFT or ARPES, gap and doping from STS) and which are fitted;
   - explain the factor of about 2 between Δ0 and the measured Δ;
   - justify the sign change of J1 between regions and the 30 nm healing length;
   - state whether the model is one-dimensional, give its band dispersions, say whether it is spin-singlet, and justify microscopically the mapping of a Te vacancy onto intra- and inter-band potentials;
   - fit all four vacancy types, both inhomogeneity regions and the tip-height series with one parameter set, with Δ0 and μ fixed from the adjacent defect-free spectra and only the quantity claimed to change (J1, J2, tip potential or local Δ) varied, and report which features the model fails to capture.

6. The claim that Ee and Eh are spatially "locked" (Fig. 3g,h) is not a distinctive signature, and the maps may contain setpoint artifacts. On p. 5 the authors argue that because the dI/dV linecuts at 10 mV and −45 mV collapse onto one curve, Ee and Eh are "locked as expected for pair-breaking subgap states". For impurity states in superconductors (ref. 19), however, the particle (u) and hole (v) components generically have different spatial profiles and oscillation phases. A single localized defect level seen at both bias polarities (concern 3) would also give identical profiles. Fig. 3g also shows clear differences in the tails beyond ±0.3 nm. The maps in Fig. 3d-f and the linecuts in Fig. 3g,h were presumably taken in constant-current mode at a fixed setpoint. With strong in-gap spectral weight at the defect, setpoint normalization alone can suppress the 60 meV map at the defect, producing the "spectral reversal" read as spectral-weight transfer, and can make the Ee and Eh profiles look alike. It is not stated whether the curves were normalized before being overlaid. The authors should state the setpoint and normalization, present the raw linecuts, and show constant-height maps or maps at different setpoint biases. They should plot the calculated u and v profiles from the model (Fig. S8) on the same axes as the data, and explain why identical profiles are expected here when they are not in the superconducting analogue.

7. The dependence of 2δ on vacancy type (Fig. 2d) is not specific to pair breaking and is not statistically quantified. The text says the vacancy-dependent 2δ "highlights their origin from the e–h pair-breaking states", but the bound-state energies of any defect depend on its local configuration. The distributions also overlap strongly: VTe4 and VTe2 differ by only 7 meV in 2δm, with widths of order 20 meV, so "statistically distinct ranges" needs a quantitative test. Because the peak energies depend on It (Fig. 2e), the histograms are meaningful only at a fixed setpoint and with a comparable tip. The authors should:
   - report the number of vacancies per type, the number of samples, cleaves and tips, and the Gaussian centres and widths with uncertainties;
   - apply a statistical test such as a two-sample test;
   - state the setpoint and confirm that the spectra in Fig. S3 are at a common setpoint or corrected for it, and address whether differences in tip condition or setpoint contribute to the spread;
   - say whether the spread within a class is linked to the local type-I/II environment of Fig. 4, which could produce the histogram widths;
   - give microscopic support for the claim that the ordering VTe2 → VTe4 → VTe1 → VTe3 reflects coupling strength, for example computed vacancy potentials or the local orbital weight on each chain.

8. The identification of the defects as surface Te vacancies on chains Te1-Te4 rests on topography alone. The assignment uses apparent morphology in cluster-decorated-tip images (Figs. 2a,b, S1, S2). The defects appear as "elongated protrusions", which is unusual for a missing chalcogen atom. No DFT-simulated STM images, formation-energy considerations or controlled defect creation or annealing are presented, and the authors do not explain why no Te5 vacancies are seen. The configuration dependence of 2δ (Fig. 2d) is central to the "coupling-tunable" argument, so the chemical identity and site assignment matter. The authors should add simulated topographs, including the defect-induced LDOS, for VTe1-VTe4. They should also discuss competing candidates (antisites, substitutions, interstitials, Pd or Ta vacancies, subsurface defects, adsorbates), and explain the absence of Te5 vacancies.

9. The type-I/type-II classification and the "secondary pair breaking" interpretation of the lower-energy states (Fig. 4, Fig. S12) are under-supported and have plausible non-excitonic origins. The regions are defined by a shift of about 20 meV in a single normal-state feature near +120 to +130 meV (Fig. 4b, black and red arrows). Neither the origin of this feature nor its use as a measure of e-h imbalance is justified, and no other measure, such as the local gap edges or band-onset positions, tests the inference that type-II is more electron-doped. Read uniquely, it could equally reflect a change in local band bending. It is also unclear whether the gap centre lies below EF at all: the edges in Fig. 1d at about −35 and +50 mV place it above EF. The lower-energy states appear only above a threshold current of about 25 nA at 0.1 V (up to 90 nA in Fig. 4e, junction resistance ≲4 MΩ), and they have a low "surviving temperature" (Fig. S12). Both are typical of tip-induced charging of a defect level, which gives a sharp, threshold-like peak and a characteristic ring in dI/dV maps whose radius depends on bias and tip height. At these currents tip-sample forces, local heating and TIBB are also substantial. The profile Δ(x) = Δ0 tanh(x/x_c) with x_c = 80 b0 (about 30 nm) in Fig. 4f is put in by hand and forces the gap to zero at the impurity. The authors should:
   - report the local gap magnitude, gap edges and gap centre in type-I and type-II regions from many spectra (if the order parameter is locally reduced, the gap should be smaller);
   - show the It dependence of a defect-free type-II spectrum up to 90 nA;
   - provide bias- and height-dependent dI/dV maps around a type-II defect to test for charging rings;
   - give statistics on how many defects of each type were measured in each region and how many type-II defects show the lower-energy states;
   - demonstrate a correlation between local doping and the lower-energy states across a field of view, for example with spatial maps of the characteristic-state energy alongside the impurity-state energies;
   - justify the Δ(x) profile and its length scale, or derive it self-consistently.

10. The dimer evidence is generic and rests on single examples. Hybridization is inferred from one dimer at 1.3 nm (Fig. S10), and its absence from one at about 2 nm (Fig. S11). Bonding/antibonding splitting is expected for any overlapping localized states (cf. ref. 36, Mn in GaAs), so it does not specifically probe "quantum pair-breaking states". The text does not say along which crystal axis each dimer is separated. That matters given the 0.6 nm versus 2.5 nm anisotropy in Fig. 3g,h, and in a quasi-1D material the coupling should depend systematically on both separation and orientation. The authors should measure several dimers at several separations and orientations (along b and along c) and report the splitting against distance. They should compare it with the anisotropic decay lengths of Fig. 3g,h and with a two-impurity calculation in the same model.

12. The non-magnetic character of the impurity is assumed, not tested. The model assumes a non-magnetic impurity and a singlet EI, but a Te vacancy may carry a local moment depending on its charge state. Spectra in an applied magnetic field (a Zeeman splitting or shift of Ee and Eh) would test this assumption and would bear directly on the singlet-versus-triplet question the authors raise. If no field is available, the authors should at least discuss the expected charge and spin state of the vacancy.
