A. Must address

1. No measurement in the paper probes Cooper pairs or vortices, yet the headline claim depends on them. The abstract, p. 5 and the Conclusion claim preformed, localized Cooper pairs and mobile vortices up to about 220 K in sample 4 and about 183 K in sample 5. The only support is the functional form of R(T) and the sign of β'. The supporting diamagnetism (p. 5) is neither shown nor quantified for these samples. It is taken from another sample set (Ref. [20], onset about 140 K, which is not "close" to 220 K) and from Ref. [27], which has been retracted and whose 220 K diamagnetism Ref. [28] attributes to Joule heating. Other mechanisms give ρ ~ −ln T. These include Kondo-like or magnetic scattering, which is plausible given the ferromagnetic transition near 180 K reported in Ref. [20], electron-electron interaction (Altshuler-Aronov) corrections, and parallel conduction through secondary phases. The authors should add at least one probe that responds specifically to pairing, measured on the same flakes (samples 4 and 5, and an insulating flake such as sample 2). The cheapest is magnetoresistance in perpendicular and parallel field across 100-300 K, comparing the 100-220 K window with the 220-300 K window. Fermionic weak localization gives negative MR of Hikami-Larkin-Nagaoka form, with a field scale set by the dephasing length. Kondo or magnetic scattering gives isotropic negative MR. Localized pairs and phase fluctuations give positive, orbital MR with a field scale tied to 2e. Other useful tests are a Nernst signal, diamagnetic susceptibility of the same flakes with its temperature dependence, the field dependence of the "vertical flow" temperature, and I-V linearity in the putative Bose-glass window. Without such data, the 220 K Cooper-pair statement should be withdrawn, or reworded as "consistent with" rather than evidence of bosonic localization, and the diamagnetism discussion should be softened or backed by nanoflake magnetization data with sample details.

2. The "vertical flow" and the sign of β' follow mechanically from the resistance maximum, so they do not identify bosons independently. From Eqs. (1)-(4), β_EXP = −(2/p) d ln g/d ln T is computed from the same R(T) curves, and β' < 0 means only that the resistance rise slows down on a ln T scale. In samples 4 and 5, R(T) has a maximum near 80-110 K (Fig. 1c, Fig. 3). There d ln g/d ln T passes through zero, β changes sign and ln|β| diverges, which produces the vertical rise at nearly fixed ln g in Fig. 5(b) and Fig. 6(a) whatever the underlying physics. An ordinary fermionic picture, with weak localization and electron-electron interaction corrections plus Aslamazov-Larkin/Maki-Thompson fluctuation conductivity above a transition near 80-100 K, gives the same upturn and the same sign of β'. So does a crossover between two conduction channels. Eqs. (3)-(4) give opposite signs of β' only if the data follow one of the two pure log forms exactly, which assumes the conclusion. The MBE samples 6 and 7 never superconduct yet are also assigned β' < 0. Their β hardly varies with T in Fig. 6(a), and their arrow in Fig. 5(b) points left rather than up; saturation or percolation would also slow their resistance rise. The scaling L² = D T^(−p) with p = 1 fixed (p. 7) is not justified at these temperatures. The authors should fit R(T) of samples 4 and 5 with a fermionic WL/EEI background plus 2D fluctuation conductivity, stating the fitted Tc and normal-state background, and show quantitatively that this fails where the bosonic form succeeds, or that β' < 0 survives the subtraction. They should show that the vertical flow begins well above the resistance maximum, and show a control with a resistance maximum of non-superconducting origin (for example a magnetic or CaRuO3-type anomaly) in which it is absent. They should also show how the results change with p (for example p = 1, 2, 3) and explain the MBE behaviour separately.

3. The temperature windows are too narrow to tell ρ ~ ln(1/T) from σ ~ ln T, and quantum-interference physics at 100-300 K is not justified. The boson/fermion assignment rests on Fig. 3(a,b). For sample 4 the Bose-glass window is 101-220 K (ln T 4.6-5.4, Δln T about 0.8, a factor of about 2.2 in T), over which R changes by about 50%. For sample 5 it is 105-183 K (Δln T about 0.55, a factor of about 1.7), over which R changes by about 25% and the data scatter visibly. The "Fermi glass" windows are narrower still: about 220-300 K (Δln T about 0.3) for samples 4 and 5, and about 210-299 K (a factor of about 1.35) for samples 1-3 (Fig. 2b). For such small fractional changes, R linear in ln(1/T) and G linear in ln T differ only at second order, and the lower panel of Fig. 3(a) shows G versus ln T for sample 4 is also close to linear over much of the window. The window boundaries appear to be chosen by eye where one fit departs from the other, and the lower end for sample 4 (101 K) sits next to the resistance maximum, so the preference for ln(1/T) may reflect the start of the downturn. Physically, weak localization at 100-300 K needs a phase-coherence length longer than the mean free path, while phonon scattering dominates in that range and the dephasing length is expected to be comparable to microscopic lengths. Samples 4 and 5 also have R□/layer of 30-125 kΩ, i.e. g = (ħ/e²)/R of about 0.03-0.15 per layer, well outside the weak-localization regime (g ≫ 1, k_F ℓ ≫ 1) assumed by Das-Doniach and by the −1/g branch of β_VW. The authors should report residuals or reduced χ² for R ~ ln(1/T), G ~ ln T, activated, VRH and power-law forms over the same windows, with and without a phonon or background term; show the sensitivity to the window endpoints and how they were chosen; show that the preferred form holds over at least a decade in T or is robust to moving the window; and estimate k_F ℓ, the mean free path and the dephasing length for each sample to justify a quantum-interference description at these temperatures.

4. The phase and structure of the measured flakes are not established, and the "relieved RuO6 distortion" mechanism is not measured. The Methods say Ca2RuO4 and CaRuO3 were told apart only "by observing the crystal shape using a scanning electron microscope". The c-axis value of 12.278 Å attributed to "nanoscale single crystals" appears to come from powder XRD (Rigaku Miniflex) on the parent material, not on the measured flakes. CaRuO3 is a metal with resistivity of order 10⁻⁴-10⁻³ Ω cm, the same range as the nanofilm resistivities in Fig. 1(a,b) and 3-4 orders below bulk Ca2RuO4. Other Ruddlesden-Popper intergrowths (e.g. Ca3Ru2O7), the metallic L phase, Ru-rich inclusions (Ru metal, RuO2), Ca-deficient surface layers from sonication, or oxygen non-stoichiometry (Ca2RuO4+δ) would each lower the resistivity by orders of magnitude without any change in correlation or distortion, and Ru inclusions could also give inhomogeneous or filamentary superconducting-like drops. The statements that impurity-induced disorder "cannot explain this phenomenon", that "the distortion in RuO6 octahedra is eliminated" (abstract, pp. 4 and 6), and that "thinning does not introduce disorder" (Conclusion) are therefore assertions, not results. The authors should characterize the measured devices or sister flakes from the same batch: Raman (which separates the S and L phases of Ca2RuO4 from CaRuO3), TEM or electron diffraction giving in-plane and c-axis lattice parameters and octahedral tilt and rotation, and EDX/EELS stoichiometry, together with a resistivity comparison against CaRuO3 reference values. Ideally the structural data should be temperature dependent.

6. The two "quantum critical" resistances come from finite-temperature data with no tuning parameter, separatrix or scaling, and the β_c-to-resistance conversion is not given. A quantum critical point needs a continuous tuning parameter and a separatrix: a temperature-independent R at R_c and collapse of R(T, x) onto F(|x − x_c|/T^(1/zν)). In Fig. 6(a), β_c = −1.6 is a horizontal line drawn between groups of different samples at 100-250 K, not a crossing point, and samples 6 and 7 near −1 to −1.5 at low T overlap it. The text says the resistance is "estimated from g_c = −1.6", conflating β_c with g (a positive dimensionless conductance), and the conversion to 65.7 kΩ is not shown. With g = (ħ/e²)σ, R = 65.7 kΩ corresponds to g of about 0.063 and ln g of about −2.8. Eq. (1) uses ħ/e² while the critical values are quoted in units of h/e² and h/4e², so factors of 2π must be tracked. The Fig. 6(a) caption and abstract call β_c the Bose glass-Mott insulator boundary, while the text calls it Bose glass-Fermi glass. In Fig. 6(b), 65.7 kΩ is the low-temperature value of the 5000 nA curve and 16.5 kΩ sits near the 150 nA curves; bias current is not a thermodynamic tuning parameter and is not shown to produce a separatrix, the curves at the quoted values are not flat in T, and flat low-T curves may be heating or depinning plateaus (see concern 8). Numerically, 65.7/25.8 ≈ 2.55 and 16.5/6.45 ≈ 2.56, while (5/2)h/e² = 64.5 kΩ and (5/2)h/4e² = 16.1 kΩ, so the values are closer to Fisher's 8/π than to 5/2, and the Pfaffian ν = 5/2 analogy (p. 8) has no physical link to this system. The authors should state the explicit relation from β_c to R_c; settle which transition β_c marks; derive β_c from a crossing or scaling analysis in which some sample or tuning parameter sits at β_c with dβ/dT ≈ 0; show a separatrix and collapse against a continuous tuning parameter (thickness series, gate or magnetic field), with the exponent product zν; and quote R_c with uncertainties that include the normalization in concern 5. Otherwise the language of "two quantum critical points" (abstract, p. 8) is too strong, and the 8/π and Pfaffian comparisons should be removed or framed as speculation.

15. The Bose-glass and Mott terminology departs from standard usage in ways that affect interpretation. In the Fisher framework the Bose glass is an insulating, compressible T = 0 phase with zero conductivity. The abstract's "absence of global phase coherence yet finite conductivity" and "bosonic order" follow the Das-Doniach usage of weakly localized bosons; a finite-conductivity bosonic state is usually called a Bose or anomalous metal. A phase entered at 220 K and left at about 100 K on cooling is a finite-temperature crossover, not a ground state, yet the boundaries are called quantum critical points, and it is unclear what the Bose glass in Fig. 4(a) would be at T = 0. "Mott insulator" in boson SIT language also differs from the fermionic Mott insulator of Ca2RuO4. The authors should state which definitions they use, what experimental property distinguishes the phases, and separate finite-temperature crossovers from ground-state claims in Fig. 4(a).
