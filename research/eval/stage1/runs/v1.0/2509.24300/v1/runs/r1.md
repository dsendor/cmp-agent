Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports transport on exfoliated/dispersed Ca2RuO4 nanoscale crystals (samples 1-5, 8) and MBE films on LaAlO3 (samples 6, 7). It argues that a regime where resistance follows ρ ~ ln(1/T), and a "vertical flow" in the β-function, show a Bose glass of localized Cooper pairs that sets in near 220 K. It also claims two critical sheet resistances, (5/2)h/e² and (5/2)h/4e², and says that thinning tunes electronic correlation by relieving RuO6 octahedral distortion rather than by adding disorder. If established, preformed, localized Cooper pairs at 220 K in a ruthenate would be a striking result. However, the evidence is entirely the functional form of R(T) over narrow temperature windows and quantities derived from those same curves. No measurement here probes pairing, vortices or structure directly. Methodologically, the work extends the authors' earlier β-function analysis of cuprate and Pb films (Ref. [11]) to a new material.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The low resistivity of the nanoscale crystals (3-4 orders of magnitude below bulk) and the resistance drops in samples 4 and 5 (Fig. 1) are intriguing. They build on the authors' earlier report of superconductivity in Ca2RuO4 nanofilms (Ref. [20]).
- Nanoscale crystals and MBE films are compared side by side, and the resulting phase diagram (Fig. 4) is organized by a single empirical parameter. This is a compact way to present a large set of R(T) curves.
- The β-function framework (Eqs. 1-4) is stated explicitly, so the sign criterion for β' can be checked and reproduced.
- The bias-current dependence of sample 4 (Fig. 6b) is a rich data set in its own right.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. Internal inconsistency in the sheet resistance of sample 4, which sets both critical resistances. In Fig. 3(a), R□/layer of sample 4 peaks at about 125 kΩ near 80-90 K and is about 60 kΩ near 290 K. The G□/layer panel agrees (about 0.8 × 10⁻⁵ Ω⁻¹ at the minimum). The same value follows from Fig. 1 (ρ ≈ 7 × 10⁻³ Ω cm, 10 nm, roughly 16 layers). In Fig. 6(b), the same sample 4 peaks at only about 41 kΩ in R□/layer (left axis). This is a factor of about 3 lower, and it is exactly the axis from which R^SC-BG = 16.5 kΩ and R^BG-FG = 65.7 kΩ are read. Unless Fig. 6(b) is from a different cooldown, contact configuration or normalization, which is not stated, the two "quantum resistances" cannot be taken at face value. The authors should state how R□/layer is computed in each figure (geometry factor, thickness, interlayer spacing), reconcile Fig. 3(a) and Fig. 6(b), and propagate the thickness (tilted-SEM) and geometry uncertainties into error bars on both critical resistances. The agreement with 5/2 × h/e² and 5/2 × h/4e² is quoted to about 2%. That precision is only meaningful if the normalization uncertainty is smaller, which seems unlikely for flake devices where thickness is measured by tilted SEM.

2. Telling ρ ~ ln(1/T) from σ ~ ln T is not quantitatively supported over the windows used. The boson/fermion assignment for samples 4 and 5 rests on Fig. 3(a,b). The ρ fits span 101-220 K (ln T 4.6-5.4) and 105-183 K (ln T 4.65-5.2), and over these ranges R changes by about 50% and 25%. For small fractional changes, ρ linear in ln(1/T) and σ linear in ln T are nearly the same function, and the distinction reduces to a slight curvature. For samples 1-3 the "Fermi glass" σ ~ ln T window is even narrower (roughly 210-300 K, a factor of 1.4 in T; Fig. 2b). The authors should give quantitative model comparisons in each window: residuals, reduced χ², and sensitivity to where the window ends are placed. They should also show that the preference for ρ ~ ln(1/T) does not simply reflect the start of the downturn toward the resistance maximum at ~80-100 K. As it stands, the window end for sample 4 (101 K) sits next to the maximum.

3. The "vertical flow" and the β' sign criterion do not uniquely identify bosons. From Eqs. (1)-(4), β' < 0 means only that |β| shrinks as T falls, i.e. the resistance rise slows down on a ln T scale. For samples 4 and 5 this is unavoidable as the resistance approaches its maximum (Fig. 1c, Fig. 3). There β passes through zero, ln|β| diverges, and the "vertical" rise in Fig. 5(b) and Fig. 6(a) follows. The ordinary fermionic explanation is weak localization plus electron-electron interaction corrections plus Aslamazov-Larkin/Maki-Thompson fluctuation conductivity above a superconducting transition near 80-100 K. That would produce the same upturn in β and the same sign of β'. Samples 6 and 7 likewise show a slowing resistance rise, which saturation or percolation would also produce. The authors should fit the R(T) of samples 4 and 5 with a fermionic WL/EEI background plus 2D fluctuation conductivity, and show quantitatively that this fails where the bosonic form succeeds. Without that comparison, Figs. 5 and 6 restate the R(T) curvature rather than giving independent evidence of localized pairs.

4. The headline claim of Cooper pairs at 220 K has no direct pairing or vortex evidence. The abstract asserts preformed, localized Cooper pairs up to about 220 K and mobile vortices dual to them. No measurement in the paper probes pairs or vortices. The cited diamagnetism (onset about 140 K in Ref. [20], about 220 K in the retracted Ref. [27]) is not shown for these samples and does not match the proposed 220 K / 183 K onsets. The paper needs at least one probe that responds specifically to pairing and vortices, measured on the same samples: magnetoresistance in perpendicular and parallel fields across 100-250 K, a Nernst signal for vortex motion, or magnetization or tunneling-gap data. Magnetoresistance is the cheapest. Fermionic weak localization gives negative MR with a characteristic field scale set by the dephasing length. Localized pairs and phase fluctuations give positive MR whose field scale is tied to 2e. If none of these is available, the claims should be reworded as consistent with, not evidence of, bosonic localization.

5. The structure and phase of the measured flakes are not established, and the "relieved octahedral distortion" mechanism is not measured. The flakes are told apart from CaRuO3 by crystal shape in SEM (Methods). The c-axis value of 12.278 Å is attributed to "nanoscale single crystals", but it appears to come from XRD on the source crystals, not on the measured flakes. CaRuO3, other Ruddlesden-Popper intergrowths (e.g. Ca3Ru2O7), the metallic L phase, or excess oxygen (Ca2RuO4+δ) would each lower the resistivity by orders of magnitude without any change in "correlation" or distortion. The ρ ~ 10⁻³ Ω cm of samples 4 and 5 lies in a range that such contaminants could produce. The statements that impurity-induced disorder "cannot explain this phenomenon" and that thinning relieves the RuO6 distortion (abstract, p. 4, p. 6, Conclusion) therefore have no direct support. The authors should provide flake-level characterization of the measured or sister devices: Raman (which separates the S/L phases and CaRuO3), TEM/electron diffraction for lattice parameters and octahedral tilts, and EDX/EELS for stoichiometry. Ideally they should also show the temperature dependence of the structure, to test whether a structural transition lies near 220 K.

6. Thickness does not order the phases, so the "thickness-tuned" framing and the disorder-versus-correlation conclusion are unsupported. Per the Fig. 1(a) legend, sample 5 (30 nm) is superconducting/Bose glass, while samples 1 and 2 (25 nm) and sample 3 (35 nm) are Mott insulators. The MBE films (50 nm), with a shorter c axis (supposedly more distortion), also show a Bose glass, while 25-35 nm flakes do not. This contradicts the narrative that thinning removes distortion and produces the Bose glass. The paper then switches to |dρ/dT| = |ρ(120 K) − ρ(280 K)|/ΔT as the tuning parameter (Fig. 4). But that quantity is computed from the same R(T) curves that define the phases, so the phase diagram is partly circular. The authors should either show an independently measured control parameter (thickness, c axis or octahedral tilt for each sample) that orders the phases, or retitle Fig. 1 and rephrase the conclusions as correlations within one set of R(T) curves.

B. Should address

7. Weak localization at 100-300 K with R□/layer ≫ h/e². Samples 4 and 5 have R□/layer of 30-125 kΩ, i.e. g = (ħ/e²)/R of about 0.03-0.15 per layer. That is well outside the weak-localization regime (g ≫ 1, k_F ℓ ≫ 1) assumed by Das-Doniach and by the "−1/g" branch of β_VW. Phase coherence over the relevant lengths at 100-220 K also needs justification. Separately, the ln g values plotted in Fig. 5(b) for sample 4 (about 0) and sample 5 (about −1 and 0) do not match ln g ≈ −3.2 and −2.2 implied by Fig. 3. The authors should define exactly what g is in Fig. 5 (per layer, per film, which normalization) and estimate k_F ℓ and the dephasing length for each sample.

8. The choice of p = 1 and the "universal curve" in Fig. 5(a). β_EXP scales as 2/p, and p is fixed at 1 without justification. The collapse onto β_VW in the strongly localized regime is also nearly automatic. For any VRH or activated law ln g ≈ −(T0/T)^x, one finds β ≈ (2x/p) ln g up to constants, so the slope is set by the hopping exponent rather than by universality. The bulk Nakatsuji data are three-dimensional, yet they are placed on a curve whose weak-localization branch is two-dimensional. The authors should show how the collapse and β_c change with p (e.g. p = 1, 2, 3). They should also explain why bulk 3D data belong on the same curve.

9. How β_c = −1.6 maps to 65.7 kΩ, and what it means. The text says "the quantum sheet resistance estimated from g_c = −1.6", which conflates β_c and g_c. The conversion from β_c to a resistance is not given. In Fig. 6(a), β_c is a horizontal line drawn between two groups of samples, not a temperature-independent separatrix of the kind that defines a quantum critical point. The Fig. 6(a) caption calls it the Bose glass-Mott insulator boundary, while the text calls it Bose glass-Fermi glass. The authors should give the explicit relation used, show that some sample or tuning parameter actually sits at β_c with dβ/dT ≈ 0, and settle which transition it is. Without a continuous tuning parameter and a scaling collapse, the language of "two quantum critical points" (abstract, p. 8) is too strong.

10. Bias-current dependence, Joule heating and non-ohmic transport. Ca2RuO4 is notoriously sensitive to current: current-driven metallization and Joule-heating artifacts (Refs. [19, 20, 27, 28]). Fig. 6(b) shows that R(T) of sample 4 changes drastically between 1 nA and 5 μA. The excitation current used for Figs. 1-3 and 5 is not stated. The 65.7 kΩ "critical resistance" is taken from the 5000 nA curve, which at 5 μA in a ~10 nm flake may involve significant self-heating. Reading a quantum critical resistance from a current-driven, possibly heated state needs justification. The authors should state the excitation current for every data set, show I-V linearity (or R(T) at two currents) in the Bose glass window, and estimate dissipated power and temperature rise for the 5 μA data.

11. The proximity of the 220 K feature to a structural or metal-insulator transition. In samples 1-3 the "Fermi glass to Mott insulator" crossover (224, 233, 208 K) falls at the same temperature as the "Fermi glass to Bose glass" crossover in sample 4 (about 220 K). The natural reading is a common, suppressed version of the bulk 357 K structural/MIT transition, broadened in the flakes. Its manifestation in transport could differ between more- and less-conducting samples without any bosons being involved. Temperature-dependent Raman or XRD on flakes, or at least a discussion of why the 220 K scale is not structural, is needed.

12. Superconductivity in samples 4 and 5 and its use as the "SC" phase. Fig. 1 shows resistance drops but no zero resistance down to the lowest temperature. The text concedes that the films "show finite resistance". Field dependence of the drop, I-V characteristics and a Meissner signal on these samples are not presented here. Since T^SC-BG and R^SC-BG rely on these being superconducting, the authors should include R(T) in magnetic field and critical-current data for samples 4 and 5, or clearly reference which of these devices were characterized in Ref. [20].

13. Mott insulator assignment and 2D VRH for samples 1-3. Samples 1-3 rise by only about one order of magnitude between 300 K and 20 K (Fig. 2a), unlike the bulk Mott state. The 2D VRH exponent is asserted for 25-35 nm crystals (40-60 RuO2 layers) without comparing to x = 1/4 or 1/2 or to activation. The authors should compare the exponents quantitatively (for example with a Zabrodskii analysis, d ln W/d ln T) and justify 2D hopping in a 40-60-layer crystal.

14. The pressure data and the AL fit in Fig. S2 and Fig. 4(b). Fig. S2 reinterprets the ferromagnetic-metal resistance drop of Ref. [19] as superconductivity. It uses a 2D Aslamazov-Larkin form with T_c0 fixed at 12 K and R_N = 44 μΩ cm. That is a resistivity, not a sheet resistance, so the prefactor e²R_N/16ħ is dimensionally inconsistent as written. The fit also departs from the data near T_c0. The residual resistivity (about 1-2 μΩ cm) is not zero, and a ferromagnetic metallic transition gives a similar drop. This reinterpretation of another group's data needs either a dimensionally consistent 3D fit with free parameters and a fermionic comparison, or removal. It should not feed the "SC" region drawn for the bulk in Fig. 4(b).

C. Minor

15. Sample bookkeeping. Sample 9 appears in Fig. 4(a) with no description. Sample 8 (300 nm) appears in Figs. 1 and 5 but is not discussed. Please give a table with thickness, geometry, growth method, contact configuration, excitation current and the extracted temperatures for every sample. Also state whether any result was reproduced after thermal cycling.

16. Sample 6 versus sample 7 temperatures. The text gives Bose glass onsets of 67 K (sample 6) and 81 K (sample 7). Fig. 3(c) (sample 7) shows the Bose glass window ending at ln T ≈ 4.2 (about 67 K), and Fig. S1 (sample 6) at about ln T 4.25 (about 70 K). The labels may be swapped. Please check.

17. Text-figure mismatches. R^SC-BG is 16.8 kΩ in the text but 16.5 kΩ in Fig. 6(b). The Fig. 3 caption says sample 5 fits "R ~ ln T" where ln(1/T) is meant. In Fig. 6(b), the relation between the left (R□/layer) and right (R) axes is not given.

18. The definition of dρ/dT (p. 6, Fig. 1b) as (ρ(120 K) − ρ(280 K))/dT is notationally a finite difference, and its sign and denominator should be stated explicitly. Because it uses absolute ρ, it inherits all geometric uncertainties. A normalized measure such as ln[ρ(120 K)/ρ(280 K)] would compare samples more robustly.

19. The phase boundaries in Fig. 4(a) appear hand-drawn through about 15 points. Please indicate this and add error bars on the transition temperatures (from the choice of fit window).

20. The comparison with the factor 8/π of Fisher et al. and with the ν = 5/2 Pfaffian (p. 8) is numerological. It should either be removed or framed as speculation, especially given concern 1.

21. Typos and wording: "Supplementaty", "more assertive" distortion (p. 9; presumably "more pronounced"), "fermi glass", and "the β-function scaling analysis can be applied to Ca2RuO4, such as copper oxides" (p. 7).

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add magnetoresistance (perpendicular and parallel field, 2-250 K) on samples 4 and 5 and on a Mott-like sample such as sample 2. The sign and field scale of the MR in the 100-220 K window would directly test fermionic versus bosonic localization at little experimental cost.
2. Replace the qualitative fit comparisons with a global quantitative analysis: fermionic WL/EEI plus fluctuation conductivity versus the bosonic ρ ~ ln(1/T) form, with χ² and window-sensitivity tests. Report all extracted temperatures and resistances with uncertainties.
3. Characterize the measured flakes structurally (Raman, TEM/EDX, ideally temperature-dependent) so that the distortion-relief mechanism and the exclusion of CaRuO3 or other phases rest on data from the same samples.
4. Consider reframing the paper around what the transport data firmly show: anomalously low-resistivity Ca2RuO4 nanocrystals with a crossover near 220 K and a resistance maximum preceding a partial superconducting drop. Present the Bose glass interpretation as one candidate explanation until a pairing-specific probe confirms it.
