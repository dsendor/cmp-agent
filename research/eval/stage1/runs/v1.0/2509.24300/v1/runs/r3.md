Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports transport on exfoliated (sonication-dispersed) Ca2RuO4 nanoflakes and MBE films, and claims a Bose glass (weakly localized Cooper pairs) from about 220 K, two quantum critical resistances (65.7 kOhm ~ (5/2)h/e^2 and 16.5-16.8 kOhm ~ (5/2)h/4e^2), and a phase diagram in which thinning tunes Ca2RuO4 from Mott insulator through Bose glass to a high-temperature superconductor. If true, preformed pairs near 220 K in a ruthenate would be a major result. However, the evidence is almost entirely the functional form of R(T) over narrow temperature windows and a beta-function construction derived from the same curves. The pairing claim has no independent test: no magnetoresistance, magnetization, Nernst signal or spectroscopy on these samples.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- Nanoscale Ca2RuO4 flakes have room-temperature resistivity 3-4 orders of magnitude below bulk (Fig. 1a). Together with the authors' earlier report of a superconducting-like drop (Ref. 20), this is an unusual and potentially interesting material system.
- The data cover several sample types (nanoflakes of different thickness, MBE films, a 300 nm flake) over a wide temperature range. The paper also collects literature Ca2RuO4 data into one localization-strength picture (Fig. 4b), which offers a phenomenological organizing parameter.
- The beta-function and the sign of its temperature derivative (Eqs. 2-4) give a compact, reproducible way to present the data that other groups could apply.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. Phase identity of the measured flakes is not established. The Methods say Ca2RuO4 and CaRuO3 were told apart "by observing the crystal shape using a scanning electron microscope", and the c-axis value 12.278 Å for "nanoscale single crystals" appears to come from powder XRD (Rigaku Miniflex) of the parent material, not from the measured flakes. CaRuO3 is a metal with resistivity of order 10^-4-10^-3 Ohm cm. That is the same range as the "nanofilm" resistivities in Fig. 1(a,b) and 3-4 orders below bulk Ca2RuO4. Ru-rich inclusions (Ru metal, RuO2), Ca-deficient surface layers from sonication, or oxygen nonstoichiometry would also produce low, weakly temperature-dependent resistivity and, for Ru inclusions, superconducting-like drops. The headline claim, that thinning relieves the RuO6 distortion, lowers the resistivity and induces Bose-glass and superconducting phases, depends on the conducting channel being Ca2RuO4. The authors should show phase and structure on the measured devices or sister flakes from the same batch: EDX or EELS stoichiometry, Raman (which separates the S and L phases of Ca2RuO4 from CaRuO3), and TEM or electron diffraction giving the in-plane and c-axis lattice parameters and octahedral tilt and rotation. Without these, the statements "the distortion in RuO6 octahedra is eliminated" (p. 6) and "thinning does not introduce disorder" (Conclusion) are assertions, not results.

2. No independent evidence for Cooper pairs at 100-220 K. The only evidence that the carriers above Tc^onset are bosons is that R is linear in ln(1/T) rather than G being linear in ln T (Fig. 3a,b). The supporting diamagnetism is taken from another sample set (Ref. 20) at about 140 K, and from a retracted bulk result (Ref. 27) that the authors themselves note was attributed to Joule heating. Other mechanisms give rho ~ -ln T: Kondo-like or magnetic scattering, which is plausible given the ferromagnetic transition near 180 K reported in Ref. 20; electron-electron interaction corrections; and any smooth crossover viewed over a narrow window. The authors should measure the perpendicular and parallel magnetoresistance in the 100-220 K window. Paired-boson localization and fluctuation superconductivity predict a characteristic field scale set by 2e and orbital suppression, whereas Kondo or magnetic scattering gives isotropic negative MR. They should also measure the Nernst effect or diamagnetism on the same flakes, and the temperature dependence of the field scale. At minimum the claim "Cooper pairs persisting up to high temperatures around 220 K" in the abstract must be tied to a measurement that is specific to pairing.

3. The ln(1/T) versus ln T discrimination rests on temperature windows too narrow to decide it. For sample 4 the Bose-glass window is 101-220 K (ln T 4.6-5.4, a factor of about 2.2 in T). For sample 5 it is 105-183 K (a factor of about 1.7). The "Fermi glass" window for samples 1-3 (Fig. 2b) runs from about 220 K to 299 K (a factor of about 1.35). Over such ranges any smooth monotonic function is nearly linear in ln T, and R linear versus G linear differ only at second order. Fig. 3(a), lower panel, shows that G versus ln T for sample 4 is also close to linear over much of the window. Moreover, weak-localization corrections at 200-300 K in an oxide need a phase-coherence length longer than the mean free path at room temperature. The paper gives no estimate of this, and phonon scattering dominates in that range. The authors should report quantitative fit comparisons (residuals or chi^2 for R ~ ln(1/T), G ~ ln T, activated, VRH and power-law forms over the same window), show how the windows were chosen, and justify that a quantum-interference description applies at these temperatures (estimate l and l_phi).

4. The beta-function "vertical flow" appears to follow mechanically from the resistance maximum. beta_EXP = -(2/p) d ln g / d ln T is computed from the same R(T) curves. For samples 4 and 5, R(T) has a maximum at about 80-100 K (Fig. 1c), where d ln g / d ln T passes through zero and changes sign. Approaching that maximum and then the superconducting drop, beta must move vertically at almost fixed ln g, whatever the underlying physics. The "vertical flow" in Fig. 5(b) and the sign change of beta' in Fig. 6(a) therefore restate the existence of a resistance maximum and are not independent evidence for boson localization. Similarly, Eqs. (3)-(4) give opposite signs of beta' only if the data follow one of the two pure log forms exactly, which assumes the conclusion. The authors should show that the vertical flow begins at temperatures well above the resistance maximum, that it is absent in a control with a resistance maximum of non-superconducting origin (for example a magnetic or CaRuO3-type anomaly), and how sensitive it is to the choice p = 1.

5. Quantum critical resistances are taken from finite-temperature data with no critical scaling. A quantum critical point needs a tuning parameter and a separatrix: a temperature-independent R at R_c, with collapse of R(T, x) onto F(|x - x_c|/T^{1/(z nu)}). In Fig. 6(a), beta_c = -1.6 is a horizontal line drawn between groups of different samples at 100-250 K. It is not a crossing point, and samples 6 and 7 near -1 to -1.5 at low T overlap it. In Fig. 6(b), 65.7 kOhm is the low-temperature value of the 5000 nA curve and 16.5 kOhm sits near the 150 nA curves. Bias current is not shown to be a tuning parameter with a separatrix, and the curves at the quoted values are not flat in T. The text also says the resistance is "estimated from g_c = -1.6", but g is a positive dimensionless conductance, so the mapping from beta_c to R_c is not defined. The authors should (i) state exactly how 65.7 kOhm follows from beta_c, (ii) show a separatrix in R(T) as a function of a continuous tuning parameter (field, gate or bias) with finite-size or temperature scaling and the exponent product z nu, and (iii) give uncertainties on R_c including the per-layer normalization (see concern 6). Without these, the "5/2" factor and the comparisons to 8/pi and to the Pfaffian state are numerology.

6. The sheet resistance per layer for sample 4 disagrees between figures. In Fig. 3(a), R_sq/layer for sample 4 peaks near 125 kOhm and is about 60 kOhm at 290 K. In Fig. 6(b), the 1 nA curve for the same sample peaks near 42 kOhm (raw R about 4.1 kOhm on the right axis). That is roughly a factor of 3 between the two figures for the same quantity. Both critical resistances, and their ratios to h/e^2 and h/4e^2, depend directly on this normalization, which in turn depends on the SEM-tilt thickness, the geometry factor and the assumed layer spacing (single-layer thickness and number of conducting layers). The authors should give for each device the thickness with uncertainty, the channel length and width, the layer count, and a single consistent conversion, and should recompute R_c with propagated errors. Fig. 1(a) resistivities, and the claim of 3-4 orders lower resistivity than bulk, rely on the same geometry.

B. Should address

7. Thickness does not order the samples monotonically. Sample 4 (10 nm) and sample 5 (30 nm) are "superconducting". Samples 1-3 (25, 25 and 35 nm) are Mott insulators. Sample 5 is thicker than samples 1 and 2. The phase diagram (Fig. 4a) is therefore plotted against dρ/dT, not thickness. This weakens the title-level framing "thickness-tuned SIT" (Fig. 1 caption) and the statement "We have succeeded in controlling electron correlation by changing the film thickness". It suggests that a flake-dependent property (composition, strain from the substrate, inclusions) controls the ground state. The authors should discuss what distinguishes sample 5 from samples 1-3. They should also report how many flakes were measured in total and how many showed each behavior, since the superconducting and Bose-glass result rests on two nanoflakes.

8. The localization-strength axis dρ/dT = [ρ(120 K) - ρ(280 K)]/dT depends on absolute resistivity, so geometry errors (concern 6) and parallel conduction move samples across phase boundaries. The phase boundaries in Fig. 4(a) are drawn by hand through a few points from about seven samples. Sample 9 appears only in Fig. 4(a), with no R(T) shown anywhere, and sample 8 (300 nm) appears in Figs. 1 and 5 but is not discussed. The authors should show sample 9's data, use a normalized measure (for example ρ(120 K)/ρ(280 K)) that does not depend on thickness, and indicate the uncertainty on each point.

9. Superconductivity in samples 4 and 5 is not characterized here. Fig. 1(c) shows a drop below about 80-100 K, but zero resistance, its magnetic-field dependence, I-V characteristics and a Meissner signal are not shown in this manuscript, and Ref. 20 is cited instead. Since "high-temperature superconductivity has been achieved in thin films at ambient pressure" (Fig. 1 caption) underpins the SC/BG boundary in Fig. 4(a), the authors should include at least R(T, B) for both samples, the residual resistance at base temperature, and V-I curves. They should also discuss why samples 1 and 3 show a resistance maximum near 20 K in Fig. 1(b), and whether this is the same feature.

10. Bias heating and current-induced effects in Fig. 6(b). Ca2RuO4 is known for strong current-induced nonlinearity and heating artifacts (Refs. 27, 28, cited by the authors). Fig. 6(b) uses up to 5 μA through a 10 nm flake on SiO2, and the 65.7 kOhm "critical" value is read from the highest-bias curve. The authors should show V-I curves, estimate the dissipated power and electron temperature, check pulsed against DC measurements, and confirm that the low-bias R(T) used throughout is in the linear-response regime.

11. The MBE-film interpretation is internally inconsistent. Samples 6 and 7 have a shorter c axis (11.592 Å) and therefore, by the authors' argument, stronger RuO6 distortion. Yet they show Bose-glass behavior from 4 to 81 K and no Mott insulator, while the less distorted nanoflakes 1-3 are Mott insulators. The text attributes the absence of zero resistance to "a superconducting path has not been formed", which is not demonstrated. Over the wide low-temperature window in Fig. 3(c) and Fig. S1, ρ ~ -ln T in a non-superconducting film is the standard Kondo or electron-electron-interaction signature. The authors should report magnetoresistance on the MBE films, the in-plane lattice parameter and strain state on LaAlO3, and explain how the c axis relates to bulk S- and L-phase values.

12. The re-analysis of bulk pressure data (Fig. S2, Fig. 4b) is speculative. The 2D Aslamazov-Larkin formula requires a sheet resistance but is applied to a bulk resistivity in μΩ cm, with Tc0 = 12 K and R_N = 44 μΩ cm fixed by hand. A good AL fit with fixed parameters over a limited range does not establish superconductivity in a sample that Ref. 19 identified as a ferromagnetic metal. The authors should either remove the claim that "superconductivity coexisting with ferromagnetism may also occur in bulk crystals" or support it with a 3D AL comparison, free-parameter fits with uncertainties, and the field dependence from the original data.

13. Dimensionless conductance and the universal curve in Fig. 5. g is defined as (ħ/e^2)σ, which for σ in S/cm is not dimensionless. For a 2D beta function g should be a sheet conductance (per layer). Bulk crystal data (Nakatsuji 1 and 2) are mixed with 2D films on the same axis. In the VRH regime ln g = c - (T0/T)^{1/3} gives beta = (2/3)(ln g - c), a straight line whose position depends on c, so collapse onto the Vollhardt-Wölfle curve in the strong-localization region is not a nontrivial test. The authors should define g precisely for each sample, state the parameters of beta_VW (any fitted constants), and explain what in the collapse is beyond construction.

14. The Bose-glass concept is used inconsistently. The abstract defines a Bose glass by "absence of global phase coherence yet finite conductivity". The Bose glass of Fisher et al. is an insulating, compressible T = 0 phase, and a finite-conductivity bosonic state is usually called a Bose or anomalous metal. A phase entered at 220 K and left at about 100 K on cooling is a finite-temperature crossover, not a ground state, yet the paper calls the boundaries "quantum critical points". The authors should state which theoretical phase they mean, and separate finite-temperature crossovers from ground-state claims in Fig. 4(a).

C. Minor

15. Text and figure values disagree: R^{SC-BG} is 16.8 kOhm on p. 8 and 16.5 kOhm in Fig. 6(b). Also, (5/2)h/4e^2 = 16.1 kOhm and (5/2)h/e^2 = 64.5 kOhm, so the quoted "~" should carry stated tolerances.

16. The Fig. 6(a) caption says beta_c separates the Bose glass and Mott insulating phases, while the text says Bose glass and Fermi glass. The Fig. 3(b) caption says R ~ ln T for sample 5, while the text says ln(1/T). The text says "g_c = -1.6" where beta_c is meant.

17. The diamagnetism discussion (p. 5) compares 140 K with "around 220 K" as "close" and leans on a retracted paper. Either give the nanoflake magnetization data with sample details, or soften the statement.

18. The Fig. 4(a) axis label is |dρ/dT| but the legend text says "dρ/dT < 0". The definition dρ(= ρ(120 K) - ρ(280 K))/dT should be written as a finite difference divided by 160 K.

19. Methods: report the base temperature of the ³He system, the current used for the R(T) curves, the contact geometry and contact resistance, and whether the results survive thermal cycling.

20. Typos and wording: "Supplementaty", "Aslamasov", "more assertive" (presumably "more pronounced"), mismatched quotation marks around "vertical flow", and "the vortex flow" versus "vortices are driven" used interchangeably.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add structural and chemical characterization (Raman, TEM or electron diffraction, EDX) on the measured flakes or sister flakes, so that the "distortion release" mechanism and the absence of CaRuO3 or Ru inclusions are shown rather than assumed.
2. Measure magnetoresistance (perpendicular and parallel) across the 100-220 K window and at low temperature on samples 4, 5, 6 and 7. This is the most direct discriminator between localized Cooper pairs and Kondo, magnetic or interaction effects.
3. Replace the hand-drawn critical values with a scaling analysis against a continuous tuning parameter (for example field at fixed thickness), showing a separatrix and a collapse, with R_c uncertainties that include the geometry normalization.
4. Reframe the 220 K claim as a crossover in the temperature dependence of resistance unless pairing-specific evidence (Nernst signal, diamagnetism on the same flakes, or a field scale set by 2e) is added.
