Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports transport on exfoliated/dispersed Ca2RuO4 nanoflakes (5 to 35 nm) and MBE films on LaAlO3. It argues that the resistance follows R ~ ln(1/T) between roughly 100 and 220 K, and that the beta function shows a "vertical flow" in the same range. From this it infers a Bose glass of localized Cooper pairs up to about 220 K, two "quantum critical" sheet resistances (65.7 kOhm and 16.5 kOhm, both near 5/2 times a quantum of resistance), and a phase diagram ordered by a localization parameter d rho/dT. If true, preformed Cooper pairs at 220 K in a ruthenate would be very important. As it stands, the bosonic interpretation rests almost entirely on telling ln T apart from ln(1/T) over less than half a decade in temperature. None of the standard discriminating measurements (magnetoresistance, evidence for charge 2e, a T -> 0 separatrix) is shown.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- Nanoscale Ca2RuO4 is much less resistive than bulk and becomes superconducting (Fig. 1). That is a striking, if previously reported (ref. 29), observation in a correlated oxide, and more transport data on it is useful.
- The authors bring together several sample types (flakes of several thicknesses, MBE films, literature bulk data under doping and pressure) on one phenomenological plot (Fig. 4), which may help organize the field.
- Applying the Takahashi et al. beta-function criterion (ref. 12) to a strongly correlated oxide is a reasonable thing to try, and the analysis is presented compactly (Eqs. 1-4).

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The distinction between R ~ ln(1/T) (bosonic) and G ~ ln T (fermionic) is not resolved by the data shown. In Fig. 3a (sample 4), the "BG" window is ln T = 4.6 to 5.4 (101 to 220 K), a factor of about 2 in T, and R changes by about 35%. In Fig. 3b (sample 5), the window is 105 to 183 K and R changes by about 25%. Over such ranges, ln(1/T) in R and ln T in G differ only at second order in the fractional change. Judging the two fits by eye, as Figs. 3a-c do, cannot separate them. The same applies to the "FG" windows (for example sample 4, 220 to about 290 K; Fig. 2b, 181 to 299 K). The headline claim depends on this. The authors should:
   - give a quantitative comparison of both models on the same data set: residuals, reduced chi-squared, and the stability of the crossover temperature to changes in the window boundaries;
   - show that the ln(1/T) form is preferred over other smooth forms, such as a power law or parallel conduction (see concern 3);
   - give uncertainties on the crossover temperatures plotted in Fig. 4a.

2. The beta-function criterion is not independent of the fits, and the "vertical flow" is a generic feature of any resistance maximum.
   - Eqs. 3-4 show that the sign of beta' is fixed by which of the two functional forms is assumed. Fig. 6a's sign test therefore restates the curvature of R(ln T) that Fig. 3 already tests; it is not, as the caption of Fig. 5 says, "an independent criterion".
   - Because beta = -(2/p) d ln g / d ln T, beta passes through zero at any extremum of R(T). Near that point g barely changes while beta sweeps through a large range. Samples 4 and 5 have a resistance maximum near 80 to 100 K (Figs. 1c, 3a, 3b), which the authors attribute to superconducting onset. That maximum alone produces a near-vertical trajectory in Fig. 5b at ln g ~ 0 to 1.
   - The authors should show which temperature interval each vertical segment in Fig. 5b covers.
   - They should also show that the vertical flow begins near 220 K (not near the resistance peak) and cannot be reproduced by a fermionic R(T) with a superconducting-fluctuation or parallel-channel downturn added. A simple test is to compute beta for a model curve (for example, an Aslamazov-Larkin term added to G = G0 + G1 ln T) and overlay it.
   - The choice p = 1 in Eq. 1 sets the scale of beta and therefore the value beta_c = -1.6. It needs justification, or results for other values of p.

3. Sample phase purity and the origin of the low resistivity are not established for the devices that were measured.
   - The Methods report about 5% CaRuO3 and 6% S-Pbca impurity phases in the starting crystals. CaRuO3 is a metal with a resistivity of order 10^-4 to 10^-3 Ohm cm. That range overlaps the resistivities of samples 4 and 5 (about 2 x 10^-3 Ohm cm, Fig. 1b), which are 3 to 4 orders of magnitude below bulk Ca2RuO4.
   - The flakes are identified only by their shape in SEM. An inclusion or surface layer of CaRuO3, or excess oxygen (Ca2RuO4+delta is known to become metallic), would give a parallel metallic channel. Combined with an insulating Ca2RuO4 channel, this naturally produces a broad resistance maximum and slowly varying, log-like R(T).
   - The claim that thinning "relaxes the RuO6 distortion rather than introducing disorder" is likewise not measured on any nanoflake. The XRD is on bulk powder (L phase) or on MBE films.
   - The authors should characterize the measured flakes, or flakes from the same batch, with Raman spectroscopy (sensitive to the octahedral tilt and to the S/L phases), TEM/electron diffraction and EDX/EELS stoichiometry. They should also show that a parallel-conduction model fails to describe R(T).

4. The "quantum critical resistances" are identified at finite temperature and do not behave as separatrices.
   - A quantum critical sheet resistance is normally identified as the T-independent crossing point of R(T) across a family of samples or tuning-parameter values as T -> 0. Here R_BG-FG = 65.7 kOhm is derived from the condition beta' = 0 at beta_c = -1.6, which occurs at about 150 to 220 K. The steps from beta_c to a resistance value are not given.
   - The measured sheet resistance per layer at the crossover differs from 65.7 kOhm: about 80 kOhm for sample 4 at the crossover in Fig. 3a, and about 32 kOhm for sample 5 in Fig. 3b.
   - Sample 4 superconducts even though its R per layer (about 125 kOhm at its maximum) is far above both "critical" values. Sample 5 (28 to 40 kOhm) is also above 16.5 kOhm. This runs against the usual meaning of a critical resistance separating superconducting from insulating ground states.
   - The values depend entirely on the undefined normalization "R per layer" (see concern 6).
   - The authors should either show a set of samples or bias currents whose R(T) fans out on either side of a common resistance at low T, or describe these values as crossover resistances. The comparisons with 8/pi (ref. 42) and with the 5/2 Pfaffian state (ref. 43) should be removed or clearly labelled as numerical coincidences.

5. There is no direct evidence that the carriers at 100 to 220 K are Cooper pairs. The paper's central physical claim is that Cooper pairs exist up to about 220 K, more than twice the superconducting onset.
   - The only supporting evidence beyond the R(T) curvature is powder magnetization from another study, referenced to work that has been retracted (ref. 34) and debated (ref. 35). No data are shown.
   - Weak-localization physics at 220 K also requires a phase-coherence length that exceeds the relevant length scales. That is not estimated.
   - Standard tests are missing:
     - magnetoresistance across 100 to 250 K, comparing the field scale with the fermionic weak-localization/electron-electron-interaction (Hikami-Larkin-Nagaoka) form against a bosonic form, with the extracted phase-coherence length;
     - the field dependence of the proposed BG-FG crossover temperature;
     - Hall data for the carrier density;
     - ideally a probe of the 2e charge (Little-Parks or h/2e oscillations in a patterned ring, shot noise) or of a pairing gap (tunneling).
   - Without at least the magnetoresistance, the interpretation should be stated as one possibility among several, including electron-electron interaction corrections, Kondo-like scattering and the parallel-channel model in concern 3.

B. Should address

6. The quantity "R per layer" and the dimensionless conductance g are not defined.
   - Figs. 3 and 6 and the critical values use R_square/layer. Presumably this is R_square times the number of RuO2 layers, which depends on the SEM-tilt thickness estimate and on the assumption that layers conduct independently and in parallel. Neither is justified, and the thickness uncertainty is not given.
   - Fig. 5 also places bulk 3D resistivity data (Nakatsuji 1 and 2) and 300 nm sample 8 on the same ln g axis, without stating how g was obtained from a 3D resistivity.
   - The authors should state the conversion, the layer spacing used and the thickness uncertainty, and propagate that uncertainty into the critical resistances.

7. The bias-current data in Fig. 6b may be affected by heating, and the panel is hard to read.
   - Currents up to 5 microamperes are applied to a nanoflake on SiO2 in a 3He system. Current-induced effects in Ca2RuO4 are known to be dominated by Joule heating (refs. 34-35, which the authors cite). No I-V curves, power dissipated or estimate of sample temperature are given.
   - The panel uses two resistance axes, 0 to 70 kOhm on the left and 0 to 6 kOhm on the right. On the right axis, the low-current R(T) of sample 4 peaks near 4 kOhm, whereas Fig. 3a gives about 125 kOhm per layer for the same sample. It is therefore unclear which quantity the 65.7 kOhm and 16.5 kOhm dashed lines refer to.
   - The authors should show I-V curves, use a single normalized axis, and show that the 65.7 kOhm feature is not a heating-limited plateau.

8. The localization parameter d rho/dT is close to a proxy for resistivity magnitude. It is a raw difference, rho(120 K) - rho(280 K), over 160 K, so less resistive samples automatically have smaller |d rho/dT|. It also inherits the thickness error. The phase diagram in Fig. 4a therefore largely restates that lower-resistivity samples superconduct. A normalized measure (for example [rho(120) - rho(280)]/rho(280)), or the activation or hopping parameter T0 from the VRH fits, would make a stronger case that "localization strength" is the controlling variable. The authors should also show d rho/dT for every sample alongside its thickness, to support the statement that thickness is not the control parameter.

9. Reproducibility and sample bookkeeping are thin.
   - Bose-glass behaviour in nanoflakes is shown for only two devices (samples 4 and 5). Their crossover temperatures differ (220 K and 183 K), and no repeat cool-downs or thermal cycling are shown.
   - Fig. 4a includes a "Sample 9" that appears nowhere else: not in Fig. 1, the text or the Methods.
   - Sample 8 (300 nm) appears in Figs. 1 and 5 but not in Fig. 4a.
   - The authors should add a sample table: thickness, method, contact geometry, number of cool-downs, T_c onset, the zero-resistance temperature with its resolution floor, and all crossover temperatures.

10. The structural argument for the MBE films is overstated. The film in-plane parameters a = b = 5.36 Angstrom equal sqrt(2) times the LaAlO3 lattice constant (about 3.79 Angstrom). This means the films are coherently clamped to the substrate, and the short c-axis is consistent with the Poisson response the text says cannot explain it.
   - The main text says in-plane parameters are "considerably smaller than those of the bulk S phase", while the Methods says "similar in-plane lattice parameters". These two statements should be reconciled.
   - The authors should compare quantitatively with the epitaxial-strain study of ref. 38. If octahedral rotations are invoked, half-order reflections or Raman measurements should be provided.

11. The superconducting state itself is only characterized through R(T). In Fig. 1c, R drops to near zero over a broad 30 to 100 K range, but the measurement floor, any field dependence and any Meissner response from these devices are not shown. Because the BG/SC boundary in Fig. 4a depends on identifying T_SC-BG, the authors should define how that temperature is extracted and state the zero-resistance criterion.

C. Minor

12. Fig. 3c and the text disagree. The text gives Bose-glass-like transport below 67 K and 81 K for samples 6 and 7 respectively, but the Fig. 3c crossover line for sample 7 is at ln T of about 4.2 (about 67 K). Please check which value belongs to which sample.

13. The title and abstract call 10 to 300 nm crystals "nanofilms". Sample 8 (300 nm) is close to bulk, and the dispersed flakes are not films in the usual sense. Consider "nanoflakes" for the dispersed crystals and keep "films" for the MBE samples.

14. Eq. 2 uses the absolute value of beta. A short note would help readers: this is what makes the sign of beta' encode the curvature, and beta is negative throughout the relevant range.

15. The Aslamazov-Larkin analysis in Supplementary Fig. S3 is applied to 2.6 GPa bulk data from ref. 28, which is not the system under study. Either connect it explicitly to the nanoflake results or move it out of the main argument.

16. Fig. 1b plots resistivity on a log scale with d rho/dT annotated across the 120 to 280 K window. Marking rho(120) and rho(280) on each curve, or listing them in a table, would make Fig. 4a reproducible.

17. The paragraph citing ref. 34 should state plainly that the magnetization evidence comes from a retracted study and from powders. As written it reads as supporting evidence.

18. The data-availability statement ("upon reasonable request") is weak for a claim of this size. Depositing the R(T) curves would allow readers to redo the fit comparisons in concerns 1 and 2.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add magnetotransport (perpendicular and parallel field, 2 to 250 K) on samples 4 and 5, and use it to discriminate between fermionic and bosonic localization and to extract a phase-coherence length at the proposed 220 K crossover.
2. Replace by-eye fitting with a statistical model comparison over the same windows, and simulate the beta-function trajectories that a fermionic R(T) with a superconducting-onset peak would give. This shows directly whether the "vertical flow" requires bosons.
3. Characterize the measured flakes for phase purity and octahedral distortion (Raman, TEM/EDX). Rule out a metallic CaRuO3 or oxygen-rich parallel channel with a two-channel fit.
4. Recast the "quantum critical resistances" as finite-temperature crossover resistances unless a T -> 0 separatrix can be shown across samples or bias currents. Drop the comparisons to 8/pi and the 5/2 Pfaffian unless a mechanism is provided.
