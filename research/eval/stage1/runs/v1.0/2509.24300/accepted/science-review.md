A. Must address

1. No measurement tests whether the carriers between about 100 and 220 K are Cooper pairs. The headline claims (abstract; Results p. 2; Conclusion) are that "signatures of Cooper pairs persist up to high temperatures of ~220 K" and that "localized Cooper pairs and moving vortices" exist at 220 K in sample 4, more than twice the superconducting onset. Every Bose glass assignment (Figs. 3, 5, 6) comes from zero-field R(T): R ~ ln(1/T) fits better than G ~ ln T over 101-220 K (sample 4) or 105-183 K (sample 5), plus the sign of beta' derived from the same curves. Neither is unique to bosons. Weak localization of charge-2e bosons has signatures that fermionic weak localization or electron-electron interaction corrections do not share: a positive magnetoresistance in the Bose glass window with a characteristic field set by the superconducting flux quantum h/2e through the localization area, turning over at a field related to pair breaking; a field-tuned insulator transition with a crossing point (separatrix) and finite-size scaling. A fermionic weak-localization (Fermi glass) regime should instead give negative magnetoresistance of Hikami-Larkin-Nagaoka form. The authors should measure R(T, B) on samples 4 and 5 (and 6/7, with samples 1-3 as fermionic references) in perpendicular and parallel field, from about 250 K down through the superconducting onset. They should state the bosonic and fermionic predictions for the sign and scale of the magnetoresistance, show which the data follow, show that the magnetoresistance changes qualitatively at the claimed Fermi glass to Bose glass temperature (about 220 K for sample 4, 183 K for sample 5) but not in samples 1-3 at the same temperatures, give the field dependence of the crossover temperature, and extract the phase-coherence length. Hall data for the carrier density would help, and if possible a direct probe of 2e charge (h/2e or Little-Parks oscillations in a patterned ring, shot noise), a Nernst signal at 150-200 K, or tunnelling evidence of a pairing gap. Without at least the magnetoresistance, the claim should be reduced to "a logarithmic-in-1/T resistance regime of unknown origin", presented as one possibility alongside electron-electron interaction corrections, Kondo-like scattering and parallel conduction (concern 4).

2. The data shown do not distinguish R ~ ln(1/T) (bosonic) from G ~ ln T (fermionic). Over the windows used the two forms differ only at second order in the fractional resistance change. In Fig. 3a (sample 4) the Bose glass window is ln T = 4.6 to 5.4 (101 to 220 K), a factor of about 2 in T, over which R changes by about 35%; in Fig. 3b (sample 5) the window is 105 to 183 K (a factor of about 1.7) and R changes by only about 25% (roughly 32 to 40 kOhm). Straight lines in R vs ln T and G vs ln T are nearly indistinguishable at that level. The text says the sample 4 data "align well" with R ~ ln(1/T) "rather than" G ~ ln T, but no residuals, chi-squared or fit-window sensitivity are given. The same applies to the Fermi glass windows (sample 4, 220 to about 290 K; Fig. 2b, 181 to 299 K). The window edges (arrows and dashed lines in Figs. 2 and 3) appear to be chosen by eye, and these same edges define the crossover temperatures plotted in Fig. 4a. Ordinary mechanisms can also produce the observed curvature: an Aslamazov-Larkin or Maki-Thompson fluctuation conductance growing on cooling, a parallel metallic or superconducting minority channel shunting an insulating matrix (concern 4), or temperature-dependent current redistribution in an inhomogeneous flake. The authors should fit the 100-290 K data of samples 4 and 5 with both logarithmic forms and with alternatives (a power law, an Altshuler-Aronov form, a G ~ ln T term plus an AL fluctuation term with T_c0 near 30-90 K, a two-channel parallel resistor) over identical windows; report residuals, reduced chi-squared or information criteria; define the window edges objectively (for example where residuals exceed a stated threshold); show that the preferred form is robust when the edges move; and give uncertainties on every crossover temperature in Fig. 4a.

3. The beta-function "vertical flow" is not an independent criterion, and it arises generically at any resistance maximum. From Eqs. (1)-(4), the sign of beta' is just the sign of the second derivative of ln g with respect to ln T, that is, the curvature of R(T) on a logarithmic temperature axis, so Fig. 5b and the sign test of Fig. 6a restate what Fig. 3 already tests; they are not "an independent criterion" as the Fig. 5 caption says. More importantly, since beta = -(2/p) d ln g / d ln T, beta passes through zero at any extremum of R(T), where g barely changes while beta sweeps through a large range; this draws a near-vertical line in beta versus ln g whatever the transport mechanism. Samples 4 and 5 have such a maximum near 80-100 K (Figs. 1c, 3a, 3b), which the authors attribute to superconducting onset, and the vertical segments in Fig. 5b (at ln g of about 0 to 1) extend to positive beta, which corresponds to dR/dT > 0, the superconducting-fluctuation side of the maximum rather than the Bose glass window. Any R(T) that rises on cooling and then rolls over must bend this way just above the maximum. The authors should state which temperature interval each vertical segment in Fig. 5b covers and mark the resistance maximum on it; show that the vertical flow begins near 220 K and persists when only data above the maximum are used; and show that it cannot be reproduced by a fermionic R(T) with a superconducting-fluctuation or parallel-channel downturn, for example by computing and overlaying beta for a model curve G = G0 + G1 ln T plus an Aslamazov-Larkin term.

4. Phase purity of the measured flakes is not established, so parallel conduction through impurity phases and other mundane explanations of the low resistivity and the R(T) shapes are not excluded. The starting crystals contain about 5% CaRuO3 and about 6% S-Pbca phase (Methods). CaRuO3 is a metal with resistivity of order 10^-4 to 10^-3 Ohm cm, overlapping the resistivity of samples 4 and 5 (about 2 x 10^-3 Ohm cm, Fig. 1b), which are 3 to 4 orders of magnitude below bulk Ca2RuO4 (Fig. 1a). Flakes are distinguished from CaRuO3 only by crystal shape in SEM, which does not exclude intergrowths. A CaRuO3 inclusion or surface layer, an interfacial layer on SiO2, Ru-rich regions, oxygen off-stoichiometry (Ca2RuO4+delta is metallic), surface damage from sonication in dichloroethane or from electron-beam lithography, or a percolating minority phase would lower rho and |Delta rho|. Combined with an insulating Ca2RuO4 channel, such a parallel path naturally gives a broad resistance maximum and slowly varying, log-like R(T); a granular metal or granular superconductor (Efetov-type sigma ~ ln T, Josephson-coupled grains) likewise gives logarithmic R(T), a resistance maximum and incomplete or broad superconducting transitions, and could account for the "superconducting" drop. No structural or chemical measurement was made on any measured flake. The authors should characterize the measured flakes, or flakes from the same batch (at least for samples 4 and 5), with Raman spectroscopy (which distinguishes L/S Ca2RuO4 and CaRuO3), TEM or electron diffraction including cross-sections (layering, second phases), and EDX/EELS stoichiometry; show that a parallel-conduction model fails to describe R(T); and discuss explicitly why a percolating mixed-phase picture cannot account for Figs. 1-3.

6. The two "quantum critical resistances" (65.7 kOhm, close to (5/2)h/e^2, and 16.5 kOhm, close to (5/2)h/4e^2) are not shown to be critical points and depend on assumptions. A quantum critical sheet resistance is normally the temperature-independent crossing point of R(T) across a family of samples or tuning-parameter values as T -> 0, with a scaling collapse (exponent zv). Here R^BG-FG = 65.7 kOhm is tied to beta_c = -1.6, a finite-temperature condition at about 150-220 K; it appears to come from reading the Vollhardt-Wolfle curve at beta_c (Fig. 5a gives ln g of about -0.9, g of about 0.39, about 66 kOhm) rather than from a measured resistance, and the steps are not given. beta_c = -1.6 is itself a horizontal line separating two groups of samples in Fig. 6a, not a sign change of beta' within one sample. The measured per-layer resistances at the marked crossovers are about 80 kOhm for sample 4 (Fig. 3a, ln T of about 5.4) and about 32 kOhm for sample 5 (Fig. 3b, ln T of about 5.2), which differ by a factor of about 2.5 and bracket 65.7 kOhm, not what a universal value would give. Sample 4 superconducts although its R per layer (about 125 kOhm at its maximum) is far above both "critical" values, and sample 5 (28-40 kOhm) is above 16.5 kOhm, contrary to the usual meaning of a critical resistance separating superconducting and insulating ground states. In Fig. 6b the 65.7 and 16.5 kOhm lines only mark where the highest-current curve levels off and roughly where curves meet at low T; there is no temperature-independent separatrix and no collapse in current, thickness or Delta rho. The values scale directly with the per-layer normalization (concern 7) and with p (beta carries 2/p; p = 2 or 3 rescales beta_c by 2-3 and changes the inferred resistance completely). 65.7 kOhm also differs from (5/2)h/e^2 = 64.5 kOhm, and the paper gives two unrelated explanations for the factor 2.5 (8/pi from ref. 42 and the 5/2 Pfaffian state, ref. 43). The authors should state exactly which sample, temperature and procedure give each value, propagate the uncertainties in thickness, layer count, geometry and p, show whether different samples give the same value, and either show a crossing or scaling collapse that defines a critical point or call these characteristic crossover resistances. The 5/2, 8/pi and Pfaffian comparisons (p. 6-7) should be removed or clearly labelled as numerical coincidences unless the error bar is well below the differences between those numbers.

8. The preconditions of the 2D weak-localization framework (Eq. 1) are not justified for 100-300 K. Setting p = 1 (electron-electron dephasing) is assumed, whereas electron-phonon dephasing (p of about 2-3) usually dominates in oxides at these temperatures; since beta scales as 1/p, the absolute beta values in Figs. 5 and 6 and beta_c depend on this choice. The ln T and ln(1/T) forms assume 2D transport, but L_phi at 200 K is likely only a few nm, smaller than the 10-35 nm thickness, and weak localization at 220 K requires a phase-coherence length exceeding the relevant scales. Fig. 2a also applies 2D Mott VRH (T^(-1/3)) to 25-35 nm flakes, and the Mott-to-Fermi-glass crossover temperatures in Fig. 4a depend on that choice. The authors should estimate L_phi or the localization length in this range (for example from magnetoresistance, concern 1) and compare it with thickness, show how the Bose/Fermi glass classification, Figs. 5 and 6 and beta_c change for p = 2 and 3, and compare 2D VRH with 3D Mott VRH, Efros-Shklovskii VRH and Arrhenius fits.

The current-induced transition in Fig. 6b may be dominated by Joule heating, transport may be non-ohmic in the claimed Bose glass window, and the panel's resistance values are inconsistent with Fig. 3a. The claim that thinning relaxes RuO6 tilt, rotation and flattening "rather than ... impurity-induced disorder", and that this sets the ground state (abstract, Fig. 1a schematic, p. 2, p. 4-5, Conclusion), has no structural evidence on the devices. The only lattice parameters for the exfoliated material (a = 5.350, b = 5.343, c = 12.278 Å) are powder data on the parent bulk crystals, matching the bulk L phase. The authors should measure distortion on representative devices (Raman phonon shifts, cross-sectional STEM, diffraction), compare the films quantitatively with the epitaxial-strain study of ref. 38 and provide a strain calculation, give half-order reflections or Raman if octahedral rotations are invoked, reconcile the two in-plane statements, quantify disorder, and soften the causal language where the evidence is only correlational.
