A. Must address

1. The PNO dxy orbital dispersion (Fig. 5a,b) is not yet shown to be intrinsic rather than a fitting or cross-section artifact, and it is the paper's main new result and the basis for the orbital-superexchange analysis. The claimed dispersion is a centroid shift of about 62–90 meV in a peak whose width is about 0.4 eV FWHM (single-ion model, SM G), roughly 15–20% of the resolution-convolved linewidth. The peak sits between the 0.7 eV "RE" feature, which by the authors' account (p. 3; Fig. 1b) grows toward the zone edge, and a broad, unresolved dxz/yz plus d3z2 tail whose fit parameters are strongly cross-correlated (SM Figs. 7–8). Several things change along each cut and could move the fitted centroid by tens of meV without any real propagation: at fixed 2θ = 149.5° the incidence angle, the incident and scattered polarization projections and L all change continuously with Q, so the relative weights of overlapping components change (Fig. 4 and p. 6 already show that the polarization character of the 1.29 eV peak differs between ±H and from theory); self-absorption differs between grazing incidence and grazing emission; and the spin-flip and non-spin-flip components of a dd excitation differ in energy by an amount of order the exchange (tens of meV for J1 ≈ 46 meV), so any Q- or geometry-dependent change in their relative weight shifts the centroid. The quoted error bars are fit confidence intervals that ignore the correlations among overlapping peak parameters, and the fitted hoppings (t1 = 12.5 ± 3.8, t2 = 0.2 ± 4.8, t3 = 5.4 ± 1.7 meV) are three free hoppings plus an offset fitted to a dispersion of 60–90 meV. Fig. 5b, with vertical lines fixed at the minimum, is not enough to judge a shift of a few tens of meV on a 0.4 eV-wide peak. The authors should: (i) compare the dxy position at +H and −H at the same |Q| (grazing incidence against grazing emission), using for example the spectra already taken at H = ±0.4 (Fig. 4a,b), since an intrinsic dispersion must be even in Q while cross-section artifacts generally are not; (ii) show that the trend survives changes to the fit model: two versus three dd peaks (as in Fig. 7), the RE peak free, fixed or removed, the dxz/yz/d3z2 widths free versus fixed across Q; (iii) report a model-independent measure such as the first moment over a fixed window around 1.3 eV; (iv) simulate with the single-ion cross sections of Fig. 4, including the sf/nsf energy offset, the apparent centroid shift expected from geometry alone along the measured path; (v) plot the fitted dxy width and amplitude against Q next to Fig. 5a; (vi) show the dxy energy from σ-polarized maps if they exist and, if possible, repeat one cut at a different scattering angle. The same tests on CCO would show whether the sign reversal between the two materials is robust. If only one sign of dispersion survives every fit model the claim stands; otherwise it should be presented as tentative.

2. The agreement between orbiton theory and experiment in Table III (t1^orb = 13.7 meV against 12.5 ± 3.8 meV) depends on chosen parameters, the parameter choices are inconsistently described, and the parameter set is not checked against the spin exchange. From Eq. 20 and the expressions below Eq. 8, t1^orb = (jHS − jLS)/2 · J̄1^orb with jHS = 1/(1 − 3J_H/U); with J_H = 1.2 eV and U = 6.5 eV, 3J_H/U = 0.55, close to a divergence. Taking J_H = 1.0 eV (the CCO value) at the same U lowers (jHS − jLS)/2 from about 0.50 to about 0.34, a roughly 30% change in t1^orb before J̄1^orb changes at all; the CCO value (t1^orb = 6.7 meV) uses J_H = 1.0 eV and U = 8 eV, so the contrast between materials depends largely on these choices. J̄1^orb in turn depends on Δ = 4.4 eV (from DFT), on hoppings set by hand to 80% of the CCO values (a qualitative nephelauxetic / larger d–p splitting argument), and on U. The Methods say U was taken from La4Ni3O8 (ref. 29), while SM H says "our estimate of the Mott energy U value is exclusively based on the orbital dispersion"; if U was tuned to the dispersion, the agreement in Table III is a fit, not a prediction. There is also an arithmetic problem: SM I gives J̄1^orb = U·J1^orb/(U − 2J_H) ≈ 27 meV, but with J1^orb = 21.6 meV, U = 6.5 eV and J_H = 1.2 eV that formula gives about 34 meV; 27 meV corresponds to U/(U − J_H), and t1^orb = 13.7 meV uses 27 meV. Finally, the same three-band parameters should reproduce the measured spin superexchange, which is not shown, although the abstract attributes the smaller J1 to larger Δ and smaller hopping: a simple estimate J ∝ t_pd^4/Δ^3 with Table II values (0.8^4 × (1.8/4.4)^3) gives J_PNO/J_CCO ≈ 0.03, far below the measured ratio of about 0.56 (46/82 meV), suggesting the parameter set may not describe PNO's superexchange. The authors should correct the J̄1^orb formula or number; state unambiguously how U was obtained; compute the spin J1 for both compounds from the Table II parameters and compare it with Fig. 3a; give a sensitivity analysis (an uncertainty band) of t1^orb, J1^orb and J2^orb over plausible ranges of U, J_H, Δ, t_pd and the hopping scaling for both PNO and CCO, showing whether the sign and NN dominance survive; and present the agreement as consistency rather than validation.

3. The claim that PNO has three-dimensional antiferromagnetic correlations with an out-of-plane coupling comparable to CCO (abstract; p. 5; SM F; Fig. 13; Conclusions, "clearly confers a certain degree of tridimensionality") is not constrained by the data. J⊥ = 5.9 meV is fixed ("fix" in the Fig. 3a table), not fitted. Fig. 13 shows it is constrained only by the few points within about 0.05–0.125 r.l.u. of the specular condition, where the magnon merges with a strong elastic line (SM D, Fig. 9), the phonon was removed by hand, and the error bars are about ±30–50 meV (around 75 meV near Q = 0.05). Other effects would raise the energy near Γ in the same way and are not excluded: an XY/easy-plane or easy-axis anisotropy gap (which the authors mention via ref. 65), a finite in-plane correlation length in a film without established long-range order (a spin-glass-like state is cited in the Introduction), and the bias from fitting a ~100 meV-wide damped mode with a single resolution-convolved DHO next to a strong elastic tail. Because 2θ is fixed, L changes along every cut, a J⊥-induced gap depends on L, and the paper gives neither the L of each spectrum nor whether the LSW model was evaluated at the actual (H, K, L); no L dependence was measured at fixed in-plane Q. The authors should tabulate or plot L against H for each spectrum (this also bears on the dd intensities); state whether the fit uses the actual (H, K, L); measure the near-Γ magnon energy at two or more L for the same in-plane Q (for example H = K = 0.05–0.1 at a different incidence or scattering angle), the direct test of J⊥ against anisotropy; or fit J⊥ freely, report its confidence interval and correlations with J1–J4, and compare χ² against an anisotropy-gap or correlation-length model. Otherwise J⊥ should be quoted wherever used as an upper bound, "consistent with J⊥ = 0–10 meV", and J⊥/J1 = 13% as a range (0–22%), with the comparison to CCO (J⊥/J1 = 8.5%) and the "comparable value of the spin excitation energy at in-plane zone center" restated accordingly. The statement that the results "give a proof of the fully occurred topotactic reduction of the PNO samples and the absence of interstitial oxygen atoms" (p. 5) does not follow from a fixed J⊥ (or from the charge-order null result of Fig. 6) and should be removed or supported by structural data.

4. The damping comparison in Fig. 3b does not support the claim that self-doping has "a much milder impact on spin order than chemical doping" and offers "a non-disruptive way to achieve superconductivity" (p. 4; abstract; Conclusions). Fig. 3b compares absolute damping, about 80–100 meV for PNO against about 200–500 meV for Bi2201 (ref. 54), but the PNO magnon bandwidth is roughly half that of the cuprates (J1 = 46 against 82 meV); relative to the mode energy, PNO damping (about 90/150–190 meV, γ/ω ≈ 0.5–0.6) is close to that of underdoped Bi2201 at comparable momenta, so PNO looks like an underdoped cuprate rather than a nearly undamped system. Other contributions to the PNO width are not separated from intrinsic damping: inhomogeneity or partial reduction in a 5 nm, topotactically reduced, capped film, residual apical oxygen and interfacial layers, the impurity phases invoked for the 0.7 eV peak, L-integration, the unresolved phonon, phonon-overtone and multi-magnon/bimagnon content that was left out of the PNO fit (SM B) but included for CCO, and the single-peak DHO fit near X, which the authors admit overestimates damping in CCO. The Bi2201 values come from a different sample family, experiment and fitting chain, and the self-doped hole concentration of the measured PNO film is not determined, so self-doping and chemical doping cannot be put on a common axis. The authors should plot damping normalized by magnon energy or by J1 for all samples; fit PNO with a model that includes the phonon and a multi-magnon component and show how much width remains; confirm that the Bi2201 values were obtained with the same DHO definition and resolution treatment, ideally by refitting both with the same model; estimate the doped-hole concentration of the RIXS sample (for example from the Ni L3 XAS lineshape, the O K pre-edge, or Hall data in ref. 42); discuss extrinsic sources of damping; and ideally compare with a non-superconducting or differently reduced PNO film.

B. Should address

5. The free-orbiton approximation behind the theoretical t1^orb drops terms larger than the one kept (SM Eqs. 15–19). The kept free hopping has prefactor (jHS − jLS)/2 ≈ 0.5, while the neglected orbiton–magnon coupling terms (Eqs. 16–17) have prefactors (jHS + jLS)/2 ≈ 1.7 and jHS ≈ 2.2. The main text (p. 8) argues that NN orbiton motion is strongly frustrated by string-type magnon dressing, so the argument relies on the very terms that are neglected; dropping them because they mostly produce incoherent weight does not show that the coherent dispersion is unrenormalized. The model also assumes a long-range antiferromagnetic, ferro-orbital ground state, whereas PNO is a self-doped metal without reported long-range order, and the fitted t3^orb = 5.4 meV, about half of t1, lies outside the model. The authors should estimate the renormalization of the coherent orbiton band (for example with the self-consistent Born or linear-string treatment used for CCO in ref. 45), or at least bound it; state the expected quasiparticle weight; relate the large PNO dxy width (about 0.4 eV FWHM) to the incoherent part; and explain why the neglected t3 does not undermine the comparison.

6. The CCO and PNO orbiton dispersions are fitted with different models, and the fitted parameters do not match the quoted bandwidths. CCO is fitted with a pure NNN term (Eq. 2, with no constant offset written) while PNO is fitted with NN, NNN and third-neighbour terms (Eq. 1), so the "opposite dispersion" and "NN versus NNN" contrast partly reflects the model choice; SM I itself predicts a finite NN hopping for CCO (t1 = 6.7 meV). Eq. 2 with t2 = −13.6 meV gives bandwidths of 54 meV along Γ–X and 27 meV along Γ–(0.25,0.25), whereas Fig. 5c quotes 70 and 61 meV, and the white curve at (0.25,0.25) appears close to the maximum rather than midway. The authors should fit both data sets with the same Eq. 1 form (with t1 and t2 free for CCO, to test compatibility with 6.7 meV) and report parameters with covariances, check that the plotted curves correspond to the quoted parameters, and reconcile the bandwidths with the arrows in Fig. 5.

7. The "coexistence of superconductivity and sharp spin excitations" (pp. 2–4; abstract; Conclusions) was not measured. All RIXS data were taken at 20 K (Methods), above both the onset (just below 11 K) and zero resistance (below 4 K); RIXS also averages over the whole film, so a superconducting fraction and a magnetically correlated fraction could be spatially separate. Superconductivity in nominally undoped PrNiO2 is itself unusual and frames the whole paper, yet it is supported only by reference to ref. 42 ("the resulting samples show a complete superconducting behavior"); the transition is broad, and no ρ(T) of the film measured by RIXS is shown. Calling the PNO magnon "sharp" (p. 2) also needs reconciling with the roughly 100 meV damping in Fig. 3b, which is far from resolution-limited. The authors should show ρ(T) for the film(s) actually measured, including after the beamtime, and state whether the same film was used for transport and RIXS; briefly discuss inhomogeneity given the broad transition; and restate the claim as normal-state coexistence in a film that becomes superconducting below a given temperature, with unknown bulk superconducting fraction, unless data below Tc or a Meissner fraction can be provided.

8. Sample characterization and reproducibility relevant to the RIXS conclusions are missing. Only one PNO film appears to have been measured, and it is not stated how many films were measured, whether the magnon and orbiton dispersions were reproduced on a second film, or whether all PNO data come from one beamtime. No Ni L3 or O K XAS is shown, so a residual Ni2+ (incompletely reduced) fraction is not excluded; such a fraction would add dd weight near 1–2 eV and could produce the mixed polarization of the 1.29 eV peak and the unpolarized continuum (Fig. 4). The 0.7 eV peak is said to be stronger in samples with impurity-phase diffraction (ref. 49), yet no diffraction of this sample (c-axis parameter, secondary phases) is shown. The CCO data are a reanalysis of datasets partly published in refs. 45 and 55, and differences from those papers (for example J values or dxy energies) are not noted. The authors should show Ni L3 (and O K) XAS, XRD and ρ(T) for the measured film; state the sample count; reproduce at least the magnon zone-boundary energy and the dxy dispersion on a second PNO film, ideally with a reference nickelate film (non-superconducting or deliberately doped) measured under the same conditions, or state clearly that this is a single-sample study; and note where the CCO reanalysis differs from the earlier publications.

9. The four-neighbour linear spin-wave fit for PNO (Fig. 3a) is under-constrained and its comparison with the literature is not quantitative. Five parameters (J1–J4 and J⊥) are fitted to a single dispersion along M–Γ–X–M that stops at H = 0.475 without reaching X, with about 45 meV of zone-boundary dispersion, large error bars near Γ, a magnon about 100 meV wide and a single-peak fit near X that the authors call approximate. J2–J4 are likely strongly correlated with each other and with J1, and the quoted uncertainties (J1 = 45.8 ± 0.3 meV, J4 = 8.0 ± 0.4 meV) look implausibly small; the effect of fixing J⊥ and how the 95% intervals were obtained are not reported. Along [H,H] the data peak near (0.2,0.2), which no J1–J4 Heisenberg model with this sign structure reproduces, and near (0.25,0.25) they lie about 20 meV below the curve (Figs. 3a, 13); the authors link this to a possible incommensurate reflection or elastic artifact (p. 6). It is not stated whether the S = 1/2 quantum renormalization factor (Zc ≈ 1.18) is included, which affects J1 directly, and the sign convention for J2 (negative) is not defined. "In agreement with the literature [12, 24, 51]" needs numbers: earlier nickelate RIXS studies mostly used J1–J2 descriptions and reported larger J1 (for example J1 ≈ 64 meV for NdNiO2 in ref. 12; ref. 47 reports PrNiO2 film values), so parameters from different Hamiltonians are being compared. The authors should give the parameter covariance matrix; show fits with fewer couplings (J1–J2, J1–J2–J3, or t/U with ring exchange) with model-selection statistics, and with the [H,H] points beyond about 0.2 excluded; refit both compounds with the same reduced model for a like-for-like comparison; state Zc and the J2 sign convention; tabulate literature J values next to theirs with the model used for each; and express the PNO/CCO comparison also in model-independent quantities such as E(0.5,0), E(0.25,0.25) and their difference.

10. The interpretation of the dd polarization data, in particular the mixed polarization character and enhanced σ intensity of the 1.29 eV dxy peak as Pr 5d (or interstitial s) hybridization (Fig. 4, Fig. 12, p. 6), is one of several possibilities and rests on an undocumented polarimetric analysis. The 0.7 eV feature that underpins this picture is itself not pinned down: the text adopts Ni 3d–Pr 5d hybridization while acknowledging a possible extrinsic origin linked to impurity phases (p. 3). Alternatives not addressed include overlap of the broad dxz/yz tail and the 0.7 eV peak (both partly non-crossed), the unpolarized continuum attributed to self-doping, a residual Ni2+ fraction, the polarimeter efficiency correction and its uncertainty, and the assumed in-plane 45° spin direction for PNO, which has no established long-range order and which sets the sf/nsf weights. The extraction of crossed and non-crossed components (polarimeter efficiency, multilayer reflectivity ratio, normalization) is not described, and the PNO components are noisy enough to need 15-point smoothing (Fig. 12). The single-ion calculations (SM G, Fig. 4g–l) use the Table I peak energies as input and hand-chosen widths (for example 1.2 eV FWHM for d3z2 in PNO, separate sf/nsf widths for dxz and dyz in CCO) to "reproduce the remaining peaks", so "well reproduced within a single-ion model" tests only relative polarization weights, and only by eye. The authors should describe the polarimeter calibration; fit crossed and non-crossed spectra simultaneously with the same component set and report the crossed fraction of each peak (including dxy) with uncertainties propagated from the calibration, against theory and against the fraction expected under each scenario; state which comparisons in Fig. 4 are predictions and which depend on tuned widths; show how the PNO predictions change for other spin orientations or a spin-averaged paramagnetic case; and show RIXS at a few incident energies across the Ni L3 edge to tell a Ni-centred excitation from a feature with different resonance behaviour. Without these, the hybridization interpretation should be presented as one of several possibilities.
