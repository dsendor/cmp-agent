A. Must address

1. All evidence of device quality comes from a single device, but the paper presents it as a property of the method. The capacitance map (Fig. 2b), nSOT magnetization (Fig. 2c), TMF (Fig. 3), current imaging (Fig. 4a–f), channel and square transport (Fig. 4g,j) and Fig. S6 all appear to come from one 13-layer device: the 740 µm² device of Fig. 2a / Fig. S5a–c. Even there, the measurements cover only the central 10 × 10 µm² square (100 µm² of 740 µm²). The other devices in Fig. S5 (660–1330 µm², including the 1330, 1080 and 1070 µm² devices) are shown only as optical and IR images taken after transfer, not after nanofabrication, and have no transport or capacitance data. This includes the >1300 µm² device the abstract cites ("highly uniform devices exceeding 1300 µm²"). The abstract and conclusion say the approach "enable[s] highly uniform devices" and moves "area, yield, and uniformity" into "a qualitatively new regime". One device shows that such a device can exist. It cannot show that the quality comes from the method rather than from one good flake, and it gives no device-to-device spread. Device quality, more than domain area, is what the community cares about. The authors should report at least basic measurements on several more cryo-shock devices, at the same (n_e, D) where possible: a capacitance (n, D) map with sharp features, R(n_e, D) phase diagrams with the known spin/valley-ordered features, TMF or low-temperature negative bend resistance, and a mobility or quantum-mobility estimate (for example the onset field of Shubnikov–de Haas oscillations). They should report the spread. Ideally the set would include a >1000 µm² device, a thinner (<10-layer) device, and a standard-exfoliated control device made with the identical transfer process, to show whether the cryogenic shock leaves disorder unchanged or improves it. A compact table of all Fig. S5 devices would help: layer number, rhombohedral area, whether stacking survived fabrication and cooldown, and one transport metric. If no other devices were measured, the abstract and conclusion should say clearly that the transport and imaging come from one device, and narrow the claims accordingly.

2. The headline "disorder mean free path exceeding 200 µm" does not follow from the TMF analysis as constructed (abstract; Fig. 3j, labelled i in the text; Methods Eqs. 3–6; Fig. S7b). Eq. 5 gives ℓ_mfp = −(πd/2)/ln[A(T)/A_0] with d ≈ 5 µm, and A_0 is the average of the four lowest-temperature points. The normalization divides out any temperature-independent scattering, including the disorder scattering the abstract refers to, so ℓ_mfp → ∞ as T → 0 by construction, whatever the actual disorder level. Eq. 5 therefore returns only the temperature-dependent part of the scattering rate, and fitting it to Matthiessen's rule with a finite ℓ_0 is internally inconsistent. The arithmetic makes the problem concrete: the first-focus path is πd/2 ≈ 8 µm, so ℓ = 200 µm corresponds to A/A_0 ≈ exp(−7.85/200) ≈ 0.96, a 4% drop in peak area. Below about 30 K the quoted value is set by percent-level changes in A_peak. These are comparable to the scatter in Fig. S7b and to the effects of several analysis choices: the integration window (dashed lines in Fig. 3e–h), the linear baseline under the peak (whose shape clearly changes between 2 K and 198 K), the linear-in-B Hall subtraction, the saturation level, and thermal broadening of the lineshape beyond the window. Eq. 5 also assumes that bulk scattering along the path is the only temperature-dependent loss. It neglects temperature-dependent boundary specularity (p = 0.5 in the simulations), diffuse loss at the edge between the contacts, and contact transmission. The multiple focusing peaks (1R–6R) show boundary specularity, not a mean free path far above 8 µm, and the negative bend resistance (Fig. S6c) supports ℓ of order 10 µm. Fig. 3j has no error bars. The Methods concede the point ("not well suited to determine the mean-free path at lowest temperatures"; "we can only note that ℓ_0 ≥ 200 µm"), but neither caveat reaches the abstract or main text. The comparison with "best-in-class" mono- and bilayer graphene [44–46] sets side by side values obtained by different methods at different densities. The authors should: add error bars to ℓ_mfp(T) that propagate the noise in A_peak, the uncertainty in A_0, and the window and baseline choices; show that the result is robust to those choices; state the temperature range used in the Matthiessen fit and whether ℓ_0 is free or fixed, since α = 1.56 ± 0.05 depends on this; and state the lowest temperature at which ℓ_mfp is actually resolved rather than bounded. They should obtain an absolute, not self-normalized, bound on ℓ_0. Options include: fitting the absolute low-temperature focusing amplitude and the 1R/2R/… peak ratios with the Boltzmann solver (Fig. 3b) as a function of ℓ_mr, with p fixed independently; mapping the measured low-temperature negative square resistance (about −6.5 Ω, Fig. S6c) onto the simulated R(ℓ_mr, ℓ_ee) map (Fig. S9a); stating quantitatively the temperature of the bend-resistance sign change, where ℓ_mfp ≈ 10 µm; or measuring focusing over several contact separations or longer paths. Failing that, the abstract should state only that the temperature-dependent scattering length exceeds a lower bound below 30 K, with the method's sensitivity limit. It should either explain how the literature values were obtained or compare mobilities instead.

3. The exfoliation statistics (Fig. 1g,h; Methods; Fig. S2) do not establish that the shock itself raises the rhombohedral fraction from about 12% to 32% and the mean flake area from 1200 to 3100 µm². The Methods state only that "the same total Si substrate area" was compared. Several things are not stated: the number of flakes, exfoliation runs, chips and tape preparations per method; the number of bulk crystals, whether both methods used the same crystal, and its source (natural, Kish or HOPG); and the run-to-run spread. Rhombohedral content is known to depend on thickness and flake size. Flakes from 3 to about 20 layers are pooled, the thickness distributions of the two methods are not shown, and cryo-shock flakes are systematically larger (standard flakes cluster near 10³ µm² in Fig. 1g). Differences in thickness or size distributions alone could therefore shift the averages. The exclusion criteria (cracks, wrinkles, dirt, aspect ratio, IR-visible micro-domains, areas < 80 µm²; Fig. S2b) are said to introduce "approximately equal systematic error" for both methods. A thermal shock plausibly creates more cracks and wrinkles, so this needs checking. The ovals in Fig. 1g show ±1 standard deviation with no statistical test. It is not stated whether 12%/32% are per-flake arithmetic means or area-weighted, how Bernal-only flakes plotted at zero are handled, or whether 1200/3100 µm² are geometric means (as the Fig. 1g caption suggests) or arithmetic means. The authors should give the flake, run and crystal counts for both methods and the per-run fractions with their spread. They should show the rhombohedral fraction against layer number for both methods and compare size-matched subsets (for example, cryo versus standard flakes of about 1000 µm²). They should report the excluded area fraction for each method, define the averages, and give a significance test. Without this, the factor of about 3 is not yet a controlled, reproducible effect.

4. The stacking identification does not distinguish ABC stacking from other non-Bernal or mixed stackings, especially in thick flakes (Fig. 1g; Fig. 2b; Fig. S5). The y-axis of Fig. 1g is (1 − A_Bernal)/A_total, a non-Bernal fraction, but the text calls it "32% rhombohedral". IR contrast and sMIM separate Bernal from non-Bernal regions. For 10–20 layers, however, mixed sequences (for example ABCB, or ABC blocks separated by a buried twin or stacking fault) can give similar contrast, and sMIM was done only on selected regions. In the device, a buried stacking fault could leave the surface-polarized phases, and hence the uniform magnetization of Fig. 2c, largely intact. The capacitance argument ("no additional features") is indirect. The authors should state how many IR-identified domains in Fig. 1g were confirmed as ABC by Raman 2D lineshape, sMIM or a stacking-sensitive IR spectrum, as a function of thickness, preferably on a random subset. They should say whether the IR classification was done blind to the method. For the device, they should give a bulk-sensitive stacking check after nanofabrication, since the Fig. S5 IR images were taken after transfer, not after etching and annealing. A precise effective mass matching 13-layer ABC (concern 11) would also support uninterrupted ABC stacking.

5. The "~90% fabrication yield" in the abstract is not clearly defined and its comparisons are confounded (abstract; Fig. S4b; Methods, "Transfer statistics"). Fig. S4b defines yield as "the fraction of transfers in which RMG is successfully incorporated without relaxation to Bernal stacking". It is not stated how and when this was judged (IR after pickup, after full stack assembly, or after nanofabrication and cooldown). It is also not stated whether partial relaxation or a minimum retained area counts as success, or whether relaxation during later processing counts (etching, annealing, contact deposition, the 200 °C PC melt, the top-gate drop). "Fabrication yield" in the abstract implies finished devices. The cumulative 24/26 counts only transfers using all starred techniques, on flakes pre-screened by sMIM and aligned along crystallographic axes, with sub-samples of 12–36. The text says the yield rises "from ~30% to > 80% with statistics acquired over a sample of 24 and 62 devices", but these are transfers, not devices. The data come from seven people of different experience, and the techniques were adopted over time rather than assigned at random or interleaved. Learning-curve effects and simultaneous adoption of several steps therefore cannot be separated from the effect of each technique. Several individual comparisons are not statistically distinguishable at these sample sizes (LN2 18/24 vs 32/36; single sequence 13/17 vs 38/48; armchair/zigzag/arbitrary). The yield is lower below 10 layers (22/29), and the thinner multilayers used for chiral superconductivity are the most widely studied. The authors should define yield quantitatively and consistently, and report the end-to-end yield of measured devices with rhombohedral signatures, with post-fabrication IR or sMIM. They should report the cumulative yield separately for <10 and ≥10 layers. The claim that the cryo shock does not degrade transfer reliability is reasonable; claimed improvements from individual steps are not established.

6. The "size-driven crossover from Poiseuille to porous flow" (abstract; Fig. 4g–l) compares two different measurement geometries, not a width series. The channel data (Fig. 4g) are a four-terminal resistance in a 0.5 µm channel. The square data (Fig. 4j) are a bend-type resistance, with injection at the top corners and voltage across the bottom (Fig. 4k; Fig. S6 insets). That configuration is negative in the ballistic limit for reasons unrelated to viscosity (Fig. 4l; Fig. S9a), so the difference between Fig. 4g and 4j may reflect contact geometry as much as width. In the square, R_s rises monotonically from negative values. A crossover from ballistic bend resistance to ohmic transport driven only by decreasing ℓ_mr, with ℓ_ee playing no role, would give the same behaviour (compare Fig. S6c with the ℓ_mr axis of Fig. S9a). The authors themselves say R in the square is "insensitive to ℓ_ee" (Fig. 4l). The absence of a minimum in the square therefore does not by itself show porous flow, and with only two sizes there is no crossover to track. The comparison with simulation is only qualitative. The simulated ballistic channel resistance is about 210 Ω/sq and drops by about an order of magnitude (Fig. 4i). The measured Gurzhi dips are a few tens of Ω/sq on 100–400 Ω/sq, a 10–25% decrease (Fig. 4g). The authors should measure channels of several widths (for example 0.5, 1, 2 and 5 µm) in the same stack with identical contact geometry, or a local four-terminal configuration across the square. They should show that the dR/dT < 0 window and its amplitude scale with W/ℓ_G as predicted. Using Fig. S9a along a realistic (ℓ_ee(T), ℓ_mr(T)) trajectory, they should show whether the measured R_s(T) in the square requires small ℓ_ee. Magnitudes should be compared quantitatively with Fig. 4i/l using parameters from the same device. The authors should confirm that the channel and the square lie in the same rhombohedral domain and were measured in the same cooldown (Fig. S6a/b suggests the same device). If the separation of porous from ohmic flow rests on the imaging, the text should say so; otherwise it should present the crossover as consistent with, rather than established by, the data.

7. The assignment of the resistance minimum in the 0.5 µm channel to the Gurzhi effect (Fig. 4g–i) lacks the standard discriminating tests. The minimum near 15–30 K is shown at (n_e, D) points in the single-surface, spin/valley-polarized regime, where correlated phases are present (Fig. 2b). Melting of magnetic order, changing spin/valley fluctuations, or a phase boundary crossed with temperature could produce a non-monotonic R(T) without any hydrodynamics. The orange (2.5, 0.85) curve has a sharp kink near 7–10 K that looks more like a phase transition than a smooth viscous crossover. A ballistic-to-diffusive crossover with temperature-dependent boundary specularity is another candidate. The transport is also taken at (n, D) values different from those of the hydrodynamic image (6.5 × 10¹² cm⁻², 0.41 V/nm). Fig. 4i maps temperature to ℓ_ee only as a "proxy", so the comparison is qualitative. The authors should: show the perpendicular-field dependence in the channel (negative magnetoresistance from suppressed viscosity, with a characteristic field set by r_c ~ W); show the dR/dI-versus-current dependence, since Joule heating of the electron fluid should mirror the temperature dependence; measure at a density without symmetry breaking, for example the n_e = 10¹³ cm⁻², D = 0 point used for TMF, and show whether the minimum is absent where theory predicts it should be; measure the channel at the (n, D) of the nSOT image, or image at the transport points; map the (n, D, T) region where dR/dT < 0 to rule out a phase boundary; and give the implied ℓ_ee(T), checking it against the ℓ_ee ≈ 50 nm used elsewhere.

9. Electron heating during current imaging and transport is not discussed (Fig. 4a,c,e; Fig. 4g,j; TMF). The nSOT images use I = 10 µA at T ≈ 1.6 K. Electron–phonon cooling is weak in an ultraclean sample, and sheet resistances reach hundreds of Ω/sq in the narrow regions, plus contact resistance. The electron temperature could therefore rise well above the bath and strengthen e–e scattering, which would mimic a hydrodynamic profile. The rise could also differ between the high-density (ballistic) and flat-band (hydrodynamic) points. ℓ_ee depends strongly on T_e, so this bears directly on the hydrodynamic interpretation. The authors should show that the normalized flow profiles do not depend on current (for example 1–3, 5 and 10 µA) and estimate T_e from the dissipated power. They should state the excitation current, its frequency, and whether it is AC, for the imaging, the TMF and the R(T) transport.

11. The effective-mass result is presented as stronger than it is (Fig. 3i/j; Fig. S7; Methods Eq. 8). The text gives m* = 0.075 ± 0.05 m_e, an uncertainty of 67%, and calls the agreement with the tight-binding 0.078 m_e "excellent". 12. The claim that the cryogenic shock converts Bernal stacking to rhombohedral stacking through differential-contraction stress (Introduction; p. 3, "suggesting that the cryogenic shock induces a partial conversion") is untested and not separated from selection effects. Bulk natural graphite already contains 10–15% rhombohedral stacking. A cleaving process that yields larger, less fragmented flakes (Fig. 1g) could preserve more native ABC regions without any conversion. Other possibilities are that the shock changes where the crystal cleaves, preferentially extracting rhombohedral-rich regions, or that it reduces relaxation during peeling. The authors should add at least one discriminating control. Options: the same protocol with the LN2 step replaced by slow cooling, or heating without the quench; LN2 immersion without the 110 °C step; a varied quench temperature differential or rate; the rhombohedral fraction of the source crystal measured by XRD or Raman; examination of flakes left on the tape; and IR imaging of the same already-exfoliated flakes on chip before and after a shock cycle. Evidence of shear, such as partial dislocations, domain-wall density, or residual strain from Raman 2D/G mapping, would support the proposed mechanism. If the mechanism cannot be established, the text should soften "likely" and present conversion as one hypothesis among others.
