Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript introduces "cryogenic shock exfoliation" (tape plus SiO2/Si chip heated to 110 °C, then plunged into liquid nitrogen) and a low-pressure PC/PDMS micro-cavity pickup. Together these are reported to raise the non-Bernal area fraction of exfoliated multilayer graphene from about 12% to 32%, to give rhombohedral domains above 1000–1500 µm², and to reach a transfer yield of about 90% (24/26). Device quality is shown on one 13-layer dual-graphite-gated device: penetration-field capacitance, nanoSQUID-on-tip (nSOT) magnetization and current imaging, transverse magnetic focusing (TMF), and a Gurzhi-type resistance minimum in a 0.5 µm channel that is absent in a 10 µm square. If the yield gains hold, this is a useful materials advance for a fast-growing field, since scarce rhombohedral source material is a real bottleneck. The hydrodynamic imaging and the Poiseuille-to-porous comparison add physics interest. However, the device-quality claims currently rest on a single device, and some extracted figures of merit are stronger than the analysis supports.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The method is simple and cheap, and could be adopted quickly by many groups working on rhombohedral graphene and other metastable van der Waals stackings.
- Exfoliation is compared head-to-head on equal substrate areas (Fig. 1g,h; Fig. S2), and transfer yields are broken down by technique with explicit counts (Fig. S4b).
- Several independent probes are applied to the main device: global capacitance (Fig. 2b), nSOT magnetization imaging (Fig. 2c), TMF on all four edges with up to five boundary reflections (Fig. 3c), negative bend resistance (Fig. S6), and spatially resolved current flow (Fig. 4a,c,e).
- The direct current-flow images of the ballistic, near-neutrality and flat-band regimes, compared with Boltzmann simulations (Fig. 4, Fig. S8), are an attractive demonstration in their own right.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. All device-quality evidence comes from a single device. The capacitance map (Fig. 2b), nSOT magnetization (Fig. 2c), TMF (Fig. 3), current imaging (Fig. 4a–f), the channel and square transport (Fig. 4g,j) and Fig. S6 all appear to come from one 13-layer device. Fig. S5 shows four more completed devices (660–1330 µm²), but only as optical and IR images. Yet the abstract and conclusion claim that the approach "enable[s] highly uniform devices" and moves "standard metrics of RMG device quality—area, yield, and uniformity—into a qualitatively new regime."
   - Why it matters: a yield or reproducibility claim cannot rest on one device. It is also unclear whether the device in Figs. 2–4 is one of the largest ones (it is the 740 µm² device of Fig. S5a–c), or whether its central 10 × 10 µm² square samples only a small part of the rhombohedral area.
   - What to do: show at least basic transport for the other Fig. S5 devices, measured at the same (n_e, D) where possible. Useful quantities are R(n_e, D) phase diagrams showing the known spin/valley-ordered features, TMF or negative bend resistance, and a mobility or quantum-mobility estimate (onset field of Shubnikov–de Haas oscillations). Report the device-to-device spread.

2. The headline "ℓ_mfp > 200 µm" is not determined by the analysis used.
   - How it is extracted: Eq. (5) uses ℓ_mfp = −(πd/2)/ln[A(T)/A_0], with d ≈ 5 µm and A_0 the average of the four lowest-temperature points (Fig. S7b). This forces ℓ_mfp → ∞ at base temperature by construction.
   - The arithmetic: ℓ = 200 µm corresponds to A/A_0 ≈ exp(−7.85/200) ≈ 0.96, a 4% drop in peak area. The value quoted below 30 K is therefore set by percent-level changes in A_peak. Those changes are comparable to the scatter visible in Fig. S7b, and to changes from temperature-dependent background subtraction (linear Hall term, the window limits in Fig. 3e–h) or from the onset of thermal broadening of the lineshape.
   - The Methods concede the problem: the analysis "is not well suited to determine the mean-free path at lowest temperatures" and "we can only note that ℓ_0 ≥ 200 µm." Neither caveat appears in the abstract or main text.
   - Fig. 3j has no error bars.
   - Specular boundary reflection (p = 0.5 in the simulations) and diffuse loss at the edge between the contacts are not included in W(E,T).
   - What to do:
     - Give error bars on ℓ_mfp(T), propagated from the uncertainty in A_peak, including the background-subtraction and integration-window choices.
     - State the lowest temperature at which ℓ_mfp is actually resolved rather than bounded.
     - Restate the abstract claim as a lower bound together with the method's sensitivity limit.
     - Independently, cross-check the low-temperature value using the decay of successive focusing peaks (1R…6R in Fig. 3c), the temperature of the bend-resistance sign change (Fig. S6c), or Boltzmann simulations with varying ℓ_mr fitted to the TMF peak series.
   - Also, "comparable to the mean free paths reported for the best-in-class mono- and bilayer graphene devices [44–46]" compares quantities obtained by different methods and at different densities. State how those values were obtained, or compare mobilities.

3. The exfoliation statistics (Fig. 1g,h) do not yet establish that the shock itself raises the rhombohedral fraction, rather than flake selection.
   - Missing counts and the thickness confound: the numbers of flakes, substrates and source crystals per method are not stated. Fig. 1g plots non-Bernal fraction against flake area, and the two populations occupy different flake-size ranges (standard flakes are mostly near 10³ µm²). Rhombohedral content is known to depend on thickness, and flakes from 3 to ~20 layers are pooled, so the comparison may be confounded by different thickness distributions.
   - Exclusion criteria: these rules (cracks, wrinkles, dirt, aspect ratio, IR-visible micro-domains, areas < 80 µm²; Fig. S2b) are said to introduce "approximately equal systematic error" for both methods. A thermal shock plausibly creates more cracks and wrinkles, so that assumption needs to be checked. Report the excluded area fraction for each method.
   - Classification: classification is by IR contrast, and the y-axis of Fig. 1g is (1 − A_Bernal)/A_total. The text calls this "32% rhombohedral". Non-Bernal regions can also be mixed or partially rhombohedral stackings (for example ABCB or twin boundaries), and sMIM was done only on selected regions. State how many IR-identified domains were confirmed as rhombohedral by Raman or sMIM, and whether the IR classification was done blind to the method.
   - Mechanism: the claimed conversion of Bernal to rhombohedral stacking via differential-contraction stress is untested. A control that heats without the LN2 quench, or that varies the quench temperature differential or rate, would separate the thermal-shock effect from other changes in protocol. Even better, the same flakes could be imaged before and after a shock cycle.
   - What to do: give the counts, the thickness distributions and fractions binned by layer number, and at least one mechanism control.

B. Should address

4. The "~90% fabrication yield" in the abstract needs clearer definition and controls (Fig. S4b, Methods "Transfer statistics").
   - What yield means: it is "the fraction of transfers in which RMG is successfully incorporated without relaxation to Bernal stacking." Say when and how this was judged (IR after pickup, after full stack assembly, or after nanofabrication and cooldown), and whether relaxation during later etching, annealing or contact deposition is counted. "Fabrication yield" in the abstract implies finished devices.
   - Small, overlapping samples: the cumulative 24/26 comes from flakes pre-screened by sMIM and aligned along crystallographic axes, with sub-samples of 12–36. The techniques were adopted over time by seven people with different levels of experience, so learning-curve effects and simultaneous adoption of several steps cannot be separated from the effect of each technique.
   - What to do: give binomial confidence intervals, indicate which transfers overlap between bars, and, if possible, give the order in which the techniques were introduced. Report stacking stability after thermal cycling and over storage time.

5. The uniformity claim from capacitance and nSOT needs a detection threshold.
   - Fig. 2b: the statement that "no additional features are observed" in C_P(n_e, D) does not say what Bernal area fraction would have produced a visible feature. Estimate this, for example by comparing with a Bernal 13-layer region, which Fig. S5b marks as present in the same flake, or with a simple area-weighted model.
   - Fig. 2c: the magnetization is mapped at a single (n_e, D) point. Say how strongly a Bernal-stacked or relaxed patch of a given size would show up there; a non-magnetic patch below the ~300 nm tip resolution would be missed.
   - Scope: the abstract's "uniform spin magnetism over the full central 10 × 10 µm² area of our devices" refers to one device and 100 µm² out of 740 µm².

6. The effective-mass fit and its stated precision are inconsistent.
   - The text quotes m* = 0.075 ± 0.05 m_e. That is a 67% uncertainty, yet it is called "excellent agreement" with 0.078 m_e from tight binding. If the uncertainty is really ±0.005, correct it.
   - The model behind the fit: the thermal-broadening model (Eq. 8) assumes a parabolic band and a Lorentzian kernel, but the Lorentzian visibly misfits the tails of the low-T peak (Fig. S7a). The black curve in Fig. S7b also deviates systematically from the data between 50 and 150 K.
   - The comparison calculation: the tight-binding calculation omits any interlayer potential "as the measurements were carried out at zero displacement field." At n_e = 10¹³ cm⁻² in a 13-layer flake, however, screening puts the carriers on both surfaces, and a self-consistent Hartree potential will change the surface-band dispersion even at D = 0. The 4% agreement may therefore be partly coincidental.
   - What to do: show the fit residuals and the sensitivity of m* to the kernel shape, and comment on the electrostatics.

7. The Gurzhi-minimum assignment (Fig. 4g) needs alternative explanations ruled out.
   - The concern: the minimum near 15–30 K is shown at (n_e, D) points in the single-surface, spin/valley-polarized regimes. Melting of magnetic order, or changing spin/valley fluctuations, could produce a non-monotonic R(T) there with no hydrodynamics involved. Ballistic-to-diffusive crossovers with temperature-dependent boundary specularity in a 0.5 µm channel are another candidate.
   - What to do: the standard discriminating checks are:
     - the dependence of the minimum on channel width, ideally in more than one channel width;
     - the dR/dI-versus-current dependence (Joule heating of the electron fluid should mirror the temperature dependence);
     - the same measurement at a density without symmetry breaking, for example the n_e = 10¹³ cm⁻², D = 0 point used for TMF.
   - Quantitative comparison: Fig. 4i maps temperature to ℓ_ee only as a "proxy", with no quantitative comparison. Give the implied ℓ_ee(T) and check it against the ℓ_ee ≈ 50 nm used elsewhere.

8. The Poiseuille-versus-porous contrast between the channel and the square is not unique to hydrodynamics. In the 10 µm square (Fig. 4j), R_s rises monotonically from negative values. A crossover from ballistic bend resistance to ohmic transport, driven only by decreasing ℓ_mr with ℓ_ee playing no role, would also give this (compare Fig. S6c and the ℓ_mr axis of Fig. S9a). The absence of a minimum in the square therefore does not by itself show porous flow. The authors should show, using the Fig. S9a map along a realistic (ℓ_ee(T), ℓ_mr(T)) trajectory, that the measured R_s(T) in the square requires small ℓ_ee. They should also confirm that the channel and the square are in the same rhombohedral domain and were measured in the same cooldown.

9. The hydrodynamic and diffusive assignments of the current images (Fig. 4c–f, Fig. S8) depend on parameters that are not independently constrained.
   - The parameters: ℓ_ee = 50 nm is imported from bilayer graphene [25], a circular Fermi surface is assumed (the inset of Fig. 3a and the flat surface bands are not circular), the boundary specularity p = 0.5 is chosen empirically, and ℓ_mr is set by visual comparison.
   - What is missing: a goodness-of-fit measure, and a demonstration that other combinations (large ℓ_ee with an anisotropic Fermi surface, or different p) fail. Fig. S8k shows only "best match" curves.
   - Measurement conditions: a short ℓ_ee at T ≈ 1.6 K is unusual for a degenerate Fermi liquid and calls for discussion. The images use I = 10 µA, so show that the flow profile does not depend on current (electron heating). A temperature series of the Fig. 4e profile would directly test the hydrodynamic interpretation.

10. Residual strain and defects from the thermal shock are not characterized. A rapid ~300 K quench that is proposed to convert stacking by stress could also leave strain, folds or point defects. Raman 2D-peak positions or widths for shock-exfoliated versus standard flakes (or sMIM/AFM statistics on wrinkle density) would show whether the shock affects anything beyond stacking order. The argument from the excellent single-device transport is limited by concern 1.

C. Minor

11. The Fig. 3 caption and the main text do not match the panels. The caption assigns (i) to ℓ_mfp(T) and (j) to V_peak/A_peak. In the figure, panel j shows ℓ_mfp(T) and panel i shows V_peak/A_peak. The text cites "Fig. 3i" for ℓ_mfp and "Fig. 3j" for m*. Also, "First TMF peak for at T = 2.0 K" contains a typo.

12. Methods Eq. (5) uses V_area(T)/V_area,0, while Eqs. (3)–(4) use A(T) and A_0. Use one notation. State the collector width w, so that the condition k_BT/E_F < w/d can be checked up to the highest temperatures used (k_BT/E_F ≈ 0.13 at 250 K).

13. Matthiessen fit: interpret α = 1.56 ± 0.05 physically (phonon versus electron-electron contributions), or present it as purely phenomenological. "Extrapolating to ℓ_mfp(300 K) ≈ 2 µm" is barely an extrapolation, since the data reach about 270 K.

14. The tight-binding parameters use sign conventions (γ_3 = −290 meV, γ_4 = −210 meV, γ_2 = −22 meV) that differ from common literature choices. Cite the parameter source and state the convention.

15. "Exceeding maximum sizes of rhombohedral devices in the literature by at least a factor of two" (p. 4) should cite the specific prior device areas being compared.

16. Fig. 1g caption: state the number of flakes per method. Fig. 1h: state the bin width and whether the ">1500" bar is an overflow bin.

17. Fig. 2c colour bar: give the units of M (µ_B per unit cell?), and comment on whether the absolute value is consistent with a full spin polarization of the measured density.

18. The text says yield rises "from ~30% to > 80% with statistics acquired over a sample of 24 and 62 devices". These are transfers, not devices (Fig. S4b). Keep the terminology consistent throughout.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a compact table or figure covering all completed devices in Fig. S5: layer number, rhombohedral area, whether stacking survived fabrication and cooldown, and one transport metric (mobility, TMF visibility or bend resistance). This would turn the single-device demonstration into a reproducibility claim.
2. Replace or supplement the area-ratio ℓ_mfp extraction with a method that is sensitive at ℓ ≫ d. Examples are Boltzmann fits to the full multi-reflection TMF spectrum, or longer-path focusing geometries. Report the result with uncertainties.
3. Add a mechanism control for the cryogenic shock (no quench, or a varied quench differential) and report the non-Bernal fraction binned by layer number, with Raman or sMIM confirmation on a random subset of IR-classified domains.
4. Strengthen the hydrodynamic section with current-dependence and channel-width-dependence of the Gurzhi minimum, and with a temperature series of the nSOT flow profiles.
