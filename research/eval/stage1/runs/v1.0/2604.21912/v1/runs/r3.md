Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper presents "cryogenic shock exfoliation": tape and chip are heated to 110 °C and then plunged into liquid nitrogen. The authors report that this raises the average non-Bernal (rhombohedral) area fraction of exfoliated multilayer graphene from about 12% to 32% and produces contiguous rhombohedral domains larger than 1500 µm². Combined with a low-pressure PC-membrane transfer, it gives a reported transfer yield of about 90% (24/26). The quality of a 13-layer device is then examined with capacitance, nSOT magnetization imaging, transverse magnetic focusing (TMF) and nSOT current imaging. The authors claim a mean free path above 200 µm and a size-driven crossover from Poiseuille to porous hydrodynamic flow. A simple, reproducible route to large rhombohedral multilayer graphene (RMG) would matter to a large community. The fabrication statistics are the most novel and useful part. The transport claims, as currently argued, depend on a normalization and on parameter choices that the paper does not yet justify.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The method is simple, cheap and easy to adopt. It addresses a well-known bottleneck: rhombohedral domains are scarce and small.
- The exfoliation comparison uses equal substrate areas and stated inclusion and exclusion criteria (Methods, Fig. S2). Transfer yields are broken down by technique, with counts (Fig. S4b).
- Several independent probes are applied to the same device: penetration-field capacitance (Fig. 2b), magnetization imaging over the whole 10 × 10 µm² square (Fig. 2c), focusing with up to six peaks on all four edges (Fig. 3c), and negative bend resistance up to about 100 K (Fig. S6c).
- Current-density images in three regimes (Fig. 4a,c,e) are visually striking and are compared with a kinetic-equation solver.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. **The headline "disorder mean free path exceeding 200 µm" does not follow from the TMF analysis as constructed.**
   - Where: abstract, Fig. 3j (labelled i in the text), Methods Eqs. (3)–(6).
   - The extraction normalizes the peak area to its low-temperature limit A0 (Eq. 5: ℓmfp = −(πd/2)/ln[A(T)/A0]). Any temperature-independent scattering, including the disorder scattering the abstract refers to, is therefore divided out. By construction, ℓmfp → ∞ as T → 0, whatever the actual disorder level.
   - What Eq. (5) returns is the temperature-dependent part of the scattering rate. Fitting it to Matthiessen's rule with a finite ℓ0 is then internally inconsistent: ℓ0 should be unconstrained.
   - The ballistic path for the first focus is πd/2 ≈ 8 µm. ℓ = 200 µm corresponds to A/A0 ≈ 0.96, a 4% change that is comparable to the uncertainty from the linear baseline choice (Fig. 3e–h) and the saturation level (Fig. S7).
   - The multiple focusing peaks (1R–6R) all have roughly the same total path length (about πd/2). They show boundary specularity, not a mean free path far above 8 µm. The negative bend resistance (Fig. S6c) supports ℓ of order 10 µm.
   - What to do: obtain an absolute rather than self-normalized bound on ℓ0. Options include:
     - compare the absolute low-temperature focusing amplitude, or the 1R/2R/… peak ratios, with the Boltzmann simulation (Fig. 3b) as a function of ℓmr, with p fixed independently;
     - map the measured low-T negative square resistance (about −6.5 Ω, Fig. S6c) onto the simulated R(ℓmr, ℓee) map (Fig. S9a);
     - use focusing over several contact separations.
   - Otherwise, reword the abstract and main text to state that only the temperature-dependent scattering length exceeds 200 µm below 30 K. Also note that the comparison with "best-in-class" mono- and bilayer mean free paths [44–46] is then not like for like.

2. **The device-quality and uniformity claims rest on a single device, and the largest devices are not characterized at all.**
   - Where: abstract ("highly uniform devices exceeding 1300 µm²"), Figs. 2–4, Fig. S5.
   - All capacitance, nSOT, TMF and hydrodynamic data come from one 13-layer device, measured within a 10 × 10 µm² (100 µm²) square of a 740 µm² rhombohedral region.
   - The 1330, 1080 and 1070 µm² devices in Fig. S5 are supported only by IR images taken after transfer. Their IR images were not repeated after nanofabrication, and no transport or capacitance data are shown for them.
   - The title and abstract present the method as enabling ultrahigh-mobility devices in general. One device cannot show that the quality is a property of the method rather than of one good flake, nor give the device-to-device spread.
   - What to do: show at least TMF or bend resistance, and a capacitance phase diagram, for one or two more devices, ideally including a >1000 µm² device and a thinner (<10-layer) one. If that is not possible, narrow the claims to the one device.

3. **The "size-driven crossover from Poiseuille to porous flow" compares two different measurement geometries, not a width series.**
   - Where: abstract, Fig. 4g–l.
   - The channel data (Fig. 4g) are a four-terminal channel resistance at 0.5 µm width. The square data (Fig. 4j) are a bend-type resistance in a 10 µm square, which is negative in the ballistic limit for reasons unrelated to viscosity (Fig. 4l, Fig. S9a).
   - A monotonic R(T) in the square is therefore expected from the change of geometry alone. The comparison does not isolate the role of width at fixed contact configuration.
   - With only two sizes there is no crossover to track. The paper also does not say explicitly whether the channel and the square belong to the same device and stack region; Fig. S6a/b suggests they do.
   - The comparison with simulation is only qualitative:
     - the simulated ballistic channel resistance is about 210 Ω/sq and drops by an order of magnitude (Fig. 4i);
     - the measured Gurzhi dips are a few tens of Ω/sq on 100–400 Ω/sq (Fig. 4g).
   - Correlated phases are present in this part of the (n, D) map (Fig. 2b). A phase boundary crossed with temperature could also produce a resistance minimum; the orange (2.5, 0.85) curve has a sharp feature near 10 K.
   - What to do:
     - Measure channels of several widths (for example 0.5, 1, 2 and 5 µm) in the same stack, with identical contact geometry, and show that the dR/dT < 0 window and its amplitude scale with W/ℓG as predicted.
     - Show the density dependence of the minimum away from phase boundaries.
     - Compare magnitudes quantitatively with the simulations of Fig. 4i/l, using parameters taken from the same device.

B. Should address

4. **ℓee = 50 nm is imposed rather than extracted, and it conflicts with the low-temperature transport in the same regime.**
   - Where: Fig. 4e,f, Fig. S8, Methods ("For the spatial simulations we use this value").
   - ℓee = 50 nm at 1.6 K is taken from bilayer graphene [25]. It is not fitted to this device. Fig. S8i shows that y_max(x) barely distinguishes ℓee = 1 µm from 100 nm or 50 nm at ℓmr = 200 µm, so the images do not constrain ℓee well.
   - Fig. 4j (purple, (4, 0.51), same single-surface regime) shows a negative square resistance at the lowest temperatures. By Fig. 4l and Fig. S9a, that requires ℓee of micrometres or more. This contradicts ℓee = 50 nm at 1.6 K for the nearby point (6.5, 0.41) imaged in Fig. 4e. The text itself says ee scattering should be weakest at the largest n.
   - Fig. 4c is described as "near charge neutrality … dominated by electron–hole collisions". At n = 0.6 × 10¹² cm⁻² and T = 1.6 K, the density of thermally excited carriers needed for strong electron–hole scattering should be estimated, not assumed.
   - What to do:
     - Fit the images with ℓee and ℓmr both free and report confidence intervals.
     - Show nSOT current images as a function of temperature at fixed (n, D); this is the direct test that the bulk-concentrated profile comes from ee collisions.
     - Report the square resistance at exactly the (n, D) of Fig. 4e and reconcile it with the imaging parameters.

5. **Possible electron heating during current imaging.**
   - Where: Fig. 4a,c,e, which use a 10 µA current at T ≈ 1.6 K.
   - In an ultraclean sample, electron–phonon cooling is weak. A current this size could raise the electron temperature well above the bath and strengthen ee scattering, which would mimic a hydrodynamic profile. It could also differ between the high-density (ballistic) and flat-band (hydrodynamic) points.
   - What to do:
     - Show that the normalized current profiles are independent of current (for example 1, 3 and 10 µA).
     - Estimate T_e from the dissipated power.
     - State the excitation frequency and whether the current is AC.
     - Give the same information for the TMF and R(T) measurements.

6. **The exfoliation statistics need controls for thickness, source and identification.**
   - Where: Fig. 1g,h, Methods.
   - The rhombohedral fraction is known to depend on layer number. Flakes from 3 to about 20 layers are pooled, and the thickness distributions of the two methods are not shown. Cryo-shock flakes are also systematically larger (Fig. 1g).
   - The following are not given: the number of bulk crystals, the number of independent exfoliation runs and chips per method, and whether both methods used the same crystals.
   - The y-axis of Fig. 1g is (1 − A_Bernal)/A_total, a non-Bernal fraction, but the text calls it the "rhombohedral" fraction. IR contrast and sMIM do not obviously distinguish ABC from mixed stacking (for example ABCB) in thick flakes.
   - The ovals show ±1 standard deviation, with no statistical test. It is also not stated whether the quoted averages of 1200 and 3100 µm² are geometric or arithmetic means.
   - What to do:
     - Provide thickness-resolved comparisons.
     - Report crystal and run counts and the run-to-run spread.
     - Confirm the stacking assignment by Raman 2D lineshape on a subset.
     - Give a significance test.

7. **The mechanism ("partial conversion of Bernal to rhombohedral stacking") is not separated from selection effects.**
   - Where: p. 3.
   - The data are equally consistent with alternatives:
     - the thermal shock changes where the crystal cleaves, preferentially extracting regions already rich in rhombohedral stacking;
     - the shock reduces relaxation during peeling rather than inducing conversion.
   - What to do: since the word "likely" carries weight here, either soften the claim or add a discriminating control. Candidates:
     - shock-treat standard flakes already on the substrate and image them by IR before and after;
     - examine the flakes left on the tape;
     - run LN₂ immersion without the 110 °C step, and heating without the LN₂ step.

8. **Yield definition and possible confounds.**
   - Where: Fig. S4b, Methods.
   - "Yield" counts transfers in which RMG is incorporated "without relaxation". It is not stated:
     - how that is judged (IR after transfer? any surviving area? a minimum retained area?);
     - whether partial relaxation counts as success;
     - whether stacking survives nanofabrication and cooldown.
   - The statistics come from seven co-authors of varying experience, apparently not interleaved in time, so a learning curve may confound the comparison between techniques.
   - Yield is lower below 10 layers (22/29), and the thin multilayers used for chiral superconductivity are the most widely studied.
   - What to do: define success quantitatively, report post-fabrication IR or sMIM on finished devices, give chronological or per-operator breakdowns, and report the cumulative yield separately for <10 and ≥10 layers.

9. **The sensitivity of the uniformity probes is not quantified.**
   - Where: Fig. 2b,c.
   - "No additional features" in the area-averaged capacitance supports uniformity only if one knows what a Bernal inclusion or a density gradient would add.
   - The magnetization map is taken at a single (n, D) point and gate modulation.
   - What to do:
     - Estimate the smallest Bernal area fraction, and the density or displacement-field inhomogeneity, that would be visible in Cp. For example, give the widths of the phase boundaries in Fig. 2b expressed as δn.
     - Quantify the spatial spread of M in Fig. 2c, excluding residue regions, and convert it into a bound on local δn using the dM/dn at the boundary.
     - Ideally, add one more (n, D) point or a gate-dependent map.

10. **The effective-mass fit is presented as stronger than it is.**
   - Where: Fig. 3 (panel i/j), Fig. S7b, Methods Eq. (8).
   - The quoted m* = 0.075 ± 0.05 mₑ has a 67% uncertainty, which makes "excellent agreement" with 0.078 mₑ uninformative. If the uncertainty should read ±0.005, please correct it and explain how it was obtained.
   - The model assumes parabolic bands at E_F = 160 meV, while Fig. 3a shows a visibly non-parabolic, warped band. The fit in Fig. S7b also deviates systematically between about 50 and 150 K.
   - The condition k_BT/E_F < w/d is invoked without giving the collector width w.
   - What to do: correct or justify the uncertainty, use the tight-binding dispersion directly in Eq. (2), and state w.

11. **Simulation assumptions affect the ballistic comparison.**
   - Where: Fig. 4a,b, Fig. 3b, Methods.
   - The kinetic-equation solver uses an isotropic Fermi surface. Ballistic jets and caustics depend strongly on Fermi-surface shape, and Fig. 3a (inset) shows clear trigonal warping at n = 10¹³ cm⁻².
   - The boundary specularity p = 0.5 is said to be chosen from the decay of the focusing peaks, but that analysis is not shown.
   - What to do: show the p determination, and either include warping for the ballistic comparison or state explicitly that Fig. 4a–b is only a qualitative comparison.

C. Minor

12. Fig. 3 panel labels disagree with the caption and text. In the figure, "j" labels the ℓmfp plot and "i" the Vpeak/Apeak plot; the caption and main text use the opposite assignment.
13. Methods, before Eq. (3): W(E,T) ≈ exp(πd/(2ℓmfp)) should carry a minus sign. Eq. (5) uses "V_area" where the rest of the paper uses A_peak.
14. Fig. S8: panel (f) uses ℓmr = 10 µm, but panel (k) states that the best match is ℓmr = 20 µm (orange), while the main text gives ℓmr ≈ 10 µm. Please reconcile. The Methods also cite "Fig. S8h,g" in reverse order.
15. Methods describe nSOT magnetization reconstruction but not how the current density is reconstructed from B_z. Please describe it: tip height, regularization, and treatment of the contacts.
16. Fig. 1h: the meaning of the arrows labelled "130" and "77", and of the ">1500" bin, should be stated in the caption. The 80 µm² inclusion threshold also does not match the histogram's apparent lower edge of about 150–200 µm².
17. Stability of the metastable stacking over time and thermal cycles is not reported. Please state whether any device relaxed after cooldown or fabrication.
18. Typos and editorial: "First TMF peak for at T = 2.0 K"; "Hexagonal Boron Nitride" capitalization; reference [22] lacks journal information. Add a data and code availability statement; the solver is cited only via [61].

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Replace the self-normalized ℓmfp with an absolute bound: fit the absolute TMF amplitude and peak ratios, and the low-T negative bend resistance, to the existing Boltzmann solver with p fixed independently.
2. Add a width series of channels (several widths, same contact geometry) in one stack, plus temperature-dependent nSOT current images. Together these would turn the hydrodynamic section from a qualitative illustration into a quantitative test.
3. Add transport or capacitance data for at least one more device, preferably a >1000 µm² device and a thin (<10-layer) one, to show that the quality comes from the method.
4. Strengthen the exfoliation statistics with thickness-resolved comparisons, crystal and run counts, Raman confirmation of the stacking assignment, and one control that separates shock-induced conversion from selection.
