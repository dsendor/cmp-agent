Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript introduces "cryogenic shock exfoliation": heating tape and an SiO2/Si chip to 110 C and then plunging them into liquid nitrogen. The authors report that this raises the non-Bernal (rhombohedral) area fraction of exfoliated multilayer graphene from about 12% to 32% and produces single-stacking domains above 1000 µm². They combine it with a low-pressure PC/PDMS micro-cavity transfer that reaches about 90% transfer yield. To show device quality, they use a 13-layer dual-graphite-gated device with nanoSQUID-on-tip (nSOT) magnetization imaging, transverse magnetic focusing (TMF) and Biot–Savart current imaging, plus a comparison between a narrow channel and a square that they interpret as a Poiseuille-to-porous hydrodynamic crossover. Rhombohedral graphene is limited by materials, so a reproducible route to large, uniform domains would matter to a large community. The technique is the main contribution. The transport physics is presented as evidence of quality and does not stand as an independent result.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The technique is simple and cheap and could be adopted widely. It addresses a recognized bottleneck: small and scarce ABC domains, and their relaxation during transfer.
- Exfoliation statistics are given for both methods over equal substrate area, with explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria (Methods; Fig. S2). The domain-size distribution (Fig. 1h) shows a tail beyond 1000–1500 µm² that standard exfoliation does not produce.
- The transfer yield is broken down by technique (Fig. S4b). The cumulative 24/26 is a useful practical benchmark.
- The device-level evidence is unusually rich. A full capacitance phase diagram shows no extra features (Fig. 2b). The nSOT magnetization map is spatially uniform over 10 × 10 µm² (Fig. 2c). TMF shows up to six focusing peaks on all four edges (Fig. 3c), and negative bend resistance persists to about 150 K (Fig. S6c). Current imaging shows qualitatively distinct flow profiles in three regimes (Fig. 4a,c,e).
- The Methods give enough detail on the transfer recipe and simulations to be reproduced, and the simulation code is cited.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. All electronic and magnetic evidence of device quality comes from one device. The abstract and introduction present uniform magnetism, ℓ_mfp > 200 µm and the hydrodynamic crossover as properties that the method enables. However, the capacitance map (Fig. 2b), the nSOT image (Fig. 2c), TMF (Fig. 3), current imaging (Fig. 4a–f) and channel transport (Fig. 4g,j; Fig. S6) all appear to come from the single 13-layer device of Fig. 2a / Fig. S5a–c. The other devices in Fig. S5, including the 1330 µm² device cited in the abstract ("devices with areas exceeding 1300 µm²"), are shown only as optical and IR images, with no measurement. One device shows that such a device can exist, not that the process "enables highly uniform devices" at a ~90% yield. This matters most because device quality, rather than domain area, is the quality the community cares about.
   - The authors should report at least a basic quality metric for several cryo-shock devices: for example, a capacitance (n, D) map with sharp features, or low-temperature bend resistance or TMF.
   - Ideally they should include the >1300 µm² device and a matched device made from a standard-exfoliated flake processed identically. That comparison would show that the cryogenic shock does not degrade, or does improve, disorder.
   - If no other devices were measured, the abstract and conclusion should say clearly that the transport and imaging come from one device.

2. The headline ℓ_mfp > 200 µm rests on a very small change in peak area, and no uncertainty is given (Fig. 3j, Eq. 5, Fig. S7b). By Eq. 5, ℓ_mfp = −(πd/2)/ln(A/A0). With d ≈ 5 µm, ℓ_mfp = 200 µm corresponds to A/A0 ≈ 0.96, a 4% drop in integrated peak area. Moreover, A0 is defined as the average of the four lowest-temperature points (Fig. S7b caption). Below ~30 K, the extracted ℓ_mfp is therefore set by percent-level changes in a quantity that also depends on several choices:
   - the integration window (dashed lines in Fig. 3e–h);
   - the linear baseline under the peak (shaded region), whose shape clearly changes between 2 K and 198 K;
   - the linear-in-B Hall subtraction.
   The paper itself says the method "is not well suited to determine the mean-free path at lowest temperatures". Yet ">200 µm" is in the abstract and is compared with best-in-class mono- and bilayer graphene (Refs. 44–46), which used different extraction methods. Eq. 5 also assumes that the only T-dependent loss is bulk scattering along a semicircular path of length πd/2. That neglects T-dependent boundary specularity and contact transmission, and possible broadening of K(B − B_f) beyond the integration window at higher T.
   The authors should:
   - add error bars to Fig. 3j that propagate the noise in A_peak and the uncertainty in A0;
   - show that the result is robust to the choice of integration window and baseline;
   - state which T range enters the Matthiessen fit and whether ℓ0 is free or fixed. The quoted α = 1.56 ± 0.05 depends on this.
   If the low-T value is only a lower bound, the abstract should state it as such and give the bound's confidence. Agreement with the bend-resistance sign change (Fig. S6c) at the T where ℓ_mfp ≈ 10 µm is a useful independent check; it could be stated quantitatively.

3. The exfoliation comparison (Fig. 1g,h) does not say how many independent runs and crystals it contains, or whether flake thickness was matched. The central claim is that cryogenic shock raises the rhombohedral fraction from 12% to 32% and the mean flake area from 1200 to 3100 µm². The Methods state only that "the same total Si substrate area" was compared. A reader cannot tell:
   - how many exfoliation runs, chips and tape preparations contribute to each population;
   - whether both used the same bulk graphite crystal, and its source (natural, Kish or HOPG);
   - how the layer-number distribution (included range 3–~20 layers) compares between methods;
   - how much the result varies from run to run.
   Because the rhombohedral fraction may depend on thickness and flake size, a difference in thickness or size distributions alone could shift the averages.
   The authors should:
   - give N (flakes, runs, crystals) for both methods, and the per-run fraction with its spread;
   - show the rhombohedral fraction against layer number for both methods;
   - compare size-matched subsets. For example, in Fig. 1g, is the fraction for cryo flakes of ~1000 µm² higher than for standard flakes of the same size?
   - say whether the 12%/32% values are per-flake arithmetic means or area-weighted, and how Bernal-only flakes plotted at zero are handled.
   Without this, the factor of ~3 is not yet a controlled, reproducible effect.

B. Should address

4. The conclusion that cryogenic shock converts Bernal stacking to rhombohedral stacking is not separated from the alternative that it selects for native rhombohedral regions (Introduction; p. 3: "suggesting that the cryogenic shock induces a partial conversion"). Bulk natural graphite already contains 10–15% rhombohedral stacking. A cleaving process that yields larger, less fragmented flakes, as Fig. 1g shows, could preserve more native ABC regions and larger domains without any conversion. Controls that would separate the two explanations:
   - the same protocol with the LN2 step replaced by slow cooling, or with a smaller ΔT (no heating, or a different bath temperature);
   - the rhombohedral fraction of the source crystal measured by XRD or Raman;
   - if possible, IR imaging of the same flake region before and after a thermal shock applied to already-exfoliated flakes on chip.
   If conversion is real, evidence of shear (partial dislocations, domain-wall density, residual strain by Raman 2D/G mapping) would support the proposed mechanism. If the mechanism cannot be established, the text should present conversion as one hypothesis among others.

5. The stacking identification does not distinguish ABC stacking from other non-Bernal or mixed stacking, particularly for thick flakes. The y-axis of Fig. 1g is (1 − A_Bernal)/A_total, so any non-ABA region counts as "rhombohedral". IR contrast and sMIM separate Bernal from non-Bernal regions. However, for 10–20 layers, mixed sequences (for example ABCB, or ABC blocks separated by a buried twin or stacking fault) can give similar IR and sMIM contrast. For the measured device, a buried stacking fault could leave the surface-polarized phases, and hence the uniform nSOT magnetization of Fig. 2c, largely intact. The capacitance argument in Fig. 2b ("no additional features") is indirect.
   The authors should:
   - state what fraction of the flakes counted in Fig. 1g were confirmed as ABC by Raman 2D lineshape, or by a stacking-sensitive IR spectrum, as a function of thickness;
   - for the device, give a bulk-sensitive check after nanofabrication. The IR images in Fig. S5 are taken after transfer, not after etching and annealing.
   - note that the agreement between the TMF effective mass and the 13-layer tight-binding value would support uninterrupted ABC stacking if the uncertainty were smaller (see concern 9).

6. In the square, ℓ_ee is fixed from bilayer literature rather than constrained by the data, and the simulations show that the current images are only weakly sensitive to it. The hydrodynamic interpretation of Fig. 4e,f uses ℓ_ee = 50 nm, "recently found ... in bilayer graphene [25]", together with ℓ_mr = 200 µm taken from TMF. The TMF value was measured at a different state (n = 10^13 cm^-2, D = 0) from the image (n = 6.5 × 10^12 cm^-2, D = 0.41 V/nm). Fig. S8i, however, shows that the y_max(x) profile barely changes for ℓ_ee from 10 nm to about 10 µm at ℓ_mr = 200 µm. Fig. S8j shows that it depends mainly on ℓ_mr, and the experimental y_max in Fig. S8k has wide shaded uncertainty bands. The images therefore pin ℓ_mr more than ℓ_ee. They do not obviously exclude a ballistic, or ballistic-with-weaker-ℓ_mr, description at 1.6 K.
   The authors should:
   - fit ℓ_ee and ℓ_mr jointly to the measured |J| maps, using the full 2D profile rather than y_max only, and report confidence intervals;
   - show the χ² (or a similar metric) against ℓ_ee, to state what range is excluded;
   - add nSOT images at the same (n, D) as a function of temperature through the 15–30 K Gurzhi window. That would test the T-dependent ℓ_ee directly.
   Separately, at 1.6 K an ℓ_ee of 50 nm sits uneasily with TMF ℓ_mfp > 200 µm at 10^13 cm^-2, since TMF also suffers from e–e scattering. The authors should explain why ℓ_ee changes by more than three orders of magnitude between these two states.

7. The Gurzhi-effect evidence in the 0.5 µm channel lacks the standard discriminating tests and is taken at (n, D) values different from those in the images (Fig. 4g–i). A resistance minimum with dR/dT < 0 at 15–30 K in the single-surface regime could have other causes, and the regime contains symmetry-broken phases. The orange (2.5, 0.85) curve in Fig. 4g has a sharp kink near 7–10 K that looks more like a phase transition or an order-parameter feature than a smooth viscous crossover. The experimental decrease of R_s is only 10–25%, whereas the simulated decrease in Fig. 4i is close to an order of magnitude. Without a mapping from T to ℓ_ee, the comparison between experiment and Fig. 4i is qualitative.
   The authors should:
   - show the perpendicular-field dependence in the channel (negative magnetoresistance from suppressed viscosity, with a characteristic field set by r_c ~ W);
   - measure the channel at the same (n, D) as the nSOT hydrodynamic image (6.5 × 10^12 cm^-2, 0.41 V/nm), or image at the transport (n, D) points;
   - show that the minimum is absent where the theory predicts it should be, for example in the high-density D = 0 regime;
   - map the (n, D, T) region where dR/dT < 0, to rule out a phase boundary.

8. The "size-driven crossover" rests on two geometries that also differ in measurement configuration. The narrow channel uses a four-terminal channel geometry. The square uses injection at the top corners and a voltage across the bottom (Fig. 4k; Fig. S6 insets), a configuration that the paper itself shows is dominated by negative bend resistance at low T. The difference between Fig. 4g and Fig. 4j could therefore reflect contact geometry as much as width. The simulations include the contact configuration (Fig. 4k,l; Fig. S9), which helps. But the claim that the crossover is "achieved here simply by increasing the device width" would be stronger with:
   - intermediate channel widths (for example 1, 2 and 5 µm) in the same configuration;
   - or a local four-terminal measurement across the square.
   Also, in the square a monotonic R(T) is expected in ordinary diffusive flow as well. The authors state that R in the square is "insensitive to ℓ_ee" (Fig. 4l), so the transport data in the square cannot by itself separate porous hydrodynamic flow from ohmic flow. The text should say that this separation relies on the imaging.

9. The effective mass is quoted with an uncertainty too large to support "excellent agreement", and the fit quality is not shown quantitatively (Fig. 3i; Fig. S7b; Methods). The text gives m* = 0.075 ± 0.05 m_e, an uncertainty of about 67%, and calls the agreement with 0.078 m_e "excellent". If the uncertainty is a typo (0.005?), please correct it. If it is not, the agreement carries no weight. In Fig. S7b the data lie systematically above the fit at intermediate T. The model assumes parabolic bands, B_f − B_f(E) = m*B_n/ħ²k_F², although the inset of Fig. 3a shows a trigonally warped Fermi surface. The authors should:
   - report how the uncertainty was obtained;
   - show residuals;
   - test whether the result depends on the parabolic assumption, for example by using dB_f/dE computed from the tight-binding band directly.
   Because an independently determined m* matching 13-layer ABC is one of the few quantitative checks of stacking integrity, getting this right matters.

10. Boundary specularity is described inconsistently. The TMF section says the peaks imply a "high degree of specularity". The simulations, however, use diffuse probability p = 0.5, "chosen in agreement with the measured decay" of the focusing peaks. Half of all reflections being diffuse is not "high specularity". The authors should:
    - reconcile the two statements;
    - show the comparison between measured and simulated peak decay (1R…6R) that fixes p;
    - test whether the hydrodynamic and Gurzhi conclusions (Fig. 4h–l; Fig. S9) change with p, since Poiseuille flow depends on boundary conditions.

11. Heating and electron temperature during current imaging and transport are not discussed. The nSOT images use 10 µA (Fig. 4a,c,e). With sheet resistances of hundreds of Ω/sq in the narrow regions, and with contact resistance, the dissipated power and the resulting electron temperature at a lattice temperature of 1.6 K may be substantial. ℓ_ee depends strongly on T_e, so this bears directly on the hydrodynamic interpretation. The authors should:
    - estimate T_e for the imaging current, or show that the flow profiles do not change with current (for example at 2, 5 and 10 µA);
    - state the excitation current used in the Fig. 4g,j transport.

12. The yield statistics are confounded and partly mislabeled (Fig. S4b; abstract). The abstract speaks of "fabrication yields of ~90%". Fig. S4b defines yield as successful incorporation without relaxation during transfer, and the cumulative 24/26 counts only transfers that used all starred techniques. Relaxation during later processing (etching, contact deposition, PC melting at 200 C, top-gate drop) does not seem to be counted, and success is not defined by measurement. The data were collected by seven people of different experience, and the techniques were not assigned at random. Several individual comparisons are not statistically distinguishable at these sample sizes: LN2 18/24 vs 32/36; single sequence 13/17 vs 38/48; armchair/zigzag/arbitrary.
    The authors should:
    - define "yield" consistently;
    - report the end-to-end yield of measured devices with rhombohedral signatures;
    - give confidence intervals;
    - state which comparisons are significant. The claim that "cryo shock does not degrade transfer reliability" is reasonable; claims of improvement for the individual steps are not established.

13. The stability of the cryo-produced domains is not documented. If the cryogenic shock creates rhombohedral regions through stress, they may carry residual strain or be more prone to relaxing during heating (110–200 C in the transfer), thermal cycling or storage. The authors should report:
    - whether the rhombohedral areas of cryo flakes change over days to weeks of storage, or after the 200 C PC melt (IR imaging before and after);
    - whether the measured device's capacitance or TMF signatures are unchanged after a thermal cycle to room temperature.

C. Minor

14. In Fig. 3, the panel labels for i and j are swapped between the figure, the caption and the text. In the figure, j shows ℓ_mfp(T) and i shows V_peak/A_peak. The caption describes (i) as ℓ_mfp and (j) as V_peak/A_peak, and the main text cites "Fig. 3i" for ℓ_mfp and "Fig. 3j" for the mass fit. Also, the caption of Fig. 3e reads "First TMF peak for at T=2.0K".

15. Fig. 4 caption: (j) says "under the same conditions as panel h", but should presumably say panel g. (k) says "identical simulation as panel f", which is unclear, since f is the hydrodynamic image simulation; please check. The color bar in Fig. 4a gives |J| in µA, where µA/µm is presumably meant.

16. The value of ℓ_mr for the near-neutrality image is inconsistent. The main text and the Fig. S8f caption use ℓ_mr = 10 µm, while the Fig. S8k caption gives the best match as ℓ_mr = 20 µm (orange).

17. In the Methods, W(E,T) ~ exp(πd/(2ℓ_mfp)) should carry a minus sign. Eq. 5 switches notation from A(T)/A0 to V_area(T)/V_area,0.

18. "Extrapolating to ℓ_mfp(300 K) ≈ 2 µm": the data in Fig. 3j already extend to about 280 K, so this is close to an interpolation. Please give the measured high-T value directly.

19. p. 3: "averages rising from 1200 µm² and 12% to 3100 µm² and 32%". The Fig. 1g caption says the x-values are geometric means. Please use consistent wording and add the spreads.

20. Tight-binding parameters (Methods): please cite the source of γ0–γ4. In particular, the sign and magnitude of γ4 = −210 meV and γ3 = −290 meV differ from commonly used values, so the convention should be stated. Please also say whether m*_cal depends on these choices.

21. The statement that 740 µm² exceeds "maximum sizes of rhombohedral devices in the literature by at least a factor of two" should cite the device it compares against.

22. Fig. 2c: please give the measured magnetization (or its modulation amplitude) quantitatively and compare it with the value expected for the half-metal at the marked (n, D). Please give a histogram of M over the active area, so that "uniform" has a number attached, and state the spatial resolution set by the 100 nm tip height.

23. Reproducibility details for the exfoliation are missing: the tape type, how long the sample stays in LN2, the time and temperature of the 110 C step, and the cooling rate. Please state whether reheating versus slow warming affects the outcome.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a second fully measured cryo-shock device, preferably the >1300 µm² one, together with a standard-exfoliation control device made by the same transfer process. Compare capacitance-map sharpness, TMF or bend resistance, and nSOT uniformity directly. This turns a single demonstration into a claim about the process.
2. Make the ℓ_mfp extraction quantitative: error bars, robustness to window and baseline, a fixed or free ℓ0. Give the low-T result as a bound with stated confidence rather than ">200 µm".
3. Strengthen the hydrodynamic section with temperature-dependent current imaging and field-dependent channel transport at a common (n, D), and with a joint fit of ℓ_ee and ℓ_mr. Alternatively, present the Poiseuille-to-porous crossover as consistent with, rather than established by, the data.
4. Run a minimal control of the exfoliation mechanism: the same protocol without the LN2 shock, or with a reduced ΔT, using the same crystal. Report results resolved by thickness and size, with run-to-run spread, so that readers can judge which parameters matter when they adopt the method.
