A. Must address

1. No control shows that the Raman memory is thermal rather than an effect of laser exposure. All Raman cycling (Figs. 1, 3) is driven by laser power of up to 150 mW at 532 nm on a ~50 µm spot, with "almost instantaneous" heating and cooling and T_B ≈ 38 K. The "standard" heater-driven data (Fig. 2) were never run as a cycling protocol. Visible light is known to cause photo-induced melting of charge-orbital order, insulator-metal switching, photo-doping, light-induced metastability and local oxygen loss in (Pr,La)CaMnO3-based manganites, and the spurious ~660 cm⁻¹ feature that the authors attribute to oxygen vacancies is already present. Strong thermal gradients and strain from local heating could also contribute. None of these is present in the bulk cryostat cycles to which the Raman results are compared. The central claim, that the spectra at T_B encode the maximum temperature reached and that this is thermally driven memory, therefore does not follow. The authors should repeat the T_T sequence on a fresh spot using the cold-finger/cryostat heater for the excursion, with the Raman probe fixed at 10 mW (or lower), at the same T_B, ramp rate and dwell as the PPMS runs and otherwise identical acquisition, and compare directly with Fig. 3. Agreement would establish that the memory follows temperature and not illumination; a combined set of heater-driven and laser-driven cycles on the same spot would also show whether the laser adds anything.

2. The laser-heating temperature scale, the x-axis of the central result, is partly circular and its uncertainty is underestimated. T_T for every laser cycle comes from Table S1, built by "visual inspection and comparison" of power-dependent with temperature-dependent spectra on a ~25 K grid, using mainly the width and relative intensity of the 250, 480 and 614 cm⁻¹ structures (Fig. S1: inflections at 75, 100, 125, 150 and 175 K matched to 40, 50, 60, 70 and 80 mW). These are the same JT-mode intensities and widths whose history dependence is the claimed effect, so the coincidence of the Raman thresholds in Fig. 3 with T_C, T_MI and T_COO is partly built in. The matching was also done on a continuous power ramp, which is itself a cumulative thermal history. Further problems: (i) below about 75 K (40 mW) the spectra are admitted to be indistinguishable, so T_B = 38(4) K is pure extrapolation; (ii) a linear T(P) is imposed with the intercept fixed at zero (footnote, p. 12; a free fit gives −3(5) K, and it is not explained why the intercept was not fixed at the cold-finger temperature), and a temperature-independent 21.8 K offset at 10 mW is then added by assumption, although the thermal conductivity and optical absorption of LPCMO change strongly across T_C and T_COO (metallic versus insulating percolation), so the temperature reached at a given power should depend on the phase state of the spot, which by the paper's own claim changes from cycle to cycle; (iii) the Gaussian spot has strong lateral and depth gradients, so "T_T" is a distribution, smearing the axis exactly where the effect sits; (iv) Fig. S5d–f shows integrated areas under laser heating roughly 40% below those under cryostat heating near the maximum (~4.5 versus ~7.5 in S5d), contradicting the stated "great agreement" and suggesting an offset or gradient; (v) if high-power exposure changes absorption, surface roughness or thermal contact, the "T_B" read-out spectrum may be taken at a different temperature after each cycle, and since JT intensity is strongly non-monotonic in temperature (Fig. 2d), a modest offset could mimic the effect. The quoted ±4–8 K errors (Figs. 1, 3; Table S1) are fit-propagation errors only. Spectra were recorded down to about −250 cm⁻¹ (Fig. S2), so anti-Stokes signal is available but was absorbed into the background (Fig. S6). The authors should determine the local temperature independently from the Stokes/anti-Stokes ratio of the 250 cm⁻¹ (or 485 cm⁻¹) mode at each power and for every T_B spectrum in Fig. 3, before and after cycling; re-plot Fig. 3 on that scale; give realistic uncertainties including the matching-grid resolution; and estimate the radial and depth temperature profile (heat-diffusion model with known thermal conductivity and optical penetration depth, or a spot-size series).

3. The cumulative protocol confounds the T_T dependence with accumulated history, elapsed time, illumination dose and relaxation, and it cannot separate a new regime from ordinary minor-loop hysteresis and kinetic arrest. T_T increases monotonically and the same spot is probed throughout (Fig. 1b; Methods), so the state before cycle n is the state after cycle n−1 (Fig. 4c makes this explicit: M_i(n) ≈ M_f(n−1), and the M_i values change from cycle to cycle). LPCMO in the dynamic phase-separation regime at 30–40 K relaxes slowly (Ghivelder2005, Sacanell2004, cited), and the 5-minute wait before a 5-minute acquisition is comparable to or shorter than the known relaxation times, so the rise and fall in Fig. 3c–e could partly reflect relaxation or drift. The observation is also what one expects from a first-order transition: on warming above T_C^W ≈ 91 K, FMM converts to COO; on recooling from inside the hysteresis window (T_T < T_COO), part of that COO is kinetically arrested or supercooled at T_B; warming above T_COO erases it. The authors should add: a null measurement with repeated spectra at T_B (10 mW) for the full elapsed time of a cycle sequence with no excursion; non-cumulative cycles, recooling from above T_COO or from 300 K before each single T_T, for several T_T; repeated cycles to the same T_T, to see whether the effect saturates or accumulates; a decreasing or otherwise non-monotonic T_T sequence (for example T_C < T_T < T_MI after a reset above T_COO); a reference spot that went through the same cryostat history but was never illuminated at high power; and time dependence at T_B (Raman intensity and ρ for at least tens of minutes after each cycle). Without these, "previously unidentified regime" and "new form of nonequilibrium phase dynamics" are not supported.

4. The headline conclusions of laser-induced non-volatile resistive switching, laser-assisted phase engineering, and switching "associated with the local structure for the first time" (Abstract, Discussion, Conclusions) are not demonstrated. Magnetization and resistance (Fig. 4) were measured on the whole pellet in a PPMS with global cycling at 5 K/min, T_B = 30 K and a different T_T grid (50, 60, … K versus 59, 81, 102, … K for Raman). The Raman cycles use local laser heating with "almost instantaneous" cooling, T_B ≈ 38 K, and probe a surface layer tens of nm thick in a 50 µm spot. Kinetic arrest and the FMM fraction in phase-separated manganites depend strongly on cooling rate, so rates differing by orders of magnitude are not equivalent histories. No resistance was measured on a laser-cycled region and no Raman spectrum was taken after bulk cycling, so the structure–transport "correlation" rests on qualitative similarity between two different experiments. Non-volatility is also untested: there is no retention measurement of the high-resistance state at T_B, no repeated set/reset (T_C < T_T < T_MI followed by T_T > T_COO, several times), and no energy or time cost per cycle; erasing requires heating above about 205 K and reading requires staying near 30–40 K, which should be stated plainly if neuromorphic relevance is claimed. The authors should either measure transport or magnetization on laser-cycled material (a narrow bar or bridge, a laser-addressed constriction or patterned two-terminal gap, or a contacted pellet in an optical cryostat), or take Raman spectra after bulk cycling at PPMS rates, and add retention, repeated write/erase and cooling-rate dependence measurements. Otherwise the claims should be reduced to thermally driven, history-dependent resistivity qualitatively correlated with a separate Raman experiment, and the laser-related claims removed from the Abstract and Conclusions.

6. Laser-induced modification of the sample surface is not ruled out. The same spot is irradiated repeatedly up to 150 mW on a 50 µm spot (several kW/cm², roughly 7–8 kW/cm²) over more than 15 cycles. The spurious peak near 660–670 cm⁻¹ (red dashed line, Fig. 2a) is attributed to "local oxygen vacancies" without evidence; a band near this frequency is also the fingerprint of laser-induced Mn3O4-like surface decomposition in manganites, and its intensity in Fig. 2b changes sharply near 180–210 K. The high-shift background is visibly higher after some cycles (Fig. 1e,f) and much higher at 150 mW (Fig. S6f). The authors should track the ~660–670 cm⁻¹ band and the background through all cycles; show quantitatively, beyond the visual comparison in Fig. 1h, that after a full sequence and reset (including a final warm-up to room temperature and re-cooling) the as-cooled spectrum is recovered; and inspect the irradiated spot afterwards by optical microscopy or a micro-Raman map.

7. Intensity normalization and background subtraction may produce or distort the reported trends. JT intensities are normalized to the ~250 cm⁻¹ rotational mode, but Fig. S5d shows that this mode's area itself changes about fourfold with temperature and with phase fraction, peaking near 120 K where the JT modes peak; the ratio therefore mixes changes in both, and the reference at T_B could also depend on history. The normalization appears to shift the apparent maximum: Fig. S1 and Fig. S5e,f place the JT maximum near 100–125 K, whereas Fig. 2d places it near T_MI ≈ 150 K. Backgrounds were fitted with a fixed 7th-degree polynomial plus several broad Lorentzians (FWHM 50–120 cm⁻¹) over the whole range, with fit quality "judged based on visual inspection", while the background shape changes markedly between conditions (Fig. 1e,f; Fig. S6a,b,e). The authors should show the absolute (unnormalized) JT and rotational-mode areas at T_B versus T_T, with I_rot reported on its own; show that the trends in Fig. 3c–e survive background-independent metrics (peak heights above a local linear baseline, integrated intensity in fixed windows), a lower-order or physically motivated electronic-continuum background, the background left in, and a different number of Lorentzians; and report how laser focus and collection efficiency were kept constant between cycles.

8. JT-mode intensity is used as a measure of the AFMI-COO phase fraction without justification. The statement that "the strength of the COO phase achieved at T_T is retained", and the claimed "quantitative correspondence for T_T < T_C", rest on comparing intensities measured at temperature T (Fig. 2d) with intensities measured at T_B after visiting T_T (Fig. 3c). Phonon intensities and widths depend intrinsically on temperature through Bose factors, polaron dynamics and electronic screening. Raman intensity per phase also depends on the optical penetration depth (much shorter in metallic FMM regions), on resonance conditions at 532 nm close to JT-polaron and charge-transfer excitations, and on screening by metallic regions. Structural characterization is limited to synchrotron PXRD at 278 K (Fig. S7), so the assignment of JT changes to the P2₁/m COO phase, and the claim that the irreversibility is "structural" rather than purely electronic, are not directly supported. The authors should discuss these factors and calibrate JT intensity against an independent phase-fraction estimate: low-temperature synchrotron diffraction after selected cycles, even at one temperature in the DPS regime (P2₁/m versus Pnma fraction or superlattice intensity; their PAINEIRA access would allow this), saturated DC magnetization at T_B, or field-induced COO-to-FMM conversion at T_B.

9. The "magnetization" is low-field AC susceptibility and does not measure the FMM fraction. M–T was measured in ACMS mode with a 10 Oe, 11 Hz AC field and no DC field, and reported as µB/Mn at the ~10⁻² level, two orders of magnitude below saturation. In a polycrystalline ferromagnet this response is dominated by domain-wall dynamics and demagnetization and is not proportional to FM volume fraction. The authors should label and report it as χ′ (and χ″), and add DC magnetization at a fixed field (of order 1 T, or a small fixed field) or M(H) loops to saturation at T_B after each cycle, so that Raman and ρ changes can be related to a real FMM fraction.

10. Resistance was measured with two probes, silver-paint contacts about 0.7 mm apart and 0.1 µA on a polycrystalline pellet. Contact and grain-boundary resistance contribute and can themselves be history dependent, so they cannot be separated from bulk percolation; the conversion to resistivity (Ω mm) also needs a stated geometry. The ~10³ change across T_IM makes a reversal of the trends unlikely, but the quantitative claims (the "two-fold" and "about three times" gains) are affected. The authors should repeat the key ρ data with four-probe contacts or at least check contact resistance before and after cycling.

11. The novelty relative to established thermal-history, memory and kinetic-arrest phenomena in phase-separated manganites is overstated. History-dependent FM fraction, glassy relaxation, cooling-rate dependence, minor-loop memory and erasure by heating above the charge-ordering temperature are well documented in LPCMO and related compounds (Ghivelder2005, Sacanell, Ward2011, Kundhikanjana2015, all cited, and the kinetic-arrest/CHUF literature), and the M and ρ behaviour in Fig. 4 resembles what those works predict. The authors should compare directly with these results and state what is new beyond them, for example a lattice signature, a spatially local read-out, or a quantitative difference in the T_T dependence absent from transport, and frame the M/ρ results accordingly. "Irreversible" is a misnomer for a state that resets on heating above T_COO; "history-dependent" or "metastable" would be more accurate.

15. The differences between local laser heating with surface Raman and bulk cryostat cycling with M/ρ are not discussed. A laser-heated spot is mechanically constrained by a cold surrounding matrix, and phase separation in LPCMO is strongly strain-sensitive, as the introduction notes, so thermal stress absent from uniform PPMS heating may drive part of the effect. Raman at 532 nm probes only the top tens of nm of a freshly broken polycrystalline surface, where surface strain and grain-boundary effects may shift the phase balance relative to the bulk-averaged M and ρ. The authors should discuss these differences and estimate them where possible.
