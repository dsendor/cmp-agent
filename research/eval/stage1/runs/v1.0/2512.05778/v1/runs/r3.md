Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors use a focused 532 nm laser as a local heater on polycrystalline La0.275Pr0.35Ca0.375MnO3. They cycle the probed spot between a base temperature T_B ≈ 38 K and a series of rising target temperatures T_T, and use Raman spectroscopy to show that the intensities and widths of the Jahn-Teller (JT) stretching modes measured back at T_B depend on how far the spot was taken. The changes grow when T_T lies between T_C and T_MI, and they reset once T_T exceeds T_COO. Bulk magnetization and two-probe resistivity, measured on cryostat-driven cycles in a PPMS, follow similar trends. A Raman fingerprint of thermal memory in the dynamic phase-separated (DPS) regime, read out locally on the lattice, would be a useful complement to the well-established magnetic and transport history effects in LPCMO. As presented, however, the evidence does not yet separate a genuine memory of the phase-separated structure from calibration, aging, laser-exposure and protocol effects. The claims of "laser-induced resistive switching" and a "previously unidentified regime" go beyond what is shown.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The Raman cycling protocol (Fig. 1b) is simple and well suited to local, contactless probing of history dependence in a phase-separated oxide.
- The T_T dependence at T_B (Fig. 3a, 3c, 3e) tracks the transition temperatures and resembles the standard heating evolution (Fig. 2b, 2d). Both JT modes behave in the same way, and the reset above T_COO argues against simple cumulative laser damage.
- Three probes (Raman, M, ρ) respond with a similar T_T dependence (Figs. 3d-e and 4d), which supports a common origin in reconfiguration of the FMM/AFMI-COO phase fractions.
- The supplementary material documents the power-temperature calibration (Table S1, Figs. S1-S5), the fitting model (Fig. S6) and the structural characterization (Fig. S7, Table S2) openly.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. No Raman control separates thermal memory from laser exposure, and the effective temperature at T_B is not verified after each cycle. All Raman cycling (Figs. 1, 3) is driven by laser power of up to 150 mW on a ~50 µm spot. The "standard" heating data (Fig. 2) were never run as a cycling protocol. The central claim is that the spectra at T_B encode the maximum temperature reached. At least two other explanations fit the same data.
   - Photo-induced effects: light-induced metastability, photo-doping and local oxygen loss are all known in (Pr,La)CaMnO3. The spurious ~660 cm⁻¹ feature that the authors attribute to oxygen vacancies is already present.
   - A shift in the local temperature reached at the 10 mW read-out power: if high-power exposure changes absorption, surface roughness or thermal contact, the "T_B" spectrum is taken at a different temperature. The JT intensity is a strongly non-monotonic function of temperature (Fig. 2d), so a modest offset could mimic the effect.
   The authors should:
   - repeat the T_T sequence on a fresh spot using the cryostat heater for the excursion, with Raman read out at T_B at constant low power. Matching Fig. 3 would establish that the effect is thermal.
   - for every T_B spectrum in Fig. 3, report the local temperature from the Stokes/anti-Stokes intensity ratio of the ~250 cm⁻¹ mode, the low-frequency region already used in Fig. S2. This shows that T_B is the same after each cycle.

2. The cumulative protocol confounds the T_T dependence with time, aging and accumulated exposure, and there is no null measurement. Each cycle starts from the state left by the previous one (Fig. 1b; Methods), so T_T increases monotonically with elapsed time and total dose. LPCMO in the DPS regime is known to relax slowly at fixed temperature (refs. Ghivelder2005, Sacanell2004, which the authors cite). A 5 min wait and acquisition times of several minutes per spectrum are comparable to those relaxation times. The rise and fall in Fig. 3c-e could therefore partly reflect relaxation at T_B and drift, rather than the value of T_T. The authors should:
   - record repeated spectra at T_B for the same total elapsed time without any excursion (the null);
   - run at least one non-monotonic sequence, for example a T_T between T_C and T_MI after a reset above T_COO, or a decreasing sequence;
   - repeat a given T_T twice in succession, to test whether the state depends on T_T alone or on the full history.
   The magnetization data have the same issue: the Fig. 4c M_i values change from cycle to cycle.

3. The key Raman result is shown for one sequence on one spot, with no measure of spread. Methods states that "different spots and pieces of the sample were measured with the same protocol", but only one data set appears in Figs. 1 and 3. The error bars in Figs. 2-3 appear to be single-fit uncertainties. Several features rest on one or two points: the peak of ΔI/I_i at T_T ≈ 100 K in Fig. 3d, and the near two-fold gain in Fig. 3e. The authors should:
   - show the T_T dependence from at least two further spots and one further piece, overlaid or as mean ± spread;
   - state how the error bars were obtained.
   The same applies to the M and ρ cycles in Fig. 4, which appear to come from one pellet and one run.

4. The conclusion that laser heating induces non-volatile resistive switching is not demonstrated. The Abstract, Discussion and Conclusions ("laser-induced thermal cycling to induce non-volatile structure/resistive-switching", "a novel method for ... inducing resistive switching") rest on two separate experiments.
   - The resistivity changes (Fig. 4b-d) were produced by cryostat cycling of the whole pellet in a PPMS, at 5 K/min, with T_B = 30 K and a different T_T grid.
   - The laser cycling probes a surface layer of a few tens of nm within a 50 µm spot, and no transport was measured on laser-cycled material.
   The cooling after the laser is switched off is described as "almost instantaneous", which differs from 5 K/min by orders of magnitude. Kinetic arrest in phase-separated manganites is known to depend strongly on cooling rate, so the Raman-transport correspondence in the Discussion is only qualitative. Retention, the defining property of non-volatility, is not measured either. The authors should do one of the following:
   - measure resistance across a region laser-cycled in situ, for example a narrow bridge or a pellet in an optical cryostat with contacts;
   - restrict the claims to thermally driven, history-dependent resistivity, and add measurements of retention time at T_B and of cooling-rate dependence.

B. Should address

5. The local-temperature calibration is coarse, partly circular and probably underestimates the uncertainties. T(P) in Table S1 was obtained by visually matching laser-heated and cryostat-heated spectra in ~25 K steps. The matching uses the same JT-mode intensities and widths that serve as the observable in the cycling experiment. It was done on a continuous power ramp, which is itself a cumulative thermal history. Several further points weaken it:
   - The linear form and the zero intercept are imposed (footnote, p. 12). A free fit gives -3(5) K, and the thermal conductivity and absorption of LPCMO change across T_C and T_COO.
   - The offset correction of 21.8 K at 10 mW is applied by assumption.
   - A Gaussian spot at these powers has strong radial and depth gradients, so the probed volume contains material that reached different T_T. This smears the T_T axis precisely where the claimed effect sits.
   - Fig. S5d-f shows the integrated areas under laser heating roughly 40% below those under cryostat heating near the maximum (for example ~4.5 vs ~7.5 in S5d). This conflicts with the stated "great agreement" and points to an offset or a temperature gradient.
   The quoted uncertainties of ±4-8 K on T_T (Figs. 1, 3; Table S1) do not reflect any of this. The authors should calibrate with the Stokes/anti-Stokes ratio, give realistic uncertainties on T_T, and estimate the radial temperature profile, for example by heat-diffusion modelling or a spot-size series.

6. Possible laser-induced sample modification is not ruled out. The highest powers correspond to several kW/cm². The spurious ~660 cm⁻¹ peak (red line, Fig. 2a) is assigned to oxygen vacancies, and its intensity in Fig. 2b changes sharply near 200 K. The background at high Raman shift is visibly higher after some cycles (Fig. 1e, f) and much higher at 150 mW (Fig. S6f). The authors should:
   - track the ~660 cm⁻¹ feature and the background versus cycle number;
   - show that a full sequence on one spot, followed by a reset, reproduces the as-cooled spectrum quantitatively, beyond the visual comparison in Fig. 1h;
   - inspect the spot afterwards by optical microscopy.

7. Intensity extraction depends on a heavily parametrized background and on normalization. A fixed 7th-order polynomial plus Lorentzians is fitted over the whole range, and fit quality was judged visually (Fig. S6). The JT modes sit on a broad, history-dependent electronic background, and in Fig. 1e-f the background changes after cycling. The headline quantity is I_JT/I_rot. The rotational mode itself changes strongly with phase fraction (Fig. S5a, d), so a change in the reference cannot be separated from a change in the JT modes. The authors should:
   - show that the trends in Fig. 3c-e hold with unnormalized, background-independent metrics, for example peak heights above a local linear baseline or integrated intensity in fixed windows, and with a lower-order background;
   - report I_rot at T_B versus T_T on its own.

8. Mapping JT-mode intensity onto the AFMI-COO phase fraction is assumed, not justified. The Discussion reads intensity gains as "strength of the COO phase" and FMM-fraction changes, but Raman intensity per phase depends on several other factors:
   - optical penetration depth, which is much shorter in the metallic FMM regions;
   - resonance conditions at 532 nm, which lie close to the JT-polaron and charge-transfer excitations;
   - the electronic screening by the metallic regions.
   The two-fold Raman gain versus the halving of M (Fig. 4d) is attributed loosely to "the AFM character of the insulating phase". The authors should discuss these factors and, ideally, calibrate the intensity ratio against an independent phase-fraction estimate, such as saturated DC magnetization at T_B.

9. The magnetization measurement does not give FM fraction directly. M was measured in ACMS mode with a 10 Oe AC field and no DC field, then reported as μB/Mn at the ~10⁻² level. This is a low-field susceptibility, dominated by domain-wall motion and demagnetization in a polycrystalline pellet. It is not the ferromagnetic volume fraction. The authors should measure M(H) up to saturation, or DC M at a field of order 1 T, at T_B after each cycle, so that the Raman and ρ changes can be related to a real FMM fraction.

10. The resistivity is two-probe on a polycrystalline pellet, at a contact spacing of ~0.7 mm and 0.1 µA. Contact and grain-boundary resistance contribute, and both can be history dependent. The ~10³ change across T_IM is large enough that this is unlikely to reverse the trends, but the quantitative claims are affected: the "two-fold" gain (Results) and the "about three times" increase (Discussion). The authors should use four-probe geometry or show that the contact contribution is negligible.

11. The novelty relative to known thermal-history and kinetic-arrest phenomena is overstated. History-dependent FM fraction, glassy relaxation, cooling-rate dependence and memory effects in LPCMO and related compounds are well documented, by Ghivelder, Sacanell, Ward and Kundhikanjana (all cited) and in the kinetic-arrest and CHUF literature. The M and ρ behavior in Fig. 4 resembles what those works would predict. Calling this a "previously unidentified regime" therefore needs a direct comparison. The authors should state what the Raman data show that is new, for example a lattice signature or a spatially local readout, and frame the M/ρ results accordingly. "Irreversible" is also a misnomer for a state that resets on heating above T_COO; "history-dependent" or "metastable" would be more accurate.

12. The transition temperatures used to interpret the Raman cycles come from mismatched protocols. T_C ≈ 91-95 K and T_MI ≈ 153 K in Figs. 2-3 are warming values. On cooling, the corresponding values are 63 K and 120 K (Fig. S8). A cycle crosses both branches, and the hysteresis is ~30 K, comparable to the T_T spacing. The Raman T_B is 38 K, while the PPMS T_B is 30 K. The authors should state which branch governs each regime boundary and justify comparing the T_B values. They should also comment on the T_N ≈ 188 K feature in Fig. S8a, which is not mentioned in the main text but falls inside the 167-205 K window where the reset occurs.

13. Structural characterization is limited to room temperature. The interpretation rests on Pnma (FMM) versus P2₁/m (AFMI-COO) structures, but the synchrotron XRD (Fig. S7) was taken only at 278 K. Low-temperature diffraction, even at one temperature in the DPS regime, would support the assignment of the JT-mode changes to the monoclinic COO phase. It would also give a bulk phase fraction to compare with the Raman results.

C. Minor

14. The magnetization values are inconsistent between panels. In Fig. 4a, M at ~30 K on the warming branch is ~1.9×10⁻² μB/Mn, while in Fig. 4c M_i at T_T = 50-70 K is ~3.4×10⁻² μB/Mn. Please explain the difference, for example different cooling histories, or correct it.

15. Numbers and references are inconsistent across the text, captions and Methods:
   - The text refers to "figure 2 e", which does not exist; Fig. 3e appears to be meant.
   - The Fig. 1 caption says "d - f" for six T_T values shown in panels d-h.
   - Methods lists Raman T_T = 59(4), 81(4), ..., 317(7) K and PPMS T_T = 50, 60, ..., 250 K, but Fig. 1 starts at 102 K and the Fig. 4 caption lists 70, 90, 100, 150 and 210 K.
   - The resistive gain is given as "two-fold" in Results and "about three times" in the Discussion.
   Please reconcile these.

16. Notation is inconsistent: T_FPS/T_OCO in Figs. S4-S5 versus T_C/T_COO in the main text, and T_IM versus T_MI. Several sentences have typos ("It was observed for the that", "show an clear agreement").

17. Citations appear as unresolved BibTeX keys (for example [Sacanell2004], [Kim], [Merten]), and the supplementary sections are labelled only "S". Please compile the references properly.

18. Fig. 2 and Fig. S1 intensities are not corrected for the Bose-Einstein factor, yet intensities at different temperatures are compared quantitatively with the T_B data (p. 5, "quantitative correspondence ... for T_T and T < T_C"). Please apply the correction or limit the comparison.

19. Fig. 5 is a schematic with no quantitative link to the data. Please label it as illustrative in the main text as well as in the caption, or tie the domain fractions to a measured quantity.

20. The Fig. 2c-d error bars at high temperature are large. The FWHM of AS-JT above T_COO falls between 250 and 321 K, which the text does not discuss. Please comment on this or state the fit constraints used.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add the decisive control: the same T_B/T_T sequence driven by the cryostat heater, with low-power Raman read-out. Report Stokes/anti-Stokes-derived local temperatures for every spectrum, and a no-excursion aging null at T_B.
2. Show reproducibility across spots and pieces, and include at least one non-monotonic or repeated-T_T sequence, to establish that the state at T_B is a function of the maximum temperature reached and not of elapsed history.
3. Strengthen the bulk comparison: DC M(H) at T_B after each cycle to obtain a real FM fraction, four-probe resistivity, cooling-rate dependence and retention-time measurements. If a laser-induced resistive change is to be claimed, measure transport across a laser-cycled region directly.
4. Reframe the claims against the existing thermal-history and kinetic-arrest literature on LPCMO. Make the local lattice readout the main new result, and replace "irreversible" and "non-volatile" with terms the data support.
