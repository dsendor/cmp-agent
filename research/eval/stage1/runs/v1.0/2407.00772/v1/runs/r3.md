Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors use a cryogenic attosecond broadband XUV transient absorption setup (ABXAS) on exfoliated 1T-TiSe2. They report three findings. First, static core-level photoemission and absorption show no anomaly at Tc. Second, the pump-induced transmission change at 200 fs in a Ti M2,3 "many-body continuum" window and in Se M5 peak 2 turns on like an order parameter below Tc. Third, the rise time of the Se peak-2 signal scales as 1/sqrt(F) both above and below Tc, which the authors take as time-domain evidence for short-range excitonic correlations in the normal state. Showing that broadband core-level transient absorption can report a low-energy ordering transition would interest both the attosecond and the correlated-materials communities. The excitonic-fluctuation claim bears on a long-running debate. As presented, however, that claim rests on a single fluence-scaling argument without controls, and the long-range-order signature has not been separated from simpler spectroscopic effects.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The instrument is technically impressive. It combines sub-4-fs pump pulses, a 5.4 fs instrument response, broadband coverage of both the Ti M2,3 and Se M4,5 edges in one shot, and a cryostat reaching about 20 K with drift correction.
- The paper benchmarks against ARPES on the same material: replica-band spectral weight and gap (Fig. 1e,f) and simultaneous Ti 3p / Se 3d core-level positions (Fig. 1j–l). This gives a clean static baseline.
- The temperature scans in Fig. 3d,e are dense (about 5 K steps) and show a clear change at about 200 K, matching the Tc seen by ARPES.
- Using the absence of the 6 THz A1g oscillation in Se peaks 2 and 3 (Fig. 2e) to choose a phonon-free spectral window is a sensible, data-driven choice.
- Comparing fluence-dependent rise times in phonon-dominated windows with those in a phonon-free window (Fig. 4e) is a useful internal comparison.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The 1/sqrt(F) rise-time scaling does not, on its own, show excitonic correlations above Tc, and there is no null or reference measurement (Fig. 4b,e; pp. 9–10).
   - **Alternative explanation.** The argument is that a faster initial response at higher fluence reflects exciton breaking by plasmon screening, whose frequency scales as sqrt(n). But the build-up of a Se 4p hole population near the valence-band top after 1.65 eV excitation also speeds up with excitation density in any semimetal or narrow-gap semiconductor. Carrier–carrier scattering, the cascade of hot holes to the band edge, and band-gap renormalisation all do this, with no excitonic correlation involved. Above Tc, 1T-TiSe2 is exactly such a semimetal.
   - **Fit is not discriminating.** With four fluences per temperature, a free prefactor and a free offset (tau0 = 26 fs), a 1/sqrt(F) law is not separated from 1/F, a logarithm or other monotonic forms. The trend also leans heavily on the single 0.2 mJ/cm² point at each temperature, which has the largest error bar (Fig. 4e).
   - **The plasmon picture predicts a temperature difference.** In that picture the screening density is the total density, n0(T) + alpha·F, not the photoexcited density alone. The equilibrium free-carrier density changes substantially across the CDW transition and with temperature. So the 23 K and 300 K curves would be expected to differ. That they collapse onto one curve "within experimental uncertainties" argues against, not for, the specific mechanism invoked.
   - **What the authors should do:**
     (i) Measure the same window and fluence series on a reference where excitonic correlations are absent or much weaker. Options include TiSe2 well above the fluctuation regime (e.g. ≥400 K, if reachable), an isostructural non-CDW compound such as TiS2 or ZrSe2, or electron-doped (e.g. Cu-intercalated) TiSe2.
     (ii) Give a quantitative estimate of the photoexcited carrier density and the resulting plasmon period at each fluence, and compare it with the measured 50–190 fs times. Rough estimates suggest plasmon periods well below 50 fs at these densities.
     (iii) Fit the 23 K and 300 K data separately. Report the prefactors and offsets with their uncertainties, and compare competing functional forms using a goodness-of-fit criterion.
     (iv) Use the TDDFT capability already in the paper to simulate the rise time of this spectral feature at several fluences in a model without excitonic interactions.

2. Window iii is used both as a reporter of long-range order and as a reporter of short-range correlations, and the two uses conflict (Fig. 3e vs. Fig. 4).
   - **Fig. 3e.** The integrated window-iii signal is described as "quasi-constant above Tc" with an order-parameter-like onset below Tc. This is unlike the ARPES observables in Fig. 1e,f, which the authors highlight as having a fluctuation tail above Tc.
   - **Section 4.** The same peak-2 hole population is then said to be "agnostic about the existence of long-range phase coherence" and sensitive to local excitonic correlations that persist above Tc.
   - **The tension.** If window iii were sensitive to local excitonic pairing, the fluctuations the authors claim above Tc should produce a tail in Fig. 3e, as the replica weight does in Fig. 1e. If it is insensitive to them in amplitude but sensitive in rise time, the authors need to explain why.
   - **What the authors should do:** Reconcile these two readings explicitly. Plot Fig. 3e and Fig. 1e on a common normalised temperature axis. Ideally, report the window-iii rise time at fixed low fluence as a function of temperature, which is the more direct test of a precursor regime.

3. The temperature-dependent quantity in Fig. 3d,e may reflect spectroscopic artefacts rather than order (Fig. 3, Fig. 1p, Methods). At least three effects need to be excluded.
   - **(a) Edge shift through a fixed window.** The window-iii transient has a derivative-like lineshape: negative then positive within about 0.5 eV (Fig. 3c). It is integrated over a fixed 0.29 eV window (53.04–53.33 eV). Meanwhile the static peak-2 position shifts by about 40 meV between 25 and 310 K (Fig. 1p). The fixed-window integral of a derivative-shaped signal is very sensitive to such a shift. Integrate in a window that tracks the edge position, or decompose the transient into shift, broadening and amplitude components at each temperature.
   - **(b) Delay falls on the rising edge.** Fig. 3 is taken at a single delay of 200 fs at 0.2 mJ/cm². At this fluence the window-iii initial response time is about 150–190 fs (Fig. 4e), so the 200 fs point still lies on the rising edge. Any temperature dependence of the rise time, or of time zero during the temperature ramp, feeds directly into the amplitude plotted in Fig. 3e. Show full delay traces at several temperatures above and below Tc, at minimum at 25, 150, 200, 250 and 310 K, or plot the plateau amplitude.
   - **(c) Absorbed fluence changes with temperature.** The temperature scan is at fixed incident fluence, but the optical constants of TiSe2 at 500–1000 nm change across the CDW transition. The absorbed energy density therefore varies with temperature. Report the absorbed fluence versus temperature, or normalise to it.
   - **Same issues for the Ti window.** The Ti window-ii sign reversal is also shown only at 200 fs (Fig. 2b,d, Fig. 3). Show that it is robust at other delays.

B. Should address

4. The claim that equilibrium core-level absorption shows no transition signal (abstract; Fig. 1o–q) rests only on fitted peak positions. The transient data are differential, with sensitivity at the 10^-3 level. The static comparison is a peak-position fit with roughly 10 meV precision.
   - **Why it matters.** Changes in lineshape, width or spectral weight, especially in the broad Ti many-body continuum (38–53 eV) the authors later find most sensitive, could be present in equilibrium and missed by this analysis. The contrast "absent in static, present in transient" is central to the framing, so the static null needs a like-for-like test.
   - **What the authors should do:** Show static difference spectra A(E,T) − A(E,310 K), normalised in the same way as the transient data. Show the temperature-integrated signal in windows ii and iii in the same format as Fig. 3d,e, with the noise floor stated.

5. Sample heating and the effective excitation conditions are not quantified (Methods; Figs. 2–4).
   - **Average heating.** The samples are free-standing flakes under 100 nm thick on copper TEM grids, pumped at 1 kHz with up to 2.9 mJ/cm². The Methods state that pre-time-zero signals from "millisecond cumulative heating" were subtracted and "do not change the conclusions", but give no estimate of the steady-state temperature rise. If the average heating at the higher fluences is tens of kelvin, the "23 K" points in Fig. 4e may sit at a different effective temperature than labelled.
   - **Depth profile.** The pump penetration depth at 750 nm is comparable to or smaller than the flake thickness. Because the XUV probe is in transmission, it averages over a depth-graded excitation. This complicates any scaling with incident F.
   - **What the authors should do:**
     - Estimate the steady-state and per-pulse temperature rise. For example, use the static peak shifts in Fig. 1o–q as an internal thermometer for the pre-time-zero spectra.
     - Give the absorbed fluence and excitation depth profile for each flake.
     - Discuss how the depth averaging affects the fitted rise times.

6. The fitting procedure behind Fig. 4e is under-specified.
   - **Which parameter is plotted.** Eq. (1) has both an error-function width omega ("intrinsic system response time") and a decay time tau. It is not stated which one is the "initial response time" in Fig. 4e. The caption's tau ~ 1/sqrt(F) + tau0 uses tau, which in Eq. (1) is the relaxation time.
   - **Oscillatory windows.** For windows i and iv, explain how the rise time is separated from the A1g oscillation, whose quarter period (about 42 fs) is close to the reported constant of about 50 fs. The constancy may simply reflect the phonon period rather than a contrast in physics.
   - **What the authors should do:**
     - State which parameters are free and which are fixed, and whether t0 is free or fixed by the He calibration.
     - Give the parameter correlations, since t0 and omega are strongly correlated for the slow, noisy 0.2 mJ/cm² traces in Fig. 4a,b.
     - Show the residuals.

7. The number of samples and their characterisation are not reported.
   - **Reproducibility.** It is not stated how many flakes were measured, or whether Fig. 3 and Fig. 4 come from the same flake. It is also not stated whether the key results (the onset in Fig. 3e and the Fig. 4e scaling above Tc) were reproduced on a second flake or after thermal cycling.
   - **Flake quality.** Exfoliated TiSe2 processed in ethanol and transferred in air can oxidise. Ti oxides contribute absorption in the Ti M2,3 region, which overlaps the many-body continuum used in Fig. 3d. The flake Tc is inferred only from the XAS data themselves.
   - **What the authors should do:**
     - Report the number of flakes and their thickness, and show the key results on at least two flakes.
     - Give evidence of stoichiometry and surface oxidation (e.g. Raman, or the absence of oxide features in the static XAS).
     - Ideally, give an independent Tc for the flakes (e.g. Raman amplitude mode or resistance).

8. The CDW amplitude mode is not discussed (Fig. 2c–f). Below Tc, 1T-TiSe2 has a well-known A1g CDW amplitude mode near 3.4 THz in addition to the 6 THz mode. At 0.2 mJ/cm² and 23 K, the amplitude mode or its absence directly reports whether the pump has melted the order, and whether the probed volume is in the CDW state at all. The authors should search for it in the low-fluence data and comment.

9. The novelty relative to ref. 22 (Heinrich et al., PRX 2023) is not delineated. That work reported electronic and structural CDW fingerprints in XUV transient absorption of 1T-TiSe2. The abstract's claims that core-level spectroscopies are "not traditionally regarded as an appropriate probe for long-range order", and that this is the "first demonstration", need to be reconciled with that work. The authors should state clearly what the present data add, for example the temperature-resolved onset and access to both edges.

10. Several conclusions go beyond the data.
   - **(a) Excitons and 2D in-plane order.** The Discussion concludes that exciton correlations are "instrumental in forming the short-range 2D order in-plane". The data contain no momentum or dimensional information, and nothing shows that the correlations drive, rather than accompany, the lattice fluctuations.
   - **(b) "Resolves the debate".** The claim to "resolve the debate" with the EELS results (refs. 10, 16) is too strong, given concern 1.
   - **(c) Attosecond resolution.** The attosecond and few-femtosecond resolution is not actually used. All extracted times are 50–190 fs, which conventional femtosecond XUV sources can resolve. Calling ABXAS "indispensable" should be toned down, or supported by a result that needs sub-10-fs resolution.

11. The interpretation of the Ti many-body continuum rests on the TDDFT comparison in Supplementary Note 1 and Fig. S3.
   - **Missing details.** The Methods describe only ground-state DFT, with PBE, no stated spin-orbit treatment, and a 24-atom CDW cell with unstated atomic displacements. They do not say whether the CDW structure was relaxed or taken from experiment.
   - **Missing comparison.** It is also not shown whether TDDFT reproduces the sign reversal of the continuum signal between the CDW and normal states, which would directly support the claim in Fig. 3d.
   - **What the authors should do:** Give the TDDFT settings and, if feasible, the calculated transient change in the continuum for both phases.

   - The low-fluence window-iii trace appears comparable in magnitude to the high-fluence trace, and no scaling factor is indicated.
   - The text (p. 8) instead describes "near-proportional scaling-down", and Fig. 4a shows the 0.2 mJ/cm² amplitude well below the 1.8 mJ/cm² amplitude.
   - Clarify the scaling and labelling of Fig. 2e.
15. Wording on p. 8: "commensurate with a fluence regime where the CDW order and excitonic correlations are close to be fully suppressed" sits awkwardly with calling 0.2 mJ/cm² the low fluence that preserves sensitivity to order. State the 0.2 mJ/cm² fluence relative to the literature thresholds (for both the electronic and the lattice order) as a ratio.
16. Fig. 4e:
   - Some points lack the horizontal (fluence) error bars described in the caption.
   - Give the prefactor of the blue curve and say whether it is a joint fit.
   - The gray band should state its width (s.d. or s.e.m.).
17. Fig. 1n: the absorption, defined as 1 − I_sample/I_blank, reaches 0.6–0.9. Give the flake thickness for this spectrum and discuss whether the absorbance is in a regime where transmission changes map linearly onto absorption changes.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add one reference measurement where excitonic correlations should be absent or weaker (an isostructural non-CDW dichalcogenide, doped TiSe2, or TiSe2 at a much higher temperature), using the same window and fluence series as Fig. 4. This would turn the fluence-scaling argument into a controlled comparison.
2. Replace the single-delay, fixed-window analysis of Fig. 3 with a global lineshape decomposition (shift, width, amplitude) as a function of temperature and delay. Include static difference spectra processed in exactly the same way, so the static-versus-transient contrast is like-for-like.
3. Measure the window-iii rise time at fixed low fluence as a continuous function of temperature through Tc. A precursor excitonic regime should appear as a characteristic temperature dependence, which would test the short-range-correlation claim more directly than two temperatures.
4. Give quantitative estimates connecting the data to the proposed mechanism: photoexcited carrier density, plasmon period, absorbed fluence versus temperature, and steady-state heating. Discuss which observed time scales are and are not consistent with plasmon-mediated exciton breaking.
