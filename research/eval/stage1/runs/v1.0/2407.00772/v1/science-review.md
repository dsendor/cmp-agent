A. Must address

1. The 1/sqrt(F) rise-time scaling in Fig. 4e does not uniquely identify excitonic correlations above Tc, the fit is underconstrained, and there is no null measurement (Fig. 4b,e; pp. 9–11). The normal-state excitonic claim rests entirely on the fluence dependence of the window-iii (Se peak 2) "initial response time": four fluences at 23 K and four at 300 K, fitted with tau = a/sqrt(F) + tau0, with the exponent fixed and tau0 = 26 fs free. The argument is that tau follows the plasmon period, the plasmon frequency goes as sqrt(n), and n is proportional to F. Several mechanisms without excitons also shorten the rise at higher excitation density in any semimetal or narrow-gap semiconductor, which is what TiSe2 is above Tc: electron–electron scattering and thermalisation, and the cascade of hot carriers (created far from E_F by 500–1000 nm photons) into the Se 4p hole states near Gamma; density-dependent screening of the free-carrier plasma; band-gap renormalisation; state filling, Pauli blocking and saturation of the probed window; the phonon-free window simply following the build-up of the hot-carrier distribution; and a fluence-dependent shift of t0 absorbed into the rise parameter. The ad hoc offset tau0 ("time needed for creating mobile Se 4p holes"), together with a free prefactor and a fluence range of only about one decade, largely removes the distinction between 1/sqrt(F), 1/F, logarithmic, linear-decreasing or saturating forms. The trend leans heavily on the single 0.2 mJ/cm² point at each temperature (about 190 fs, error bars of about ±20–30 fs), and the data scatter: the 300 K points are non-monotonic (1.5 mJ/cm² at about 65 fs, 1.9 mJ/cm² at about 87 fs), and the 23 K point at 2.9 mJ/cm² (about 81 fs) lies above the curve. The plasmon picture is also internally inconsistent with the result: in a semimetal the screening density is the total density n0(T) + alpha·F, including equilibrium carriers whose density changes strongly across the CDW transition and with temperature, so the mechanism would predict different curves (different prefactors) at 23 K and 300 K; their collapse onto one curve "within experimental uncertainties" argues against the specific mechanism invoked. Rough estimates also suggest plasmon periods well below 50 fs at these densities, far shorter than the measured 50–190 fs. The authors should: fit the 23 K and 300 K data separately, with and without tau0, report prefactors and offsets with uncertainties, and compare competing functional forms with chi-squared or information criteria; add fluences, especially below 0.6 mJ/cm², where the forms differ most; give a quantitative estimate of the photoexcited (and equilibrium) carrier density and the resulting plasmon period at each fluence and compare it with the measured times, as in ref. 40; measure the same window and fluence series under matched pump conditions on a reference where excitonic correlations are absent or much weaker (TiSe2 well above the fluctuation regime, e.g. ≥400 K if reachable; an isostructural non-CDW compound such as TiS2 or ZrSe2, or a metallic 1T dichalcogenide; or electron-doped, e.g. Cu-intercalated, TiSe2), analysed with the same Eq. (1) fitting; measure tau at fixed low fluence as a function of temperature through and well above Tc, since weakening excitonic correlations should show up in tau(T); and, if feasible, use the TDDFT capability already in the paper to simulate the rise time of this feature at several fluences in a model without excitonic interactions. Without these steps the abstract's "direct time-domain evidence for incoherent excitonic correlations" is not supported and should be softened to "consistent with".

2. The order-parameter-like onset in Fig. 3d,e may come from temperature-dependent spectroscopic and extrinsic effects rather than from the order itself (Fig. 3, Fig. 1o–q, pp. 8–9, Methods). The temperature scans are taken at a single delay (200 fs), a single incident fluence (0.2 mJ/cm²) and fixed integration windows, and at least four effects need to be excluded. (a) Delay choice: window ii carries a strong 6 THz A1g oscillation (Fig. 2d; period about 167 fs), so the 200 fs value samples a phonon phase, and the phonon amplitude, frequency and damping all change across Tc; in window iii the initial response time at 0.2 mJ/cm² is about 150–190 fs (Fig. 4e), so 200 fs still lies on the rising edge, and any temperature dependence of the rise time, or of time zero during the temperature ramp, feeds directly into the amplitude in Fig. 3e. (b) Edge shift through a fixed window: the static peak-2 position shifts by about 40 meV between 25 and 310 K (Fig. 1p), while the window-iii transient has a derivative-like lineshape (Fig. 3c: a negative lobe near 53.2 eV and a positive lobe near 53.4 eV at 25 K, within about 0.5 eV) and is integrated over a fixed 0.29 eV window (53.04–53.33 eV); a fixed-window integral of a derivative-shaped signal is very sensitive to such a shift. (c) Lineshape: the derivative shape is typical of a shift or broadening of a peak whose static width narrows on cooling, so a larger magnitude at 25 K may reflect a sharper static peak rather than "additional Se 4p holes created via the breaking of condensed excitons". (d) Absorbed energy: the optical constants of TiSe2 in the 1.2–2.5 eV (500–1000 nm) pump range change across Tc, so the absorbed energy density at fixed incident fluence, and hence every transient amplitude, can change at Tc for reasons unrelated to the excitonic picture. The authors should show full delay traces at several temperatures across Tc (at minimum about 25, 150, 190–200, 210, 250 and 300–310 K) and plot fitted amplitudes, with the incoherent and phonon parts separated, or the plateau amplitude, against temperature; integrate in a window that tracks the edge position, or decompose the transient into peak shift, broadening and amplitude components at each temperature and delay; normalise the Fig. 3d,e amplitudes to the absorbed fluence at each temperature using measured or literature reflectivity and transmission of the flake at the pump spectrum; and show that a non-specific, incoherent-carrier-dominated window does not display the same onset.

3. Window iii is used both as a reporter of long-range order (Fig. 3e) and as a probe blind to long-range coherence (Fig. 4), and these two uses conflict. On p. 9 the Se peak 2 transient amplitude "shows a clear onset right at Tc" and Fig. 3e is described as "quasi-constant above Tc", which presents it as a probe of long-range order. On p. 10 the same feature is said to be "agnostic about the existence of long-range phase coherence among the excitons" because it reflects localized dipole transitions, which is what justifies reading the 300 K fluence dependence as short-range correlations. If window iii were sensitive to local excitonic pairing, which the paper argues persists above Tc, the fluctuations should produce a tail above Tc in Fig. 3e, as the ARPES replica-band weight and gap do in Fig. 1e,f; if its amplitude is set by long-range order, the paper must explain why its kinetics are insensitive to it, and why the amplitude is insensitive to short-range correlations while the rise time is sensitive. The excitonic interpretation in Section 3 depends on this distinction. The authors should reconcile the two readings with a concrete model of what the window-iii amplitude and rise time each measure, plot Fig. 3e and Fig. 1e on a common normalised temperature axis and comment on the difference between the "quasi-constant" transient and the ARPES fluctuation tail, and report how the amplitude and the rise time behave together as functions of temperature at fixed low fluence, which is the more direct test of a precursor regime.

4. The "absence" of a static signature of the transition is not established at a sensitivity comparable to the transient measurement (abstract; pp. 5–6; Fig. 1j–l, o–q). The static XAS and XPS data are reduced to fitted peak positions, with scatter of about 10–20 meV, whereas the transient effect is an integrated transmission change of order 10⁻³ in specific windows. Changes in lineshape, width or integrated spectral weight, particularly in the broad Ti 38–53 eV many-body continuum that the paper later finds most sensitive, are not analysed, and for window ii the static absorption above and below Tc is never compared over the same energy window at the same signal-to-noise. No sensitivity estimate is given, and Fig. 1o appears to show a change in slope in the 150–200 K range. The headline contrast ("equilibrium measurements … showed no observable signals") is therefore partly a contrast between analyses rather than techniques, and it is central to the paper's framing. The authors should show static difference spectra A(E,T) − A(E,310 K) (or relative to 300 K), normalised like the transient data, and the temperature-integrated static signal in windows ii and iii in the same format as Fig. 3d,e, with error bars and the noise floor stated; report peak widths and integrated intensities versus temperature, not only positions; show the XPS Ti 3p and Se 3d lineshapes (widths, asymmetry) across Tc; fit or differentiate the peak-position curves in Fig. 1o–q for slope changes at Tc; and give a quantified upper bound on static changes. If the static integrated windows also show an onset, the claim that time resolution is required must be revised.

5. Sample heating and the true sample temperature are not quantified (Methods, pp. 18–20; Figs. 2–4). The XUV data come from free-standing exfoliated flakes (<100 nm thick, about 400 µm wide) on Cu TEM grids, pumped at 1 kHz with fluences up to 2.9 mJ/cm², in a cryostat without radiation shielding (deliberately omitted) and a base temperature of about 20 K. Thermal contact through a TEM grid is poor. The pre-time-zero signal is "uniformly subtracted" with the statement that "millisecond cumulative heating effects … do not change the conclusions" (p. 18), but this is asserted, not shown. A heating offset would shift the apparent Tc in Fig. 3d,e, and would make the "23 K" and "300 K" labels in Fig. 4 uncertain, particularly across the fluence series in Fig. 4a,b where heating grows with fluence; if the high-fluence 23 K spot sits tens of kelvin above base, or even above Tc, the below/above-Tc comparison in Fig. 4e is undermined. In addition, the pump penetration depth at 750 nm is comparable to or smaller than the flake thickness, so the transmission XUV probe averages over a depth-graded excitation, which complicates any scaling with incident F. The authors should estimate the steady-state and per-pulse temperature rise (absorbed energy, heat capacity, conduction to the grid), for example using the static peak shifts in Fig. 1o–q as an in-situ thermometer on the pre-time-zero spectra; show the pre-time-zero signal versus temperature and fluence; check that the Fig. 3d,e onset is the same on warming and cooling ramps and does not shift with fluence (e.g. repeat the temperature scan at a second fluence); and give the excitation depth profile for each flake and discuss how depth averaging affects the fitted rise times.

6. The fluence regime, fluence calibration and the assumed proportionality n ∝ F are not justified (pp. 7–8, 10; Methods p. 18). The text describes 0.2 mJ/cm² as "commensurate with a fluence regime where the CDW order and excitonic correlations are close to be fully suppressed" (p. 8) and 1.7 mJ/cm² as an order of magnitude above the melting threshold (p. 7); it is then unclear why 0.2 mJ/cm² counts as the low-fluence, perturbative regime that reveals equilibrium order in Fig. 3. Thresholds from the literature (refs. 14, 30–34) were measured at different wavelengths, thicknesses and geometries, so comparing incident fluences is not meaningful. With a 3.4 fs pulse, 1.7–2.9 mJ/cm² corresponds to peak intensities of order 10¹² W/cm², where multiphoton absorption and saturation are plausible and would break the linearity n ∝ F at the heart of the sqrt(n) argument. The authors should give the absorbed fluence and absorbed energy density per formula unit (accounting for flake thickness, reflectivity and transmission through a free-standing flake) and the excited-carrier density; restate the fluence regimes in terms of absorbed energy and quote 0.2 mJ/cm² as a ratio to the literature thresholds for both the electronic and the lattice order; and show the incoherent signal amplitude in each window (in particular windows ii and iii at 0.2 ps at base temperature) versus fluence, to demonstrate linearity or quantify the deviation and to locate any threshold or crossover.

14. The calculations, and the "many-body continuum" assignment that rests on them, are under-documented and used only qualitatively (pp. 5–6; Methods p. 20; Fig. 1g; Supplementary Note 1, Fig. S3). The broad 38–53 eV Ti feature is assigned to valence-electron excitations accompanying the core transition on the basis of TDDFT and ref. 22, but the delayed, broadened Ti M2,3 edge in early 3d metals is also commonly attributed to 3p–3d exchange and multiplet effects (a giant resonance with super-Coster-Kronig decay); under that reading, transient changes in window ii would reflect local 3d occupation or screening rather than CDW-related valence excitations. The Methods describe only ground-state PBE DFT: the TDDFT settings (core-hole treatment, whether Ti 3p is treated as valence in the pseudopotential and in the propagation, real-time propagation parameters, kernel), the spin-orbit treatment, and whether the 24-atom CDW cell is relaxed or taken from experiment, with what distortion amplitude and resulting gap, are not given. PBE is known to misdescribe the TiSe2 gap and band overlap and does not by itself stabilise the CDW gap seen in ARPES. No calculated transient or temperature-dependent spectrum is compared with Figs. 2–3, and it is not shown whether TDDFT reproduces the sign reversal of the continuum signal between the CDW and normal states. The authors should describe the TDDFT in the main Methods, discuss the multiplet alternative, state the CDW structure and test sensitivity to the functional (e.g. a hybrid functional or DFT+U), and, if feasible, compute the change in absorption in windows ii and iii between normal and CDW structures, or for a photoexcited carrier distribution, to compare with Fig. 3b,d.

15. Novelty relative to prior XUV transient absorption on TiSe2 (ref. 22) is not delineated (abstract; Introduction; Discussion p. 11). The paper claims "the first demonstration where an attosecond core-level technique … can simultaneously probe low-energy phase transitions" and that core-level spectroscopies are "not traditionally regarded as an appropriate probe for long-range order". Ref. 22 (Heinrich et al., PRX 2023) already reported "electronic and structural fingerprints of CDW excitations" in XUV transient absorption of 1T-TiSe2 at the same edges, and the present manuscript relies on it for the static temperature shift and the many-body continuum assignment; ref. 23 reported temperature-dependent hybridisation in core-level spectroscopy. Transient optical and photoemission probes are also well known to show order-parameter-like onsets at Tc in TiSe2. The authors should state precisely what ref. 22 observed (including any temperature dependence across Tc, coherent amplitude-mode signatures or Ti-edge changes) and what is new here (e.g. few-fs resolution, simultaneous Se-edge coverage, temperature scans through Tc, the normal-state fluence dependence), explain what core-level element specificity adds beyond optical and photoemission probes, and moderate the "first demonstration" and "unambiguously" wording accordingly.
