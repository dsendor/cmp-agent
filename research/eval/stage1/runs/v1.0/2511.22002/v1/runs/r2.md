Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper uses SI-STM on CsV3Sb5-xSnx (x = 0.1, 0.33, 0.68) and CsV3-yTiySb5 (y = 0.2) to ask whether electronic rotational symmetry breaking in AV3Sb5 needs the 2x2 CDW. The authors report nanoscale regions with unidirectional modulations: about 1/2 Q_Bragg modulations in dI/dV maps of the Sn-doped samples, and anisotropic Bragg-peak amplitude in topographs of the Ti-doped sample. They conclude that short-range electronic nematicity is present across the phase diagram without the CDW and is the "parent state" of kagome superconductors. The question matters, and imaging doped samples where the CDW is gone is a sensible way to separate the two orders. As written, though, the evidence is qualitative and comes from a few small regions. It does not yet rule out the simpler explanation that dopant disorder alone produces locally anisotropic patterns. The "parent state" and "unified phase diagram" claims go well beyond what was measured: one temperature (4.5 K), three or four compositions, no quantitative order parameter.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- It tackles a well-defined, widely debated question (nematicity tied to the CDW or separate from it) with a direct real-space probe.
- Two chemically distinct substitution routes are used: Sn on the Sb site and Ti in the kagome plane.
- The topographs and linecuts (Fig. 1c-k, Fig. 4b inset) clearly confirm that the 2x2 CDW is suppressed at the studied compositions.
- DFT constant-energy contours and their autocorrelation (Fig. 2i-l) give a plausible explanation of why the 1/2 Q_Bragg features appear only at some energies.
- The domain boundary imaged in the Ti-doped sample (Fig. 4a, e, f) is a visually suggestive observation.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. No quantitative evidence that the "domains" differ from the anisotropy expected from random, isotropic disorder. The nematic claim for the Sn-doped samples rests on three hand-picked boxes in each of Fig. 3a and 3c (about 7 nm and 5 nm on a side) and their FTs (Fig. 3b, d). In any finite window of a disordered QPI pattern, the three symmetry-equivalent 1/2 Q_Bragg directions will have unequal intensity by chance. With a window of about 6 to 8 modulation periods, and FT peaks that are already about 20% of Q_Bragg wide (p. 4), this statistical anisotropy can be large. Picking the three windows that each look most unidirectional will always yield three "domain types". The paper gives no anisotropy order parameter, no map of it, no distribution and no null comparison. The authors should:
   - define a local nematic order parameter, for example eta(r) = (|A_A| - (|A_B| + |A_C|)/2)/sum from lock-in demodulation at the three 1/2 Q vectors with a stated filter length;
   - map it over the full fields of view;
   - report its histogram and spatial correlation length;
   - compare these with the same analysis on synthetic isotropic data (phase-randomized surrogates, or the measured map rotated or symmetrized), and on the pristine sample away from the CDW wave vector.
   Only a correlation length or anisotropy amplitude significantly above that null supports "short-range ordered electronic nematic domains". The same applies to the mirror-symmetry statement ("breaks two in-plane mirror symmetries but appears to generally preserve" the third, p. 5 and p. 6), which is currently by eye.

2. Anisotropic QPI is not shown to reflect an anisotropic electronic state rather than an anisotropic scatterer configuration. On p. 4 the 1/2 Q_Bragg features are attributed to quasiparticle scattering between M-point pockets (Fig. 2f, i, j). If so, the intensity along each direction in a small window is set by the local arrangement of scatterers (Sn dopants, vacancies, Cs adatoms) as much as by the band structure. A cluster of dopants aligned along one direction will give unidirectional interference with no electronic nematicity. The paper does not:
   - locate the dopants (the Sn substituents at the in-plane Sb site, which should be visible in topographs at suitable bias);
   - test whether the domain orientation correlates with local dopant geometry;
   - show that the dispersion or wave vector of q differs between the three domain types, which would be the signature of a locally distorted Fermi surface rather than anisotropic scattering amplitude.
   The authors should do one or more of these. For example, they could compare the q-magnitude and its energy dependence within the A, B and C domains, or simulate QPI (T-matrix on the Wannier model of the Methods) with the measured dopant positions and show that the simulated pattern is isotropic on the domain scale while the data are not.

3. The Ti-doped evidence is a lattice-intensity anisotropy over a few nanometres, and its electronic and intrinsic origin is not established. Fig. 4 relies on Bragg-peak amplitude maps (Fig. 4e, f) over an image about 6 nm across, with "domains" of 2 to 3 nm. That is comparable to the Fourier filter width used to make the amplitude maps, which the paper does not state. Unequal Bragg-peak amplitudes in a topograph can come from several sources:
   - local strain from Ti substitution, which is a structural effect rather than electronic nematicity;
   - an asymmetric tip;
   - scan-direction and feedback effects, or uncorrected drift;
   - setpoint effects at the single bias used (100 mV).
   The argument that different orientations in one image rule out the tip (p. 7) excludes only a uniform tip anisotropy. The authors should:
   - state the filter width and drift-correction procedure;
   - show that the Bragg-amplitude anisotropy pattern is the same in images taken with rotated scan directions and at several biases, including both polarities;
   - show it over larger fields of view and more than one region and cleave;
   - quantify it against a null, as in concern 1;
   - discuss local strain from Ti as an alternative.
   As the text concedes, momentum-resolved QPI was not obtained in this sample. So the "consistent with" statement for Sn and Ti doping (p. 6) compares two different observables: QPI anisotropy versus Bragg-peak amplitude.

4. The "parent state" and "pervasive across the phase diagram" claims are not supported by the measurements shown.
   - All data are at 4.5 K. Yet Fig. 1b paints "Electronic nematicity" over the entire composition range (x = 0 to 1) and up to 100 K. No temperature dependence is measured, so the data cannot show that the state exists above the CDW or superconducting transitions, or that it is "parent" to them in any sense.
   - At x = 0.1 the paper's own nematicity marker, the 1/2 Q_Bragg feature, is absent (p. 4, Extended Data Fig. 1). The only anisotropy there is the incommensurate charge stripe known from earlier work, which is a different phenomenon. So the evidence for continuity between the pristine nematic state (CDW-linked, refs. 21, 27, 34) and the high-doping domains is missing at exactly the composition that connects them.
   - The pristine sample is not analysed with the same metric.
   - The superconducting Tc of the measured crystals is not given.
   The authors should either (a) measure the anisotropy metric at elevated temperature (for example above the pristine T_CDW, around 100 K, or at least at several temperatures in the doped samples) and apply the same metric to x = 0 and x = 0.1, or (b) substantially narrow the title, abstract and Fig. 1b to what was measured: low-temperature, short-range anisotropy at x = 0.33, 0.68 and y = 0.2.

B. Should address

5. There is a tension between "not CDW" and the labels and interpretation used. On p. 4 the authors argue that the broad 1/2 Q_Bragg peaks are QPI, not short-range 2x2 CDW. Yet Fig. 3b, d label the domain wave vectors Q^A_CDW, Q^B_CDW, Q^C_CDW, and p. 6 calls the state "q = 0 electronic nematicity" while the evidence is a finite-q modulation. Three reasons are given against residual short-range CDW (absence at x ~ 0.1, energy selectivity, slight dispersion), but they are not decisive:
   - short-range CDW correlations pinned by disorder can have energy-dependent contrast;
   - "slight dispersion" is not quantified; the reader is pointed to Extended Data Fig. 2, which is not included in this submission.
   The authors should plot the peak position against energy with error bars and compare it with the dispersion predicted by the DFT autocorrelation. They should also state explicitly how a q = 0 nematic state is inferred from finite-q modulations, and relabel Fig. 3.

6. The DFT comparison is qualitative and at a mismatched composition and energy reference. The CECs are computed for x ~ 0.25 (p. 5), not the measured 0.33. They use linear interpolation of hopping between CsV3Sb5 and CsV3Sb4Sn, at kz = 0 only. The p. 5 statement that the 1/2 Q_Bragg vector connects "exactly" the M pockets depends on where the chemical potential sits, which hole doping by Sn changes. The authors should:
   - give the calculation at the measured x, together with how the Fermi level was set;
   - show how robust the M-pocket nesting is to kz and to an energy offset;
   - compare quantitatively the energy window where the 1/2 Q feature is predicted with the measured window (the ellipse in Fig. 2d spans roughly -300 to +100 meV, while only -100 and -500 meV are calculated).
   Because the computed CECs are by construction C6-symmetric, they explain the wave vector but not the anisotropy. The paper should state this clearly.

7. Sample statistics and reproducibility are missing. The paper does not say how many cleaves, fields of view and tips were used for each composition, or whether domains were seen in all of them. For x = 0.68 and y = 0.2, single images are shown. The authors should report these numbers and show the anisotropy analysis of concern 1 for at least two independent regions or cleaves per composition.

8. Compositions are nominal and inconsistent in the text. The text says Sn compositions x = 0.15, 0.33, 0.68 (p. 3), while the figures and the rest of the text use x = 0.1 (Fig. 1d, h, k; p. 4). Fig. 1b appears to show four diamond markers, while the caption refers to "four blue circles". Only the growth ratios of the x ~ 0.33 and x ~ 0.68 crystals are given in Methods; there is no growth recipe for the x ~ 0.1 crystal and no measured composition (EDS/WDS, refined occupancies) for any sample. The Ti sample is called CsV3-xTixSb5 with x = 0.2 in Methods, CsV3-xTixSb5 with y = 0.2 on p. 5, and CsV3-ySb5Tiy in Fig. 4. Measured compositions, ideally with local dopant density counted from topographs, are needed because the central argument is about composition.

9. Setpoint and normalization effects are not addressed. The dI/dV maps in Figs. 2 and 3 use different setpoints: -200 mV/500 pA, 50 mV/200 pA, and 0.6 GOhm/1 nA. In a strongly inhomogeneous doped sample, the setpoint effect can imprint topographic or integrated-DOS anisotropy onto g(r, V). The authors should show the domain pattern in L(r, V) = (dI/dV)/(I/V) or in maps taken at a different setpoint bias, and confirm that the same regions carry the same orientation.

10. The relation to disorder is left open, but it is central. On p. 6 the authors propose that the short-range nature "likely arises due to increased disorder caused by the dopants". If disorder sets the domain size, the authors should show how domain size and anisotropy amplitude change with x (0.33 against 0.68) and between Sn and Ti doping. If they do not change systematically, that would weaken a disorder-pinned-nematic picture relative to disorder-generated anisotropy. This analysis would also help separate concern 1's null hypothesis from the claimed state.

C. Minor

11. Extended Data Figs. 1 to 3 are cited for key arguments (absence of the 1/2 Q feature at x ~ 0.1, energy dependence, a second Ti domain boundary) but are not included in the submission reviewed here. They should be provided.

12. Fig. 1b uses a broken temperature axis, and its shaded "Electronic nematicity" region extends to x = 1 and above 80 K without data (see concern 4). The marker shape (diamonds) does not match the caption ("circles").

13. Fig. 2 caption: the red dashed linecut direction is drawn in the insets of panels a and g, but panel g is an FT map, not an inset. Please clarify. Also state the FT symmetrization and drift-correction procedure used throughout (none is mentioned in Methods).

14. Fig. 4e, f: give the colour-scale values and the Fourier filter width. The orange dashed boundary appears to have been drawn by hand; state how it was determined.

15. Methods: the DFT text says CsV3Sb4Sn has Sn "within the kagome plane", which differs from the Sb-site wording on p. 3. Please make it consistent (in-plane Sb1 site). Also give the Wannier-fit energy window and the kz handling used for the autocorrelation.

16. The comparison with ref. 44 (Ti-doped CsV3Sb5, chiral-nematic parent state) should be more than a note. The two works appear to draw related conclusions (nematic versus chiral-nematic) from the same material. A short discussion of agreements and differences, especially on mirror symmetry, would help readers.

17. Data are "available upon reasonable request". Given that the conclusions rest on image analysis, depositing the raw maps would strengthen the work.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Build the paper around a quantitative local nematic order parameter (lock-in demodulation at the three 1/2 Q or Bragg vectors), mapped over large fields of view. Report its correlation length and histogram against a disorder-only null model and against the pristine sample.
2. Add temperature-dependent imaging of at least one CDW-free composition, including above the pristine T_CDW, to support or replace the "parent state" language.
3. Combine dopant-resolved topography with T-matrix QPI simulations from the Wannier model to test whether the observed anisotropy exceeds what the measured dopant configuration alone produces.
4. For the Ti-doped sample, obtain spectroscopic maps (QPI or energy-resolved Bragg amplitudes) and scan-rotation controls, so that the two dopant families are compared using the same electronic observable.
