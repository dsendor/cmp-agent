Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper uses SI-STM at 4.5 K on CsV3Sb5 with Sn substituted on the Sb site (x ≈ 0.1, 0.33, 0.68) and Ti substituted on the V site (y = 0.2), where the long-range 2×2 CDW is suppressed. It reports nanoscale regions in which modulations at about ½Q_Bragg (Sn) or atomic Bragg-peak amplitudes (Ti) are stronger along one lattice direction, and it concludes that electronic nematicity, unrelated to the 2×2 CDW, is the parent state of the AV3Sb5 phase diagram. Whether rotational symmetry breaking in AV3Sb5 is a by-product of the CDW stacking or an independent instability is an important and debated question. Data from two chemically distinct dopant sites would be a useful contribution. However, the evidence presented is a small number of hand-selected, few-nanometre windows at a single temperature in strongly disordered alloys. That supports "local anisotropy exists" much more than "pervasive electronic nematicity is the parent state".

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- It addresses a central open question in kagome superconductors with a well-chosen strategy: suppress the CDW chemically and test whether the anisotropy survives.
- It uses two substitution sites (Sb site and V kagome site), so the result does not rest on one dopant chemistry.
- It provides clear evidence that the long-range 2×2 CDW and the charge stripes are absent at x ≥ 0.33 and y = 0.2 (Fig. 1g–k, Fig. 4b inset).
- It explains the broad ½Q_Bragg features at x = 0.33 as quasiparticle interference (QPI) between M-point pockets using DFT constant-energy contours and their autocorrelation (Fig. 2i–l), which is a reasonable and testable explanation.
- Domains of different orientation appear within one field of view imaged with the same tip (Figs. 3, 4), which argues against a purely tip-induced anisotropy.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. No quantitative, unbiased measure of nematic order, and no comparison with what isotropic disorder would produce. The nematic claim rests on three hand-picked boxes per field of view (Fig. 3b,d; each roughly 5–8 nm across) and one domain boundary in a ~7 nm topograph (Fig. 4a). In a random alloy with 7–14% of Sb sites substituted, any small window will show some directional imbalance of QPI or Bragg intensity just from the local dopant arrangement and finite-window statistics. The paper does not show that the observed imbalance is larger than this baseline. The authors should:
   - define a local anisotropy order parameter, e.g. η(r) = (I_A − (I_B + I_C)/2)/(I_A + I_B + I_C), or the full three-component (Potts) vector built from the ½Q (or Bragg) intensities in a sliding window;
   - map it over the entire fields of view in Figs. 3a,c and 4a;
   - report its histogram, its spatial autocorrelation (giving a domain size or correlation length) and how it depends on window size;
   - compare these with the same analysis on (i) simulated QPI from randomly placed point scatterers on the DFT band structure with no nematic term, and (ii) the same STM data with the pixels shuffled or symmetrized.
   Only if the measured anisotropy clearly exceeds the disorder-only baseline does "short-range nematic domains" follow. Without this, Figs. 3 and 4 are illustrations rather than evidence.

2. Local strain and dopant arrangement as an alternative to electronic nematicity. Sn and Ti substitution creates local lattice distortions. A dopant cluster or a locally anisotropic strain field would produce exactly the observed signatures without any electronic instability: a directional QPI response or a Bragg-amplitude imbalance along one direction, with domains of all three orientations. The Ti-doped evidence in particular (Fig. 4c–f) is an anisotropy of the atomic Bragg-peak amplitude in constant-current topographs at a single bias (100 mV). That quantity is sensitive to:
   - local structural distortion;
   - piezo calibration and drift correction (Lawler–Fujita);
   - tip shape.
   The paper should:
   - show that the anisotropy is electronic, by its bias dependence (does η change sign or magnitude with energy, as an electronic nematic Fermi surface would imply, or is it bias-independent, as a structural distortion would be?);
   - extract local lattice constants or a strain map from the same topographs to test whether domain orientation correlates with local lattice distortion;
   - where possible, correlate domain orientation and boundaries with the positions of identifiable dopants (Sn appears as distinct contrast in Fig. 1d–f).
   The text itself notes (p. 7) that strain [30] must be excluded for the chirality question. The same applies to the nematicity claim.

3. The "parent state" claim and the phase diagram are not supported by temperature-dependent data. Every measurement is at 4.5 K. Fig. 1b nonetheless shades an "Electronic nematicity" region (green) across the whole doping range up to the 100 K top of the axis, above the CDW transition. That claim cannot be made from 4.5 K data. "Parent state of kagome superconductors" (title, abstract, p. 7) means a state that forms at higher temperature and out of which superconductivity and other low-temperature orders emerge. The paper shows neither the onset temperature of the anisotropy nor any link to superconductivity in these samples. The authors should either:
   - measure the anisotropy order parameter of concern 1 at several temperatures (e.g. 4.5, 20, 50, 80 K, or wherever the STM permits) for at least one composition, or bring in bulk probes (elastoresistance, birefringence, nematic susceptibility) on the same crystals; or
   - remove the green region from Fig. 1b and recast "parent state" as "a low-temperature local anisotropy that persists without the CDW".
   Superconducting Tc of the measured crystals should also be reported, since Fig. 1b implies all compositions are superconducting.

4. The link between the doped-sample anisotropy and the nematicity reported in pristine AV3Sb5 is not established. The paper's logic is that the nematicity seen near the CDW onset in pristine samples [21, 27, 34, 38] is the same phenomenon as the anisotropy seen here once the CDW is gone. However, the observables differ:
   - in the pristine compound, a long-range, uniform C2 anisotropy of CDW-peak or QPI intensities;
   - here, short-range anisotropy of disorder-broadened QPI near ½Q_Bragg (Sn) or of Bragg amplitudes (Ti).
   The x ≈ 0.1 sample, which sits between the two regimes, shows unidirectional stripe correlations but no ½Q_Bragg features (p. 4; Extended Data Fig. 1). The same analysis is therefore not shown for it, and continuity across the phase diagram is not demonstrated. The authors should:
   - apply one identical anisotropy measure to x = 0, x ≈ 0.1, x = 0.33 and x = 0.68 (and, for the Bragg-amplitude measure, to pristine and Ti-doped topographs taken under matched setpoints);
   - show how the magnitude and correlation length evolve with x.
   This is the core of the claim "unrelated to the 2×2 CDW" and "across the AV3Sb5 phase diagram". A related point concerns ref. 44, which is cited as parallel work on Ti-doped CsV3Sb5 and proposes a "chiral-nematic Fermi liquid" parent state. The authors should state what is new here relative to ref. 44 and whether the results agree.

B. Should address

5. Energy dependence of the domain anisotropy is not shown. QPI-based nematicity should show the same domain orientation persisting over a range of energies wherever the ½Q feature exists (roughly −300 to 0 meV, according to the red ellipse in Fig. 2d). Figs. 3a and 3c are each a single energy (−200 mV and +50 mV). The authors should show the same boxes at several energies within the window of Fig. 2d, and report whether domain orientation and boundaries are fixed or change with energy. Orientation that changes with energy would point to scattering from local defect configurations rather than to a nematic Fermi surface.

6. Setpoint effect. The maps in Fig. 3 are taken with the setpoint bias inside the energy window where the ½Q feature appears (−200 mV for x = 0.33). The topographic imprint of the setpoint can then transfer spatially varying, direction-dependent contrast into g(r,V). The authors should show L(r,V) = (dI/dV)/(I/V) maps or maps taken at a setpoint outside the relevant window, and check that the anisotropy persists.

7. Statistics: samples, cleaves, fields of view, tips. The paper does not state how many crystals, cleaves, fields of view and tips were used per composition. Nor does it state how many regions were examined and what fraction show the anisotropy. "Persists in all samples" (abstract) and "pervasive" (title) require this. Please give a table listing, per composition, the number of samples, cleaves, fields of view, and the area fraction above an anisotropy threshold. Show at least one Sn composition with data from a second crystal or cleave.

8. Actual composition is not measured; nominal and quoted values are inconsistent.
   - For Ti, the Methods give a nominal load of Cs20V12Ti3.5Sb120, which corresponds to Ti/(V+Ti)×3 ≈ 0.68 in the formula unit, yet the sample is labelled y = 0.2.
   - For Sn, the nominal Sb:Sn ratios (80:40 and 60:60) lie far above the quoted x ≈ 0.33 and 0.68.
   Flux growth makes these differences plausible, but the doping axis of the whole phase diagram then rests on unstated measurements. Please give EDS/WDS or diffraction-refined compositions for the actual crystals measured, with their spread. Ideally also give the STM-counted dopant density from topographs, and bulk characterization (resistivity or susceptibility showing the absence of the CDW anomaly and the Tc). Composition inhomogeneity is itself an alternative source of domains.

9. The DFT comparison is semi-quantitative and partly mismatched to the data.
   - The calculation is done at x ≈ 0.25 while the data are at x = 0.33.
   - It uses linear interpolation of Wannier hoppings between CsV3Sb5 and CsV3Sb4Sn (a virtual-crystal-type approach with no disorder broadening), and only the kz = 0 contour.
   - The Methods state both that "all first-principles calculations were performed on the pristine phase" and that a CsV3Sb4Sn Hamiltonian was constructed. Please clarify which structures were relaxed and with what settings. The 300 eV cutoff should be justified or a convergence check given.
   More importantly, the claim that the ½Q features "would" arise from M-pocket scattering would be stronger if the predicted energy window over which the M pockets support q ≈ ½Q_Bragg (Fig. 2i vs 2k) were compared quantitatively with the measured window in Fig. 2d. Please also discuss kz dispersion and any rigid shift of the Fermi level needed for agreement. Note that the DFT is C6-symmetric. It explains the wavevector but not the anisotropy, and the paper should say so explicitly.

10. The mirror-symmetry statement is not quantified. The text states that each domain "breaks two in-plane mirror symmetries but appears to generally preserve" the third (pp. 5–6). It also discusses a possible further transition to a chiral state (p. 7). Please quantify the intensities of the FT peaks mirror-related across the preserved axis, with uncertainties, for each domain type. Without this, the claim about the residual symmetry, and its comparison with CsTi3Bi5 [42, 43], cannot be assessed.

11. Wording: "q = 0 nematicity" versus finite-q modulations. The Discussion calls the state "short-range q = 0 electronic nematicity". The observable, however, is anisotropy of modulations at q ≈ ½Q_Bragg, and the figures label them Q^A,B,C_CDW (Fig. 3b,d). The paper argues these are not CDW. Using "Q_CDW" labels for them is confusing, and the step from a finite-q anisotropic QPI pattern to a q = 0 nematic order parameter should be stated explicitly.

12. Tip artifacts are only partly excluded. Seeing domains of different orientation with one tip rules out a uniform tip anisotropy. It does not rule out a tip that enhances whatever local anisotropy is already present. Showing the same area with a second tip, or after a controlled tip change, would strengthen the claim. The authors themselves invoke tip anisotropy [41] as a concern for chirality.

C. Minor

13. The Extended Data Figs. 1–3, cited for key steps, are not included in the submission:
   - absence of ½Q peaks at x ≈ 0.1;
   - the dispersion of the ½Q feature;
   - the second Ti domain boundary.
   They should be provided for review.

14. Composition labels are inconsistent: the Results text (p. 3) lists Sn compositions as x = 0.15, 0.33, 0.68, while Fig. 1 and the rest of the text use x = 0.1 or x ≈ 0.1. Fig. 1b's caption mentions "four blue circles" but the figure shows diamonds, and their x positions do not obviously match the stated compositions. The Ti compound is written CsV3−xTixSb5 (text, Methods, with x = 0.2) and CsV3−ySb5Tiy (Fig. 4 title).

15. Fig. 1b uses a broken and nonlinear temperature axis. Please state the scale and the sources of the phase boundaries (bulk references [39, 40]), and distinguish data points from schematic boundaries.

16. Fig. 4e,f: please give the Fourier filter width used for the Bragg amplitude maps. The apparent domain size in a 5 nm image is comparable to the filter length scale, so the domain boundary position depends on this choice.

17. Fig. 2: please state the FT symmetrization or drift-correction procedure, if any. Give the field-of-view size for the FT linecuts in Fig. 2b,d, which set the q resolution relevant to the "~20% of Q_Bragg" width quoted on p. 4.

18. Quoting a characteristic domain size (e.g. from the autocorrelation in concern 1) would make "nanoscale" and "short-range" concrete. It would also allow comparison with the average dopant spacing, which bears directly on the suggestion (p. 6) that disorder limits the correlation length.

19. "Data available upon reasonable request": given that the central claim depends on how windows were chosen, please deposit the raw maps.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Build the paper around one quantitative anisotropy order-parameter map applied identically to all compositions (x = 0, 0.1, 0.33, 0.68; y = 0 and 0.2). Show its distribution, correlation length and window-size dependence, and benchmark it against a disorder-only QPI simulation.
2. Add temperature dependence of that order parameter for at least one CDW-free composition, or bulk nematic-susceptibility data on the same crystals. This is the minimum needed to support the "parent state" language and the shading in Fig. 1b.
3. Test the strain and dopant-arrangement explanation directly. Combine local strain mapping from the Bragg peaks with dopant positions, and show bias dependence of the Ti Bragg-amplitude anisotropy.
4. Characterize the crystals actually measured (composition, Tc, absence of the CDW in bulk), and moderate the title and abstract to match what the data establish if the additional analyses are not possible.
