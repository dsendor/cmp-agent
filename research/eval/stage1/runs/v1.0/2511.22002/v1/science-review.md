A. Must address

1. No quantitative, unbiased measure of nematic order, and no comparison with the anisotropy that isotropic disorder alone would produce. For the Sn-doped samples, the nematic claim rests on three hand-picked boxes in each of Fig. 3a and 3c. Each box is roughly 5–8 nm across, and each was chosen because it "seemingly" shows modulations along one direction. The FTs of these boxes are in Fig. 3b,d. For Ti, the claim rests on two zooms (Fig. 4c,d) and one domain boundary in a ~6–8 nm topograph (Fig. 4a). In a random alloy with x = 0.33–0.68, dopant-induced QPI is intrinsically inhomogeneous. In any few-nm window, the three symmetry-equivalent ½Q_Bragg (or Bragg) directions will have unequal intensity by chance from the local dopant arrangement and finite-window statistics, even if the underlying electronic state is C6 symmetric. A window of only ~6–8 modulation periods, with FT peaks already ~20% of Q_Bragg wide (p. 4), can show a large statistical anisotropy. Picking the three most unidirectional windows will always yield three "domain types". The paper gives no order parameter, no map of it, no distribution and no null comparison, so Figs. 3 and 4 are currently illustrations rather than evidence. The authors should take the following steps. First, define a local anisotropy order parameter, e.g. η(r) = (|A_A| − (|A_B| + |A_C|)/2)/(|A_A| + |A_B| + |A_C|) or the full three-component (Potts) vector, from lock-in/Lawler–Fujita demodulation at the three ½Q (or Bragg) vectors with a stated filter length. Second, map it over the entire fields of view in Figs. 3a,c and 4a. Third, report its histogram, its spatial autocorrelation (correlation length and domain size), the fraction of area above an anisotropy threshold, and how these depend on window size. Fourth, compare all of these with the same analysis applied to null data: (i) QPI simulated from randomly placed isotropic point scatterers on the DFT band structure (or a C6-symmetric tight-binding model) at the measured dopant density, with no nematic term; (ii) phase-randomized, pixel-shuffled, rotated or symmetrized versions of the measured maps; and (iii) the pristine sample away from the CDW wave vector. "Short-range ordered electronic nematic domains" follows only if the measured anisotropy amplitude and correlation length clearly exceed this null.

2. Anisotropic QPI in the Sn-doped samples is not shown to reflect an anisotropic electronic state rather than an anisotropic arrangement of scatterers or local strain. On p. 4 the ½Q_Bragg features are attributed to quasiparticle scattering between M-point pockets (Fig. 2f, i, j). If that is right, the intensity along each direction in a small window is set by the local arrangement of scatterers (Sn dopants, vacancies, Cs adatoms) as much as by the band structure. A dopant cluster aligned along one direction, or a locally anisotropic strain field from substitution, would give unidirectional interference with domains of all three orientations and no electronic nematicity. The paper does not locate the dopants. Sn appears as distinct contrast in Fig. 1d–f and should be identifiable at suitable bias, including whether it sits on the in-plane Sb1 or the out-of-plane Sb2 site. Nor does the paper test whether domain orientation and boundaries correlate with local dopant geometry or a local lattice distortion. It also does not test whether the wave vector q or its dispersion differs between the A, B and C domain types, which would be the signature of a locally distorted Fermi surface rather than an anisotropic scattering amplitude. The authors should do one or more of the following: correlate domain orientation with dopant positions; extract a local strain map from the same topographs; compare q-magnitude and its energy dependence within each domain type; or run T-matrix QPI simulations on the Wannier model of the Methods with the measured dopant positions and show that the simulated pattern is isotropic on the domain scale while the data are not.

3. For the Ti-doped sample, an electronic origin is not separated from structural or instrumental ones, and the evidence is very limited. Fig. 4 rests on one ~6–8 nm constant-current topograph at a single bias (+100 mV, 100 pA), plus one more boundary in Extended Data Fig. 3. The observable is the anisotropy of atomic Bragg-peak amplitudes (Fig. 4e,f). The "domains" in it are ~1–3 nm across, comparable both to the Ti–Ti spacing at y = 0.2 (about one Ti per 15 V sites) and to the Fourier filter width used to make the amplitude maps, which is not stated. The domain boundary position therefore depends on that choice. Unequal Bragg amplitudes in a topograph can come from several sources, none of them electronic nematicity: local lattice distortion or strain around Ti or Ti clusters; local apparent-height variations; piezo calibration, creep, scan-direction and feedback effects or uncorrected drift (including in the Lawler–Fujita correction); an asymmetric tip; and setpoint effects at the single bias used. The authors should take five steps. (1) State the filter width and the drift-correction procedure. (2) Show the same areas at several biases of both polarities and in dI/dV maps. An electronic nematic Fermi surface should make η change in magnitude or sign with energy, while a structural distortion should be largely bias independent. (3) Perform a displacement-field (Lawler–Fujita) or local-lattice-constant analysis to test for local uniaxial strain, and correlate it with domain orientation and with Ti positions. (4) Repeat with rotated scan directions, over larger fields of view, and in more than one region, cleave and tip. (5) Quantify the result against the null of concern 1. As the text concedes, momentum-resolved QPI was not obtained in this sample. The statement that the Sn and Ti results are "consistent" (p. 6) therefore compares two different observables, QPI anisotropy and Bragg-peak amplitude. Since the claim of a generic phenomenon rests on that agreement, the Ti evidence needs to stand on its own. Ideally it would be supported by spectroscopic maps (QPI or energy-resolved Bragg amplitudes), so that the two dopant families are compared using the same electronic observable.

4. The Sn-sample evidence is a finite-q unidirectional modulation, and residual short-range CDW is not excluded; yet the paper calls it "q = 0 nematicity". The Discussion calls the state "short-range q = 0 electronic nematicity". However, all the Sn evidence is a locally 1Q modulation at ~½Q_Bragg (Fig. 3b,d), and the figures label these wave vectors Q^A_CDW, Q^B_CDW and Q^C_CDW. Unidirectional ½Q correlations are exactly what short-range, locally 1Q 2×2 CDW fluctuations or disorder- or strain-pinned remnants of the CDW would look like. The paper gives three arguments against residual CDW (p. 4): absence at x ≈ 0.1, appearance only at "some energies", and "slight dispersion". These are qualitative. Disorder-pinned short-range CDW correlations can also have energy-dependent contrast. The "slight dispersion" is not quantified, and the reader is pointed to Extended Data Fig. 2, which is not included. If residual short-range 1Q 2×2 correlations nucleated by disorder remain a live alternative, "unrelated to the 2×2 CDW" does not follow. The authors should do four things. First, plot the ½Q peak position against energy with error bars and compare it with the dispersion predicted by the DFT autocorrelation. Second, test whether the feature's contrast inverts or shifts across E_F, as expected for QPI but not for a static charge modulation. Third, show a q = 0 measure of C6 breaking in the Sn samples, such as the anisotropy of the three Bragg-peak amplitudes or an intra-unit-cell signal within each domain, as done for Ti in Fig. 4e,f, and show that it is spatially correlated with the orientation of the ½Q modulation. Fourth, state explicitly how a q = 0 nematic order parameter is inferred from finite-q modulations, and relabel Fig. 3 with a neutral label (e.g. q_½).

5. The "parent state" claim and the phase diagram are not supported by temperature-dependent data. Every measurement is at 4.5 K. Nonetheless, Fig. 1b shades an "Electronic nematicity" region (green) across the whole composition range, x = 0 to 1, including where no data exist (x between 0.68 and 1), and up to the 100 K top of the axis, above the CDW transition. "Parent state of kagome superconductors" (title, abstract, p. 7) means a state that forms at higher temperature and from which the CDW, superconductivity and other low-temperature orders emerge. It would also imply that the same order exists in pristine CsV3Sb5 above T_CDW. The paper shows no onset temperature for the anisotropy and no link to superconductivity in these samples. The superconducting Tc of the measured crystals is not reported, although Fig. 1b implies that all compositions are superconducting. The authors should choose one of two routes. (a) Measure the order parameter of concern 1 at several temperatures (e.g. 4.5, 20–30, 50, 80 K and above the pristine T_CDW, ~100 K) for at least one CDW-free composition, showing that it vanishes or weakens at higher temperature, or add bulk probes (elastoresistance, birefringence, nematic susceptibility) on the same crystals. (b) Remove or mark as schematic the green region in Fig. 1b, and restrict the title, abstract and Discussion to what was measured, e.g. "short-range local C6 breaking at 4.5 K persists in CDW-free Sn- and Ti-doped CsV3Sb5". In either case they should report Tc for the measured crystals.

6. Continuity with the nematicity reported in pristine AV3Sb5, and hence "pervasive" and "in all samples", is not demonstrated. The paper's logic is that the nematicity seen near the CDW onset in pristine samples (refs. 21, 27, 34, 38) is the same phenomenon as the anisotropy seen here once the CDW is gone. The observables differ, however. In the pristine compound the anisotropy is a long-range, uniform C2 anisotropy of CDW-peak or QPI intensities. Here it is a short-range anisotropy of disorder-broadened QPI near ½Q_Bragg (Sn) or of Bragg amplitudes (Ti). At x ≈ 0.1, which connects the two regimes, the paper's own marker of nematicity, the ½Q_Bragg feature, is absent (p. 4; Extended Data Fig. 1). The only anisotropy there is the incommensurate charge stripe known from earlier work, which is a different phenomenon. So under the paper's own criterion nematic domains are not demonstrated at x ≈ 0.1, which conflicts with "persists in all samples" (abstract), and the pristine sample is not analysed with the same metric. The authors should apply one identical anisotropy measure to x = 0, x ≈ 0.1, x = 0.33 and x = 0.68, and, for the Bragg-amplitude measure, to pristine and Ti-doped topographs taken under matched setpoints. They should then show how its magnitude and correlation length evolve with composition and address x ≈ 0.1 explicitly. This is the core of "unrelated to the 2×2 CDW" and "across the AV3Sb5 phase diagram".

B. Should address

7. Energy dependence of the domain orientation is not shown. Nematicity based on QPI should show the same domain orientation persisting over the range of energies where the ½Q feature exists. That range is roughly −300 to 0 (or +100) meV, according to the red ellipse in Fig. 2d. Yet Figs. 3a and 3c are each at a single energy (−200 mV and +50 mV). The authors should show the same boxes, or the order-parameter map of concern 1, at several energies within that window, and report whether domain orientation and boundaries are fixed or change with energy. An orientation that changes with energy would point to scattering from local defect configurations rather than to a nematic Fermi surface.
Raised by: r1

8. Setpoint and normalization effects are not addressed. The dI/dV maps in Figs. 2 and 3 use different setpoints: −200 mV/500 pA, 50 mV/200 pA, and 0.6 GΩ/1 nA. For x = 0.33, the setpoint bias lies inside the energy window where the ½Q feature appears. In a strongly inhomogeneous doped sample, the setpoint effect can then imprint direction-dependent topographic or integrated-DOS contrast onto g(r, V). The same concern applies to the single-bias Ti topograph (concern 3). The authors should show the domain pattern in L(r, V) = (dI/dV)/(I/V), or in maps taken at a setpoint bias outside the relevant window, and confirm that the same regions carry the same orientation.

9. Sample statistics and reproducibility are missing. The paper does not state, for each composition, how many crystals, cleaves, fields of view and tips were used, how many regions were examined, or in what fraction the anisotropy was seen. For x = 0.68 and y = 0.2, single images are shown. "Persists in all samples" (abstract) and "pervasive" (title) require these numbers. The "same tip" argument (p. 7) rules out a global tip anisotropy within one image. It does not establish reproducibility across samples, or that the domain statistics are representative. The authors should give a table listing, per composition, the number of samples, cleaves, fields of view and tips, and the area fraction above an anisotropy threshold. They should also show the analysis of concern 1 for at least two independent regions or cleaves per composition, including at least one Sn composition from a second crystal or cleave.

10. The actual compositions of the measured crystals are not determined, and the nominal and quoted values do not agree. For Ti, the Methods give a nominal load of Cs20V12Ti3.5Sb120, which corresponds to Ti/(V+Ti)×3 ≈ 0.68 per formula unit, yet the sample is labelled y = 0.2. For Sn, the nominal Sb:Sn ratios (80:40 and 60:60) lie far above the quoted x ≈ 0.33 and 0.68. Methods give growth only for x ≈ 0.33 and 0.68 and no recipe for the x ≈ 0.1 crystal. Flux growth makes such differences plausible, but the doping axis of the whole phase diagram then rests on unstated measurements, and composition inhomogeneity or dopant clustering is itself an alternative source of domains. The authors should give EDS/WDS or diffraction-refined compositions for the crystals actually measured, with their spread and uncertainty. Ideally they should also give the dopant density counted in STM topographs, and transport or magnetization characterization of the measured batches showing the absence of the CDW anomaly and the Tc.

11. The DFT comparison is semi-quantitative and partly mismatched to the data. The constant-energy contours and autocorrelations are computed for x ≈ 0.25 (p. 5), while the data are at x = 0.33 and 0.68. They use linear interpolation of Wannier hoppings between CsV3Sb5 and CsV3Sb4Sn. This is effectively a virtual-crystal approach, with no disorder broadening and no stated treatment of rigid band shifts. Only k_z = 0 contours are shown, although STM integrates over k_z. The p. 5 statement that the ½Q_Bragg vector connects "exactly" the M pockets depends on where the chemical potential sits, and Sn hole doping changes that. Only −100 and −500 meV are calculated, while the measured window (red ellipse, Fig. 2d) spans roughly −300 to +100 meV. The Methods state both that "all first-principles calculations were performed on the pristine phase" and that a CsV3Sb4Sn Hamiltonian was constructed. The authors should do four things. (1) Clarify which structures were relaxed and with what settings, and justify the 300 eV cutoff or give a convergence check. (2) Compute at the measured compositions, stating how the Fermi level and the DFT–experiment energy alignment were set. (3) Show how robust the M-pocket nesting is to k_z and to an energy offset. (4) Plot the predicted ½Q-feature intensity against energy alongside the measured profile in Fig. 2d. The autocorrelation is C6 symmetric by construction. It explains the wave vector, not the anisotropy, and the text should say so explicitly.

12. The mirror-symmetry statement is not substantiated. The text states that each domain "breaks two in-plane mirror symmetries but appears to generally preserve" the third (p. 5, pp. 5–6, Discussion). This is judged by eye from small boxes whose FTs span only a few pixels (Fig. 3b,d). The Discussion builds on it: it proposes a possible further transition to a chiral state at low temperature (p. 7) and compares the result with CsTi3Bi5 (refs. 42, 43). The authors should give a quantitative mirror test for each domain type. Options are the intensities of the FT peaks that are mirror-related across the preserved axis, or the correlation between each domain image and its mirror image about each of the three axes. Uncertainties should come from the null model of concern 1. Otherwise the claim should be dropped.

13. Tip artifacts are only partly excluded. Seeing domains of different orientation with one tip (p. 7) rules out a uniform tip anisotropy. It does not rule out a tip that enhances whatever local anisotropy is already present. The authors themselves invoke tip anisotropy (ref. 41) as a concern for the chirality question. Imaging the same area with a second tip, or after a controlled tip change, would strengthen the claim for both the Sn and the Ti data.

14. The domain size and its dependence on doping are not reported, although the role of disorder is central. On p. 6 the authors propose that the short range "likely arises due to increased disorder caused by the dopants". If so, the domain size should shrink from x = 0.33 to 0.68, or scale with the mean dopant spacing. The authors should quote a characteristic domain size or correlation length (from the autocorrelation of concern 1), so that "nanoscale" and "short-range" become concrete. They should compare it with the average dopant spacing and report how it and the anisotropy amplitude change with x and between Sn and Ti doping. If neither changes systematically, that would favour disorder-generated anisotropy over intrinsic nematicity broken up by disorder. This analysis would also help separate the null hypothesis of concern 1 from the claimed state.
