A. Must address

1. **It is not shown that the "RDS" upturn is diffuse magnon scattering rather than a pump-induced change in the shape of the magnetic Bragg peak.** All tr-RDS points lie 0.01–0.08 r.l.u. from the (1/2 0 -1/2) peak, whose top saturates the detector (Fig. 2C caption; hatched region in Fig. 3). The main argument against a lineshape change is that "the (1/2 0 -1/2) RXD peak width remains nearly constant while the tail intensity rises" (p. 4, Fig. 2C). The Fig. 2C caption itself says "the top of the diffraction peak is missing due to saturation of the detector", so neither the width, the position nor the integrated intensity can be judged from that figure, and no unsaturated profile versus delay is shown. Several lineshape effects would raise the tail intensity with a delayed, q-dependent rise: transient thermal or magnetoelastic strain, a change of magnetic correlation length or mosaic, changes in domain structure, or a small shift of the magnetic propagation vector. Strain is a strong candidate. The X-ray probe depth is ~18 nm and the pump penetration depth ~450 nm, so an acoustic strain front crosses the probed layer in a few picoseconds, the timescale of the upturn rise (τ_RDS up to ~4 ps, Fig. 3F). An inhomogeneous strain gradient broadens the peak symmetrically in ±q, which would reproduce both the symmetric amplitude profile (Fig. 3F) and a rise that is faster far from q_RXD than close to it. Because the surface normal (1 0 -1) is close to q_RXD, a strain-induced shift matters most along the [h 0 h]-type path; that path was measured on the negative-q side only (Fig. S9A), so the symmetry test is unavailable exactly where it matters, and Fig. S9 shows the largest upturns there, growing almost linearly up to 4 ps. The resonance (Fig. 2E) and π > σ dependence (Fig. 2G) show the signal is magnetic, not that it is diffuse rather than a magnetic Bragg tail. The authors should provide attenuated, unsaturated θ–2θ and rocking profiles, or 2D maps with the 0.5M Jungfrau already used for Fig. S7, at 3 mJ/cm² (not only at high fluence) at several delays (e.g. 0, 1, 4, 20, 100 ps, 1 ns), with fitted peak position, width and integrated intensity versus delay; test whether the tail increase can be reproduced by any combination of broadening and shift, and show that the upturn survives after these are fitted and subtracted; measure both signs of q along [h 0 h]; and compare the measured q-profile of the upturn with the profile Eqs. 1–2 predict for a magnon population (e.g. the weighting by A_µ(q)/ω(q)). Without these, the central identification of the signal as magnon scattering is not secure.

2. **The thermal versus non-thermal interpretation lacks an equilibrium temperature calibration, and the probed layer may sit at T_N1.** The sample is at 190 K and the authors' own estimate puts the transient heating at up to ~20 K (p. S7). The 18 nm probed surface layer is where the absorbed energy density is highest, so it sits at or within a few kelvin of T_N1 (213 K in the text, 214.5 K in Fig. S3). Near T_N, equilibrium critical magnetic diffuse scattering at small |q| grows steeply with temperature, and the equilibrium RXD intensity changes steeply too. A delayed rise peaked at small |q| with a q-dependent rise time is what one would expect if the probed layer were simply heating towards T_N1, and heating is also relevant to the ns-scale RDS signal in Fig. 4D. The RXD level at 12 ns (~-10%, Fig. 4D), after the energy should have passed to the lattice, likewise needs to be compared with the equilibrium value at the estimated lattice temperature, since thermal recovery of order in a layer that stays hot for ≫10 ns is an alternative recovery channel. The authors should measure the static (unpumped) RDS intensity at the q points of Fig. 3A and the (1/2 0 -1/2) RXD intensity on this crystal versus temperature from ~150 K through T_N1 and T_N2 (~240 K); use them to convert the pumped 4–100 ps plateau and the 12 ns values into effective temperatures; test whether the pumped RDS and RXD match a single equilibrium temperature (thermal magnons) or not (non-thermal distribution); redo the heating estimate for the 18 nm surface layer rather than the average; and add a temperature null control at the same detuned momentum just above T_N2 and well below T_N1. This is the central test of the "non-thermal → quasi-thermal" claim; without it, the ps dynamics could equally be read as fast local heating plus critical fluctuations. The calibration also bears on the proximity to the ICM phase (concern 11) and on which magnetic state the main data probe.

3. **The RXD/RDS decomposition and the extracted τ_RDS(q), the key observable for quasi-thermalization, are not robust as presented.** Eq. S3, A·exp(-t/τ), decays to zero and cannot describe a step-like drop or a rising plateau (Figs. S6C/F); the function actually fitted, including (1 - e^(-t/τ)) terms, convolution with the ~60 fs instrument response and the time-zero handling, must be stated. The fits use a 4 ps window (Fig. 3A, Fig. S9A), yet τ_RDS reaches ~4 ps near q = 0.01 (Fig. 3F) and ~13 ps with A_RDS ≈ 18 along [h 0 h] (Fig. S9E); where τ approaches or exceeds the window, amplitude and time constant are strongly correlated and the fit error bars likely understate the uncertainty. These near-Γ points carry the claim that 1/τ_RDS "is proportional to momentum" (Fig. 3I), yet the lines in Fig. 3I are "guide to the eyes". Fig. S8 shows the upturn is non-monotonic (maximum ~5 ps, plateau after ~10 ps), so a single exponential may bias τ. The decomposition also assumes the magnon contribution is zero just after excitation, whereas the authors' own model (Fig. S14B, Fig. 3J) creates magnons across the zone instantaneously, which would add a prompt positive RDS step that the fit would absorb into the negative "RXD" term. The largest amplitudes and longest τ_RDS occur in the APD channels nearest the saturated region (Fig. 3F, Fig. S9E), and single-shot XFEL pulses can drive avalanche photodiodes non-linear. Finally, the APD momentum resolution (solid angle in r.l.u.) is not stated: Fig. S5 implies Bragg widths of ~10⁻³ r.l.u. along b*, yet Fig. 2C shows substantial intensity at 0.025–0.075 r.l.u., which could be detector acceptance, equilibrium critical or thermal diffuse scattering at 0.9 T_N, or static disorder. The authors should give the actual fit function; report A–τ covariances; use longer delay scans (as in Fig. S8) at several small-|q| points; test whether τ(q) survives a fit that allows a prompt RDS step and a global fit with the RXD time constant shared across q (Fig. 3D shows it to be q-independent); provide an attenuator-series linearity calibration at the near-Bragg q points; state the APD angular acceptance in r.l.u. and fold it into the analysis; and either fit a linear model to Fig. 3I with uncertainties or soften the "proportional to momentum" wording.

4. **The simulation–experiment comparison behind "quasi-thermalization via magnon-magnon scattering" is qualitative, and the simulation is not set up for the experimental conditions.** The evidence is a by-eye comparison of Fig. 3E (data, 0–4 ps, arbitrary units) with Fig. 3G (simulation, 0–6 ps, colour scale 0–800); simulated rise times are never extracted with the same two-exponential procedure, overlaid on Fig. 3F/3I, or compared with measured traces on a common scale with one overall scale factor. The excitation amplitude B₀Δn(t) was chosen "to reproduce the experimentally observed reduction of the AFM order by approximately 50%" (p. S13), but Fig. 2D shows a ~28% drop in RXD intensity at 3 mJ/cm², i.e. roughly a 15% reduction of the sublattice moment if I ∝ M²; if the simulation is overdriven by ~3×, the magnon density and the quartic scattering rate are overestimated. The QBE grid (32 × 48 × 32, spacing ~0.02–0.03 r.l.u.) is coarser than the experimental q points (0.010–0.025 r.l.u.), and the δ-function is broadened with a 3.0 meV kernel (p. S15), comparable to the acoustic magnon energies at these q (gap ~1.3 meV, Fig. S13C), so the simulated momentum dependence near Γ (Figs. 3G/H, S16) is set largely by interpolation and broadening. The model has two degenerate magnon branches (p. S13), while the main text assigns the signal to the acoustic branch (1.3 meV gap) as distinct from the optical one (23.2 meV), and the Conclusions and Fig. S8 invoke optical-magnon decay; the model cannot test either. Because quartic magnon-magnon scattering conserves magnon number, the "quasi-thermalized" state should be a Bose–Einstein distribution with non-zero chemical potential, the physical origin of the claimed bottleneck, and this should be shown explicitly. Lastly, the Fig. 3 caption says J–N are "derived from tr-RDS data using Eq. 2", while the main text (p. 7, with Fig. S15) presents them as simulations; their colour scale matches simulated panel H, and absolute occupations (up to 13) cannot be obtained from intensities in arbitrary units without an inversion that is not described. The authors should rerun the QBE with the excitation calibrated to the measured RXD drop; show convergence with grid density and kernel width; fit simulated traces exactly as the data are fitted and overlay τ_RDS(q) and A_RDS(q) from theory and experiment along both b* and [h 0 h]; state which gap the model has and how the optical branch enters; show the chemical potential; and state clearly what Figs. 3J–N show.

5. **The anomalous magnon-phonon recovery mechanism rests on an assumed coupling constant, so agreement with the ~7 ns recovery is not a prediction.** On p. S17 both normal and anomalous couplings are fixed at c^x = 1 eV/Å without justification. Rates scale as |c^x|², so the absolute rates in Fig. 4E/F and Fig. S17 (~0.2 ns⁻¹) do not independently test the 7 ns recovery; only the relative phase-space structure is predicted. The "effective magnon-phonon coupling ~59 µeV" (p. 9) appears to be back-derived from the measured time constant and its derivation is not given anywhere, so the claim that the coupling is small because of a dispersion-mismatch phase-space restriction rather than a small microscopic matrix element is not demonstrated. Comparing it with the 6.2 meV spin-phonon coupling from Raman (ref. 44) mixes differently defined quantities. The authors should fix the anomalous vertex independently, e.g. ∇J from frozen-phonon DFT+U displacements of the lowest optical modes (which they already run), the measured spin-phonon shifts, or magnetostriction; show that this vertex inserted into Eq. S27 gives ns rates; state the formula and inputs behind 59 µeV; show how sensitive "anomalous scattering dominates recovery" is to the assumed equality of c^anomalous and c^normal; and compute the full time-evolved recovery curve from the QBE, not only the initial rate Γ(q) = S/f at t = 0, for comparison with the RXD and RDS traces of Fig. 4D.

6. **Long-lived photocarriers are not excluded, either as a picosecond magnon-scattering channel or as the cause of the persistent demagnetization and its ns recovery.** The paper excludes magnon-electron scattering, and assigns the ns recovery to magnons, on the grounds that doublons decay within ~200 fs and "electrons are already thermalized within femtosecond timescales (30, 31)" (p. 6). Those references concern other cuprates, and no measurement on CuO is shown. In a 1.4–1.5 eV charge-transfer insulator, photo-holes and electrons can localize (polarons, traps) and survive for ps to ns; through the same spin-charge coupling invoked in Eq. S5 they could redistribute magnons, keep the order suppressed and set the 7 ns recovery through recombination. The authors themselves invoke defect-related electronic thermal conductivity in this very sample (p. S10). They should measure the photocarrier lifetime on the same crystal at the same fluence, e.g. transient optical reflectivity/absorption or tr-XAS at the Cu L3 (edge shift or bleach, measurable in the SwissFEL setup) or O K edge across ps–ns, and show that carriers have decayed well before the RXD recovers. Without this, the recovery via anomalous magnon-phonon scattering is not uniquely supported.

7. **The polarization test does not distinguish transverse magnon fluctuations from a longitudinal (Bragg-like) magnetic tail.** The text takes the π/σ ratio of the upturn (Fig. 2G) being "similar to the magnetic RXD profile" (Fig. S5B) as proof of magnetic origin. The measured ratio is ~6 at 4 ps, close to the static RXD ratio of ~3.1/0.5 in Fig. S5B. Magnons are fluctuations transverse to the b-axis moments, so Eq. 1 generally predicts a σ/π ratio different from the elastic Bragg ratio; as it stands, agreement with the RXD ratio arguably favours a Bragg-tail origin. Using the geometric tensor F (Eq. S37), the authors should compute the expected σ and π RDS intensities from transverse S^xx and S^zz correlations and the Bragg ratio from S^yy, and compare both with Figs. 2G and S5B.

8. **The energy dependence of the RDS upturn follows the absorption maximum rather than the magnetic RXD resonance (Fig. 2E).** The upturn peaks at ~931.7 eV, coinciding with the XAS maximum, whereas the RXD peaks near 930 eV and dips at 933 eV from self-absorption. Magnon-related resonant scattering is governed by the same dipole matrix elements as the magnetic Bragg intensity, so the difference needs explaining, e.g. with self-absorption corrections for each geometry or a calculated resonance profile for the inelastic channel. A pump-induced change of absorption, fluorescence or self-absorption, which would scale with the local tail intensity, should also be ruled out, e.g. with time-resolved XAS in fluorescence yield at the same fluence.

9. **The tr-NRDS evidence for phonon creation by magnon annihilation (Fig. 4D top, Fig. S10) is weak as a control and its kinetics do not obviously match the proposed energy flow.** The NRDS data come from a different facility and experiment: a ps Ti:sapphire pump at 1 kHz instead of 35 fs at 100 Hz (with possible average heating), ~100 ps X-ray pulses, pump incidence ~88°, a ~1.5 µm probe depth versus ~500 nm pump depth and 18 nm for RXD/RDS, and no stated fluence definition; the "similar timescale" therefore compares different depth-averaged volumes and excitation densities. If phonons were created at the ~7 ns magnon annihilation rate and dissipated over ~0.7 µs (Fig. S11), they should accumulate monotonically over ~10 ns, but the AFM enhancement peaks at ~1–2 ns and then decays. The AFM/PM comparison is at 190 K versus 300 K, where phonon populations, heat capacity, Debye–Waller factors, absorption, thermal expansion and conductivity all differ. NRDS within 0.1 r.l.u. of (0 0 -2) is dominated by low-energy acoustic phonons (∝ n/ω²), whereas the anomalous process emits the lowest optical phonons, so the observable is at best indirect. The ROI in Fig. S10C was chosen by hand to avoid a Bragg-peak shift visible at 0.15 and 0.6 ns, panels 2 and 4 of Fig. S10 show dipolar shift-like patterns, and AFM-specific magnetostriction from the recovering order could broaden (0 0 -2) inhomogeneously and raise intensity in the annular ROI without a peak shift. The 2 ns enhancement rests on a handful of delays with large error bars, and the ~160 ps feature, read as the "phonon quasi-thermalization timescale", is at the 100 ps resolution limit. The authors should add a PM reference just above T_N2 (~235–250 K) and an AF2 reference; show radial and azimuthal (transverse) profiles and the fitted Bragg-peak position versus delay in both phases, and the sensitivity of the trace to the ROI choice; quantify deposited energy at each temperature and steady-state heating at 1 kHz; give fit uncertainties for the 2 ns and 160 ps constants; and fit RXD, RDS and NRDS jointly with a coupled rate-equation model including magnon → phonon transfer and phonon redistribution, with an energy-budget check.
