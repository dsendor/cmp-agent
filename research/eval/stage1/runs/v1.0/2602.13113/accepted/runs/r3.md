Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors present time-resolved resonant diffuse scattering (tr-RDS) at the Cu L3 edge as a momentum-resolved probe of low-energy magnon populations after photoexcitation. They use it, together with tr-RXD, tr-NRDS and quantum Boltzmann simulations, to build a three-stage picture of energy flow in CuO. First, femtosecond spin disorder from doublon creation. Second, picosecond magnon quasi-thermalization towards the spin gap through magnon-magnon scattering. Third, nanosecond recovery limited by anomalous magnon-phonon scattering. If the diffuse signal can be shown to be magnetic and the timescales intrinsic, this is the first time-resolved, momentum-resolved view of low-energy magnon redistribution in an antiferromagnet, and it would interest the ultrafast-magnetism and XFEL communities. The method is the main novelty. The physical picture depends on separating a weak diffuse signal from a strongly varying Bragg tail, and on telling an intrinsic magnon-phonon bottleneck apart from ordinary heat transport.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The tr-RDS technique itself is new. The RXD and RDS channels are recorded simultaneously at nearby momenta, so pump-probe conditions match.
- Several checks support a magnetic origin of the upturn: resonant enhancement at the Cu L3 edge (Fig. 2e), π versus σ polarization dependence (Fig. 2g), a fluence threshold that tracks the RXD suppression (Fig. 2f), and a Bragg peak width that does not change (Fig. S4).
- The time constant depends strongly on momentum (Fig. 3f, i), and the RXD time constant does not (Fig. 3d). This is a qualitative signature of redistribution in reciprocal space and is hard to get from a purely global effect.
- The paper combines three X-ray probes with a microscopic quantum-kinetic model that goes beyond multi-temperature phenomenology. The tr-NRDS contrast between the AFM and PM phases (Fig. 4d) adds evidence that phonons are created during magnon annihilation.
- The data are deposited in a public repository.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. Heat diffusion out of the excited layer is not ruled out as the cause of the ~7 ns recovery. The paper attributes the ~7 ns recovery of RXD and RDS (Fig. 4d, p. 6) to an intrinsically inefficient anomalous magnon-phonon process, and from this time constant it extracts an effective coupling of ~59 µeV (p. 7). This is the central "dispersion mismatch creates bottlenecks" claim. At 400 nm the pump penetration depth is ~45 nm and the soft X-ray probe depth is ~18 nm (Methods). Heat leaving a ~45 nm layer into the cold bulk takes roughly d²/D. For a thermal diffusivity of order 10⁻⁶ m²/s that is a few nanoseconds, the same order as the observed recovery. If the magnon and lattice systems are already near local equilibrium by ~1 ns, depth diffusion alone would restore the RXD intensity on this timescale whatever the magnon-phonon coupling is.
   The authors should do the following:
   - Give an explicit estimate of the thermal diffusion time for the actual excitation profile, using literature values of CuO thermal conductivity at 190 K.
   - Show that recovery from heat diffusion would be clearly faster or slower than 7 ns, or model the depth-resolved recovery including both diffusion and the QBE magnon-phonon channel.
   - Ideally, test how the recovery time depends on fluence or on probe depth, for example by changing the X-ray incidence angle or energy across the edge. Diffusion and an intrinsic bottleneck predict different scalings.
   - Without this, neither the 59 µeV coupling nor the "design principle" for lifetimes follows.

2. The separation of the RDS signal from the Bragg tail relies on fits, and alternative origins of the upturn are not fully excluded. Everything in Fig. 3 rests on splitting each time trace into an RXD-reduction term and an RDS-upturn term with two exponentials (Fig. 3a, c–f; Supplementary Note 2). This is hardest exactly where the paper's key result sits. The q points closest to q_RXD (|q| = 0.010–0.020 r.l.u., only 2–4 times the stated 5×10⁻³ r.l.u. resolution) have the largest tail contribution, and they produce the slowest τ_RDS and the near-zero rate in Fig. 3i. Two non-magnetic mechanisms could also make the detuned intensity rise on picosecond timescales:
   - a transient change in the Bragg profile (peak position shift from lattice expansion or strain, or a change in line shape) rather than in width;
   - a transient change in X-ray absorption, which would alter the self-absorption that the authors themselves invoke to explain Fig. 2e.

   The authors should do the following:
   - Show full transient q-scans through the Bragg peak at several delays, giving peak position as well as width (extend Fig. S4).
   - Show transient XAS, or at least the RXD and RDS energy dependence at more than one delay.
   - Report a non-resonant or below-edge control taken under the same geometry.
   - Give the covariance between the fitted A and τ parameters at small |q|. If τ_RDS at |q| ≤ 0.02 is set mainly by how the tail is subtracted, the linear 1/τ ∝ |q| law in Fig. 3i is not established.

3. A transient incommensurate (AF2) phase or a heating-driven state is not convincingly excluded at the working temperature and fluence. The sample is held at 190 K, only ~23 K below T_N1 = 213 K, and is excited at 3 mJ/cm² into a ~45 nm layer. A photo-induced collinear-to-spiral transition in CuO has been reported (ref. 23). The AF2 propagation vector (0.506, 0, −0.483) lies within a few hundredths of r.l.u. of the commensurate peak, the same scale as the q-range in Fig. 3. The RDS detector sits at (0.49, 0.01, −0.51), and Fig. 4d uses (0.47, 0, −0.53), which also deviates in h and l. Transient incommensurate satellites or short-range AF2 correlations near these positions would give a growing, resonant, magnetic, π-enhanced diffuse signal, consistent with Figs. 2e–g, without any magnon redistribution.
   The exclusion is deferred to Supplementary Note 4. The main text should state the following:
   - the estimated peak transient temperature at 3 mJ/cm² relative to T_N1 and T_N2 (Supplementary Note 3);
   - why the measured q points and their time dependence rule out AF2 satellites;
   - for comparison, the RDS response at a lower fluence where the estimated temperature stays well below T_N1.

   A temperature series of the tr-RDS signal at fixed fluence would be the most direct test.

B. Should address

4. The comparison between the simulation and the data in Fig. 3 is qualitative, and it is unclear which parameters were fitted. Fig. 3e (data, a.u.) and Fig. 3g (simulation, 0–800) are compared by eye. The lines in Fig. 3i are described as guides to the eye rather than simulated rates. The excitation parameters "were chosen to be consistent with the experiment" (Methods), and J_AFM, J_FM, J_out and D are described as "representative" values. The authors should:
   - overlay the simulated τ_RDS(q) and A_RDS(q) on the measured values in Fig. 3f, i;
   - state which model parameters (doublon density, ramp time, exchange constants, anisotropy) were fixed independently and which were tuned to the tr-RDS data;
   - show how sensitive the predicted thermalization time and the |q|-linear rate are to the anisotropy gap, which sets the accumulation point.

5. The labelling of Fig. 3j–n is ambiguous, and the inverse problem is not discussed. The text says the simulations show the magnon distribution (Figs. 3j–n). The caption says panels j–n are "derived from tr-RDS data using Eq. (2)". The energy-integrated S(q,t) sums over both magnon branches and includes prefactors A_µ(q), Ā_µ(q). Getting f_µ(q,t) back from one scalar per q is not unique without assumptions, for example acoustic-branch dominance, Bose occupation, or neglect of longitudinal and two-magnon terms. The authors should state clearly whether j–n are simulated or inverted from data. If inverted, they should give the assumptions and the uncertainties.

6. The claim that the magnons are initially distributed "throughout reciprocal space" (abstract, Discussion point 1) is not measured. tr-RDS samples only |q| < 0.08 r.l.u. along b* and [h0h], close to one magnetic zone centre. The broad initial distribution across the Brillouin zone comes from the simulation (Fig. S8). The abstract should say that the zone-wide distribution is inferred from the model, or show supporting data at larger momentum transfer.

7. The tr-RDS and tr-NRDS excitation conditions differ, which weakens the combined timeline in Fig. 4d. The tr-NRDS experiment uses:
   - a ~500 nm pump penetration depth, against ~45 nm for tr-RDS;
   - a ~1.5 µm probe depth, against ~18 nm;
   - picosecond rather than 35 fs pump pulses;
   - a 1 kHz rather than 100 Hz repetition rate.

   The phonon-creation timescale (~2 ns peak) and the AFM/PM contrast therefore come from a different excited volume, absorbed energy density and possibly a different steady-state temperature. In addition, the PM reference is presumably taken at a much higher base temperature, where the equilibrium phonon population and the diffuse background differ. The authors should:
   - compare the absorbed energy densities in the two experiments;
   - estimate the average heating at 1 kHz;
   - give the PM measurement temperature and explain why the AFM/PM difference is not a temperature-dependent background effect;
   - discuss whether the ~2 ns NRDS rise can be compared quantitatively with the ~7 ns RDS/RXD recovery.

8. Results come from one crystal at one temperature. All tr-RDS data come from a single CuO crystal at 190 K, mostly at one fluence (3 mJ/cm²). The paper gives no evidence that the momentum-dependent τ_RDS reproduces on a second sample, a second beamtime, or another base temperature. A repeat of Fig. 3f, i at a second temperature or fluence would also test the predicted temperature dependence of the bottlenecks. The authors should state how many samples and beamtimes contributed.

9. The generality claims go beyond the evidence. The abstract and Discussion state that the framework "generalizes across antiferromagnetic insulators" and extends to ferromagnets, and that materials with overlapping dispersions "will exhibit faster recovery". These claims rest on one material. The authors should either present them as hypotheses or show calculations for at least one other system with different magnon-phonon overlap.

10. The polarization control is only partly quantified. In Fig. 2g, the σ-polarized trace also shows a positive late-time signal of about 15–20% of the π signal. The authors should compare the measured π/σ ratio quantitatively with the ratio predicted by Eq. (1) for the CuO spin direction and scattering geometry. A residual σ signal larger than predicted would point to a non-magnetic contribution.

11. The self-similarity analysis compares data with an inappropriate model. The power-law exponent 2.28 ± 0.08 is compared with 2.33 from a theory of two-dimensional Heisenberg antiferromagnets (ref. 43), but CuO has significant interlayer coupling and a 1.3 meV gap. The authors should either justify why the 2D exponent applies, or present the agreement as suggestive and give the q and t windows used, since the fitted exponent may depend on them.

C. Minor

12. The fluence dependence (Fig. 2f) is plotted against log fluence, but the threshold value and the fluence of maximal upturn are not stated in the text. The authors should quote them and relate them to the estimated transient temperature.

13. Discussion point (4) says phonon recovery on microsecond timescales "may be dominated by electron-phonon scattering", which is unexpected for an insulator whose excited carriers relax within ~200 fs. Please clarify this mechanism or remove the claim.

14. The sign convention for the coupling strengths ("−59 µeV", "−6.2 meV") is unclear. If "−" is meant as "~", it should be written as such.

15. Fig. 3 caption: describe the hatched saturation region in Fig. 3a–f in the same place for every panel. Also state the q range excluded because of saturation and why the sign of the "small error in the definition of the origin" produces the asymmetry seen in panel a.

16. Methods: give the actual model parameters used for the magnon-phonon QBE (phonon branches, coupling vertex) in the main Methods, not only by reference to Supplementary Note 10, because the 59 µeV coupling is a headline number.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a depth-resolved thermal-transport model (excitation profile × probe depth) to the analysis of Fig. 4d. Show explicitly that heat diffusion alone cannot reproduce the 7 ns recovery, or include it and refit the magnon-phonon coupling.
2. Present full transient Bragg-profile scans (position, width, integrated intensity) and transient XAS at representative delays. These would show directly that the picosecond upturn is diffuse magnetic scattering and not a Bragg-profile or absorption artifact.
3. Measure tr-RDS at one or two additional base temperatures or fluences, including one well below T_N1. This tests reproducibility, rules out transient AF2 correlations, and tests the predicted temperature dependence of the quasi-thermalization and recovery rates.
4. Put measured and simulated τ_RDS(q) and A_RDS(q) on the same axes, with a parameter table that separates fixed parameters from fitted ones. This turns the qualitative agreement into a quantitative test of the quantum-kinetic model.
