Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors parametrically pump a perpendicularly magnetized 2.1-µm YIG film through a 100-µm microstrip and detect the k ≈ 0 precession inductively, using an IQ mixer that is phase-locked to the pump. This gives single-shot amplitude and phase traces of the magnon condensate. Their main results are: (i) across 1,000 shots, the condensate phase at a fixed time is uniformly distributed while the pump phase is fixed, and (ii) a windowed autocorrelation "coherence value" rises from noise to about 0.8 as the condensate forms, with a threshold near 21 dBm. Single-shot, phase-resolved electrical access to a magnon condensate is useful, and it opens a direct test of a basic property of condensates. The interpretation as "spontaneous symmetry breaking" and as "proof of a true BEC" still needs the controls and analyses listed below.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- A simple, all-electronic, single-shot method that resolves the phase of a magnon condensate against a reference locked to the pump. It needs no ensemble averaging and avoids the limitations of BLS.
- The phase distribution is compared directly with the pump-phase distribution (grey sectors in the Fig. 2a insets) over 1,000 realizations.
- The time-resolved spectrogram (Fig. 3a) agrees with the IQ-derived spectrogram (Extended Data Fig. 2), which cross-checks the detection chain.
- There is a pump-power series with a visible threshold (Fig. 4), and the data are deposited on Zenodo.
- The out-of-plane geometry puts the condensate at k ≈ 0, which makes inductive detection possible. This is a sensible design choice.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. **A uniform phase distribution at a fixed lab time does not by itself show that the phase is chosen at random when the condensate forms.**
   - **Where.** Fig. 2a insets, Fig. 2b, and the text on p. 1433 ("the uniform phase distribution must be an intrinsic property of the BEC").
   - **Why the test is weak.** The phase is measured against a 3.2-GHz LO at fixed times t = 0.10, 0.90 and 1.25 µs. By then the condensate frequency has chirped from about 3.42 to 3.32 GHz (Fig. 3a; Extended Data Fig. 2a), and the chirp comes from the nonlinear shift set by the total magnon number. The phase at time t is therefore φ(t) = φ0 + 2π∫f(t′)dt′. Suppose the onset time varies from shot to shot by about 10 ns, or the magnon number (and hence f) varies by a few MHz over the roughly 0.5 µs since formation. Either would spread φ(1.25 µs) over many radians even if φ0 were fixed by the pump. Such variation is plausible near threshold, given the large ensemble spread in amplitude in Fig. 4b.
   - **Consequence.** The headline test does not distinguish "random phase set at formation" from "deterministic phase scrambled by shot-to-shot jitter in onset time or frequency trajectory". The second would not be spontaneous U(1) symmetry breaking.
   - **What to do.**
     - Show the phase distribution as close to the onset as possible, for example at the first time the coherence value clearly exceeds baseline (around 0.6 to 0.8 µs in Fig. 3b), before much chirp phase has accumulated.
     - Show the distribution after removing each shot's own accumulated chirp phase. For each shot, extract f(t) from the IQ data, subtract 2π∫f dt from a common reference time, and give the distribution of the residual phase.
     - Plot the phase against each shot's onset time or integrated amplitude. If the phase is set by jitter, it will correlate with these quantities.
     - Quantify uniformity with a stated test (for example a Rayleigh test, or the mean resultant length |⟨e^{iφ}⟩| with its 1,000-shot null expectation of about 0.03). Report the same statistic for the pump phase. The polar histograms alone are not enough.

2. **There is no positive control showing that the detection chain records a reproducible phase for a signal at the condensate frequency.**
   - **Unlocked oscilloscope.** Extended Data Fig. 1 states that the oscilloscope "was excluded [from the 10-MHz reference] due to technical difficulties, but phase stability of the setup as a whole was confirmed in preliminary measurements". Those measurements are not shown.
   - **Pump-phase reference is unexplained.** The pump phase (grey sectors, Fig. 2a) is the reference against which the BEC phase is called random. However, the 7.8-GHz pump is removed by the 5-GHz and <4-GHz low-pass filters (Methods; Extended Data Fig. 1), and it lies outside the 3.2-GHz IQ band. The paper does not say how the pump phase was measured.
   - **Text and figure disagree on the reference's spread.** The text says externally induced factors are stable "within a few degrees of phase shift". The grey sectors in the insets look several tens of degrees wide.
   - **Consequence.** If the phase reference itself jitters by tens of degrees or more, or trigger jitter on the unlocked scope is a sizeable fraction of the roughly 120-MHz IF period (about 8 ns), the uniformity claim loses its footing.
   - **What to do.**
     - Inject a weak CW or pulsed tone, derived from the locked synthesizers, at 3.32 GHz through the same antenna and receive chain, with the same pulse timing. Show that its phase histogram over 1,000 shots is sharply peaked, and give its width.
     - Ideally, also seed the k = 0 mode with a weak phase-locked tone during pumping, at several seed powers. If the condensate phase locks to the seed, giving a non-uniform histogram whose width depends on seed power, that would show both that the apparatus can see phase locking and that the unseeded phase is genuinely free.
     - Explain how the grey "pump phase" sectors were obtained and give their width numerically.

3. **The data do not establish that the coherent k ≈ 0 state is a thermalized Bose–Einstein condensate rather than another noise-seeded coherent instability, and a random phase alone does not single out a BEC.**
   - **Where.** Title, abstract, and the Discussion ("which has long been required as a necessary feature to prove the formation of a true BEC").
   - **What the paper shows.** Only the signal inside the antenna's narrow detection window near the band bottom.
   - **What the paper does not show.** It gives no evidence that the rest of the spectrum reaches a Bose–Einstein distribution with chemical potential at the band bottom. Nor does it distinguish condensation from a kinetic instability that transfers magnons directly to the bottom. The authors themselves describe the kinetic instability on p. 1432, and ref. 32 questions BEC claims in exactly this perpendicular-YIG geometry.
   - **Why it matters.** Kinetic instabilities, parametric self-oscillation and laser-like processes seeded by thermal noise also produce a coherent mode whose phase is random from shot to shot. Shot-to-shot phase randomness is necessary for a BEC but not sufficient. The claim of "confirming a central prediction of Bose–Einstein condensation theory" and evidence of "a true BEC" therefore rests on an identification the paper does not make.
   - **What to do.** Either:
     - add evidence of thermalization in this sample and geometry, such as wavevector- and frequency-resolved occupation from BLS showing an approximately Bose–Einstein distribution and μ reaching the band bottom, together with the expected scaling of threshold with pump power and duration; or
     - discuss the kinetic-instability alternative explicitly and narrow the claim to the spontaneous phase of the coherent band-bottom state.
   - **Prior evidence and the delay.** If earlier work by the authors (refs 27, 28) establishes thermalization under the same conditions, state the matching parameters (film, field, pump frequency and power). The roughly 0.6-µs delay before any signal appears (Fig. 3a) is itself informative. Comparing it with expected thermalization and kinetic-instability timescales would help.

B. Should address

4. **The "coherence value" mixes amplitude variation, frequency chirp, spurious tones and noise. It is not a clean measure of phase coherence.**
   - **The metric.** Methods define it as the integral of the normalized |autocorrelation| of the boxcar-windowed signal over a 100-ns window, divided by that of a pure tone.
   - **Chirp and amplitude modulation lower it without any loss of phase memory.** A perfectly phase-coherent signal whose frequency chirps by tens of MHz within 100 ns, or whose amplitude changes within the window, gives a value below 1. The authors in fact attribute the coherence dip at pump-off (Fig. 3b, Fig. 4a) to the nonlinear frequency shift, which is chirp, not decoherence.
   - **Pure noise gives a baseline of about 0.18,** not 0 as the Fig. 3b caption implies.
   - **Spurious tones can raise it.** Extended Data Fig. 2c shows IQ artifacts: "a constant band at 3.77 GHz and a shadow between 3.2 GHz and 3.3 GHz". A stationary spurious tone is itself coherent. The shadow overlaps the final condensate frequency (about 3.32 GHz).
   - **What to do.**
     - Calibrate the metric. Compute it for synthetic signals (pure tone plus recorded noise at the measured SNR; tone with the measured chirp; tone with the measured amplitude envelope) and for pump-off or no-pump traces processed identically.
     - Rescale or subtract the noise baseline.
     - Show that the spurious tones do not contribute: notch them out, or compute the metric on traces with the pump off.
     - Consider a complementary, standard measure, for example the within-shot phase-noise spectrum or linewidth after removing the chirp, or the first-order coherence g1(τ) of the demodulated, chirp-corrected signal.

5. **Two claims rest on the uncalibrated metric of concern 4: that the condensate "does not undergo dephasing" and that coherence persists after the signal is below the noise.**
   - **Where.** p. 1434: "the BEC is phase-stable until the signal vanishes … At t = 3.5 µs, the amplitude of the signal has vanished below the noise threshold, but a clear remanence in coherence can be observed."
   - **Problem with the first claim.** Fig. 4a shows the coherence decreasing steadily from about 1.8 µs onwards. Without a model of the expected coherence against SNR for a perfectly coherent tone, a fall caused by SNR cannot be separated from a fall caused by dephasing.
   - **Problem with the second claim.** At 3.5 µs the 25-dBm curve in Fig. 4a is within a few hundredths of the 0.18 baseline. A "clear remanence" needs a significance estimate against pump-off traces processed the same way, and the spurious tones of concern 4 must be ruled out.
   - **What to do.** Overlay the expected coherence-against-SNR curve for an ideal tone, computed from the measured amplitude decay, on Fig. 4a. Compare the condensate linewidth from the spectrum analyser (Fig. 3a) with the decay time of the amplitude. Either quantify these claims or remove them.

6. **The paper does not observe the incoherent stage it says it tracks.**
   - **Where.** The abstract says the measurement reveals "how an initially incoherent magnon gas thermalizes and undergoes a spontaneous transition".
   - **What the data show.** Before about 0.6 µs, the detected signal in Fig. 2 (t = 0.10 µs), Fig. 3a and Fig. 4b is indistinguishable from receiver noise. The text acknowledges that parametric magnons lie outside the detection band and that the antenna suppresses 18-µm magnons by about a factor of 18. The low coherence at early times is therefore the noise baseline, not a measurement of an incoherent magnon gas.
   - **What to do.** Show that the detector can see the parametric or thermalizing population at all, for example by widening the detection window or tuning the LO near f_p/2. Otherwise rephrase the claim as the emergence of a coherent band-bottom signal out of the noise floor.

7. **Only one sample, field, pump frequency and pulse length are shown, and shot independence and heating are not documented.**
   - **What is shown.** All data are from one 2.1-µm YIG film at 281 mT, f_p = 7.8 GHz, and 1-µs pulses.
   - **Missing for the threshold.** The roughly 21-dBm threshold (Fig. 4) is not tied to an expected parametric or condensation threshold.
   - **Missing for the statistics.** The repetition rate and the waiting time between the 1,000 shots are not given. Residual magnons or heating from 25-dBm (about 0.3 W) pulses could correlate consecutive shots, which bears directly on the phase statistics. Heating could also shift Ms and therefore the condensate frequency.
   - **What to do.**
     - Report the repetition rate and show that consecutive-shot phases are uncorrelated (for example an autocorrelation of φ_n against shot index).
     - Show the key phase-uniformity result in at least one other condition (another field, or another pump frequency or pulse length), and if possible in a second sample.
     - Check whether the result survives a change in duty cycle.

8. **The novelty statements are stronger than the literature supports.**
   - **Where.** The introduction says observing spontaneous coherence "has been impossible"; the Discussion says "no direct proof of randomness in the established condensate phase has yet been provided".
   - **Prior work to engage.** Ref. 36 (Demidov et al., "Observation of spontaneous coherence in Bose–Einstein condensate of magnons"). Earlier reports of microwave radiation emitted by magnon condensates and detected inductively. Ref. 11 (Snoke and Girvin) on phase-coherence onset.
   - **Other systems.** Shot-to-shot random-phase measurements exist in other condensates, for example interference of independently prepared atomic condensates (ref. 14) and polariton condensates.
   - **What to do.** State precisely what is new: single-shot, pump-referenced phase of a magnon condensate. Temper "first" and "proof" language accordingly.

9. **The explanation of the coherence oscillations is speculative and untested.**
   - **Where.** On p. 1434, the oscillations in the coherence plateau (period about 0.2 to 0.3 µs, Figs 3b and 4a) are attributed to magnons reflected from the sample edges.
   - **What to do.** Check this quantitatively: estimate the group velocity of the relevant magnons and the round-trip time to the edges of the 9.5 mm × 2.7 mm film, and compare with the measured period. Better still, test it experimentally by moving the antenna or using an absorber at the edges. Without this the sentence should be marked as a hypothesis.

C. Minor

10. **Figure 2.**
    - The time-axis tick labels repeat (for example "0.10, 0.10, 0.11, 0.12, 0.12", "1.25, 1.25, 1.26, …") because of rounding; use more digits or relative time.
    - State the bin count of the polar histograms, the normalization of "amplitude denotes the percentage of measurements in this cluster", and the number of shots in the grey (pump) histograms.
    - Explain why the phase-histogram time "at the start time of each panel" is representative.

11. **Fig. 3 caption.** It says 0 denotes "perfect white noise", but the plotted baseline is about 0.18 (explained only in Methods). Mention the baseline in the caption or rescale the axis.

12. **Typos.** Fig. 4 and Extended Data Fig. 2 captions: "standard derivation" should be "standard deviation". p. 1433: "Suppementary" should be "Supplementary".

13. **Methods: missing technical details.**
    - The ω used in the normalization N.
    - Oscilloscope sampling rate and bandwidth.
    - IF frequency and IQ amplitude/phase-imbalance calibration (imbalance can distort phase histograms into non-uniform shapes).
    - Trigger source and jitter.
    - Number of shots per pump power in Fig. 4.

14. **Switch inconsistency.** Methods name the switch "Miteq S138BDUI", while Extended Data Fig. 1 lists Switch 1 as "5138DU1". Extended Data Fig. 1 also says the scope-path switch was "realized by manually reconnecting the cables". Clarify which configuration was used for which dataset, and whether the cable changes altered the phase reference between datasets.

15. **Effective magnetization.** For a perpendicular film, f_k=0 ≈ γ(B − μ0Meff). At 281 mT and about 3.32 GHz this implies μ0Meff ≈ 0.16 T, somewhat below the room-temperature value for YIG (about 0.175 T). Quote Meff and the field calibration, and comment on whether pulse heating contributes to the frequency.

16. **Detection-band statement.** On p. 1432, "no coherent signal at the parametric frequency is observed" (also the dotted line at 3.9 GHz in Fig. 3a) should be qualified by the filter and antenna response at 3.9 GHz, so readers can tell absence from suppression.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a seeded-phase control: a weak phase-locked tone at the k = 0 frequency, injected during pumping at several seed powers. It would calibrate the phase detection and show directly the crossover from a pinned to a spontaneously chosen condensate phase, a much stronger demonstration of symmetry breaking than a single uniform histogram.
2. Replace or supplement the boxcar coherence value with chirp-corrected, within-shot phase analysis. Track φ(t) − 2π∫f dt per shot from onset to decay, show that it stays locked within a shot while being uniform across shots, and calibrate it against synthetic and pump-off data.
3. Add evidence that the state is thermalized, for example BLS occupation spectra under the same pumping conditions, or narrow the claim to the spontaneous phase of a coherent band-bottom magnon state.
4. Report phase statistics against pump power across the threshold (Fig. 4 powers). Show the mean resultant length and the onset-time distribution at each power, so the reader can see that phase randomness appears together with condensation and is not produced by onset-time jitter near threshold.
