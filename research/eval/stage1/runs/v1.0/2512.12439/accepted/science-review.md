A. Must address

1. Only one flake per condition, so the central comparison is not shown to be reproducible. Each of the five conditions (7L/Al2O3, 4L/Al2O3, 18L/Au, 6L/Au, 4L/Au) appears to rest on a single flake measured at a single spot. The headline contrast, 4L/Al2O3 (sharp, 280–287 K) against 4L/Au (broad, about 220 ± 40 K; Figs. 3, 4, 5), therefore rests on two flakes made by different processes. Thin-flake Tc values are known to scatter (the authors themselves cite 309 K for 8L on SiO2 and modest gate shifts in thin flakes), and the Al2O3 data show Tc depends steeply on thickness (7L at 320 K, 4L at 280 K), so a one-layer thickness error or a locally thinner region in the laser spot could move Tc by tens of kelvin. Flake-to-flake variation in strain, wrinkles, tears, defect density, ALD or evaporation conditions could likewise produce shifts and broadenings of tens of kelvin; Ta2NiSe5 Tc is strongly sensitive to uniaxial stress (Ref. 16), and these flakes are mechanically bonded to evaporated films. As it stands the substrate assignment cannot be separated from sample variation. The authors should state how many flakes of each kind were prepared and measured; measure at least two or three further flakes for each of 4L/Au and 4L/Al2O3, ideally from different exfoliation runs (and ideally 5–6L on each substrate); and show the spread of Tc onset and width in Fig. 5. If possible they should also measure a single flake spanning both substrates, for example using the transfer method to make a laterally patterned Au/Al2O3 substrate (or a partially Al2O3-covered region before Au deposition) and taking Raman line-scans across the boundary; this would remove sample-to-sample variation from the comparison entirely and would also demonstrate the junctions proposed in the Conclusions.

2. Strain and interface chemistry are not excluded as the cause of the lowered and broadened transition on Au. The paper attributes the whole difference to screening and charge transfer from a metal ("charge transfer and electrostatic screening ... are the likely culprit", pp. 14–15), but the two interfaces differ in much more than conductivity. On the Au side, Au is thermally evaporated at room temperature directly onto the Se-terminated surface, with possible intermixing, Au–Se bonding, diffusion of hot adatoms or local reaction; the authors themselves say the Au conforms to the surface ripples and binds more strongly (p. 4). On the Al2O3 side, 50–100 nm of Al2O3 is grown by ALD from TMA/H2O at 100–150 °C directly on Ta2NiSe5, a heated, chemically reactive process that could passivate, oxidize or otherwise modify the first layer, with Au further below. The two stacks also impose different interfacial strain and different thermal-expansion mismatch on cooling. Because Ta2NiSe5 Tc is very sensitive to uniaxial strain (Refs. 14–17), a strain that is inhomogeneous and temperature-dependent could alone lower and broaden Tc; a strain gradient decaying away from the substrate would produce the same vertical Tc gradient the authors attribute to screening. The sharp 4L/Al2O3 transition rules out bulk oxidation but not an Au-specific chemical or strain effect, and the substrate is evidently not neutral (4L/Al2O3 is already 40 K below bulk; see concern 12). The authors should: (i) compare the frequencies and linewidths of strain-sensitive phonons well above Tc (orthorhombic Ag(o) modes in the aa channel, for example at 340–400 K) and well below Tc across all five flakes, to bound any relative strain, and discuss interfacial strain explicitly; (ii) add a control that keeps the Au and its mechanical environment but blocks direct electronic contact, for example Au evaporated onto 1–2 nm of ALD Al2O3 or a monolayer/thin hBN spacer, and/or a non-metal deposited the same way as Au (an evaporated dielectric); (iii) if possible, characterize the interface (cross-sectional TEM/EDX or XPS of Au-deposited Ta2NiSe5) to show whether the interfacial layer stays chemically intact. Without these, screening and charge transfer are one hypothesis among several.

3. The data do not separate excitonic from electron-lattice mechanisms, and the quantitative split of Tc is not supported. The conclusion that Au "weaken[s] electron-hole interactions" rather than electron-lattice coupling, and that excitonic mechanisms account for about 150 K of Tc with "the remaining ~180 K" from the lattice (pp. 15–16), rests on assumptions none of which is shown: (a) disappearance of the quasi-elastic peak in 4L/Au means excitonic fluctuations are destroyed, not just buried in noise (see concern 5); (b) the 16% drop of the maximum of −1/q (0.38 against 0.45) means unchanged electron-phonon coupling; (c) the interfacial layers lose their excitonic contribution completely; (d) excitonic and lattice contributions to Tc add linearly. On (b), the Fano parameter depends on the continuum's density of states and its coupling, both of which change directly with doping and screening, so a "not strongly reduced" −1/q does not isolate the electron-lattice coupling constant. More generally, electron doping and metallic screening also suppress a purely hybridization-, band-Jahn-Teller- or phonon-driven structural instability (as in electron-doped CDW systems), by moving the Fermi level away from compensation and screening interband matrix elements, so a Tc reduction under charge transfer is expected in both pictures; strain or chemistry (concern 2) would also reduce Tc without being specifically excitonic. Further, even in the mixed-phase picture the interfacial layers do not reach a monoclinic ground state above the lowest measured temperature, so "suppression by up to 150 K" is a lower bound on the perturbation of those layers, not a measure of the excitonic share; and relative to the 4L/Al2O3 baseline the Au-induced suppression is about 100 K, not 150 K. The authors should either support the attribution with a model calculation (even a minimal two-band model with phonon coupling plus a screened interaction, or a Landau/mean-field model with exciton-lattice coupling in which screening reduces only the excitonic coupling) showing that the observed Fano and quasi-elastic pattern discriminates between the scenarios and predicts the size of the Tc shift, or substantially soften the abstract, Discussion and Conclusions, which say the results "confirm the existence of excitonic effects", to "consistent with", and drop or qualify the numerical 150 K / 180 K split.

4. The depth-dependent optical weighting near a metal substrate goes against the claim that the interfacial layers are detected. The mixed-phase interpretation (Fig. 5b inset, pp. 13–14) assumes the Raman signal "averages over all layers" and that the 1–2 layers next to Au are "detectable in the overall signal". But on an optically thick Au mirror the 632.8 nm standing-wave (tangential) field has a near-node at the metal surface, so for a 2.5–4 nm flake the layer next to Au is weighted far less than the top layer, in both excitation and collection, whereas the flakes on Al2O3 (50–100 nm above Au) sit in a very different standing-wave pattern. The much weaker ac signals on Au are consistent with this: scale factors ×25 and ×50 for 6L and 4L on Au (Figs. 3e, 4e) against ×3 for Fig. 3c, and colour bars of 0.57 against 52.6. Consequences: the layers whose Tc is supposedly suppressed by about 150 K may contribute little to the spectrum, so the low-temperature end of the transition (about 180 K) is poorly constrained, and if they are weighted weakly the observed broadening needs either a larger perturbed region or a different cause. The authors should compute the layer-resolved Raman weight (incident × scattered field intensity, transfer-matrix) for each stack, including the Al2O3/Au stack beneath the Al2O3 flakes, use it as the layer weights in the toy model of Supplementary Note 4, and show that the measured lineshapes (Fig. 3t, Fig. 4o) are consistent with the extent of the interface region they propose.

B. Should address

5. The absence of the quasi-elastic peak in 4L/Au may be a sensitivity limit. The statement that the quasi-elastic peak is "suppressed beyond detection"/"undetectable" (p. 12) is used on p. 15 to "affirm" its excitonic origin and as positive evidence that excitonic fluctuations are destroyed. But Fig. 4t shows χ''(10 cm^-1) at ×50 scaling, the ac signal on Au is 50–100 times weaker than on Al2O3, the Rayleigh leakage peak at 15 cm^-1 was removed by interpolation in exactly the 6L and 4L Au datasets (Methods, p. 19), and the 10 cm^-1 cut lies close to both the filter edge and the removed region. The authors should show raw, uncorrected low-energy spectra for the 6L and 4L Au data; give the noise level and an upper bound on the quasi-elastic amplitude normalized to an internal phonon reference in the same spectrum (for example the integrated Ag(m2)/B2g(o1) intensity); show that a quasi-elastic peak of the expected relative size would have been detectable at the achieved signal-to-noise; and demonstrate that the Rayleigh removal does not affect the 10 cm^-1 point.

6. The vertical Tc gradient is inferred, not tested against lateral inhomogeneity or uniform suppression. A broadened, averaged transition could also come from lateral inhomogeneity within the laser spot: thickness steps or wrinkles, bubbles, roughness of the polycrystalline/amorphous Au, or patchy Au–flake contact. The spot size is not given and no spatial map is shown. The gradient picture also has internal tensions. If 1–2 interfacial layers are perturbed, the mixed fraction is about 2/4 for 4L but only about 2/6 for 6L, yet both flakes show the same ±40 K width and similar kink spacing. And the model assumes the layers far from Au have Tc "closer to room temperature" (p. 13), yet 4L/Al2O3 with no claimed interface effect is already at 280 K, while the dominant top layers of 4L/Au begin their transition at about 220–250 K (first kink, Fig. 3t), which suggests suppression across the whole flake rather than a gradient. The authors should give the spot size and show Raman maps (for example of the Ag(m5) onset), or spectra at several positions on the 4L/Au and 6L/Au flakes at temperatures inside the broadened range, and check whether the width depends on position; state which Tc the toy model assigns to the top layers of 4L/Au (bulk, the 4L/Al2O3 value or a free parameter) and show that the model reproduces Fig. 3t and Fig. 4o quantitatively with physically motivated weights (concern 4); explain how the equal 4L and 6L widths fit the "one or two layers" picture (pp. 13–14); and present a 5L or 3L dependence, or at least discuss why a gradient model fits better than uniform suppression plus lateral spread, ideally by fitting both models directly.

7. Charge transfer is asserted, not measured or estimated. Neither the amount nor the sign of charge transfer from Au is estimated. The work function of the Au (polycrystalline, on Cr) is not compared with that of Ta2NiSe5 (~5.4 eV, cited), and no doping-related observable is shown: no XPS/UPS core-level or valence-band shift, no Kelvin-probe measurement, no doping-sensitive phonon frequency or asymmetry change at high temperature relative to Al2O3, no change in the electronic background, and no second metal with a different work function. Without at least an order-of-magnitude estimate of the transferred carrier density, set against the doping levels at which K dosing or substitution suppress Tc, charge transfer cannot be weighed against screening, yet it is part of the argument for an excitonic mechanism. The authors should give such an estimate (for example from the work-function difference and an interfacial capacitance), add any Raman, XPS/UPS or Kelvin-probe evidence available, and ideally test a high-work-function metal such as Pt, which they themselves propose, to check the doping direction. An independent probe of doping and the gap on the Au-supported flakes (XPS/ARPES through the evaporated-Au route, since the transfer can end in UHV, or low-temperature reflectance of the gap edge) would strengthen this further.

8. Sample temperature and laser heating are not quantified per sample. The laser power is about 1 mW and the effective temperature was "checked" by the Stokes/anti-Stokes ratio (Methods, p. 18), but no values are given. The stacks differ in absorption (metal mirror against 50–100 nm Al2O3 over Au) and thermal anchoring (direct Au contact against an amorphous ALD oxide), so a 4L flake on Al2O3 may be much more thermally isolated; heating by even 10–30 K would shift the apparent Tc by an amount comparable to the 40 K Al2O3 suppression and differently on each substrate. The Methods also describe a cryostat only "for measurements below 300 K"; it is not stated how the 300–400 K spectra were taken, or whether a change of setup or window calibration occurs near 300 K, which is exactly where the 7L/Al2O3 and 18L/Au transitions (~320 K) lie. The authors should report the Stokes/anti-Stokes temperature against nominal temperature for each flake (at least for the two 4L flakes), show a power dependence of a Tc marker (for example the Ag(m5) intensity near Tc) for 4L/Al2O3 and 4L/Au, describe the high-temperature setup and show that the data join continuously across 300 K, and state that the Bose correction of Eq. (2) uses the calibrated temperature.

9. The transition width is defined by an ad hoc "second kink" construction. The ±40 K broadening for the 6L and 4L flakes on Au (pp. 9–10) comes from the spacing between the first and second kinks in the Ag(m4) position: 100 K on Au versus 60 K elsewhere, giving 40 K. This assumes the second kink lies a fixed intrinsic offset below Tc (a value taken from only three flakes) and that its extra separation maps directly onto the distribution of Tc. Neither is justified, the offset could itself depend on doping or strain, and the kinks in Fig. 3s, t are described as "broadened and less defined", on peak positions quantized at 1.3 cm^-1. The authors should justify or drop the second-kink argument and instead define the transition by a single model-based fit applied identically to all five flakes, for example the temperature dependence of the integrated Ag(m4)/Ag(m5) intensity in the ac channel (which scales with the square of the monoclinic order parameter), or −1/q(T), fitted to an order-parameter form convolved with a (Gaussian) distribution of Tc, reporting fitted centres and widths with uncertainties.

10. Temperature smoothing may create or exaggerate the broadening. The 2D maps are resampled on a 5 K grid and Gaussian-smoothed with σ = 10–15 K in temperature (Methods, p. 19), comparable to the 10 K measurement steps near Tc and to the widths claimed for the sharp flakes; Fig. 3k–o are second derivatives of these smoothed maps. If the peak positions in Fig. 3p–t were taken from the smoothed second-derivative maps, the kinks are artificially broadened, and for the noisy 4L/Au data smoothing plus differentiation can turn a weak, noisy onset into an apparently gradual one. The authors should state whether Fig. 3p–t uses raw or smoothed spectra and whether the same smoothing was used for every sample, and show peak positions fitted to the raw spectra at each measured temperature, with uncertainties, alongside Fig. 3p–t.
