Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors report a dry exfoliation and transfer route (evaporated Au or ALD Al2O3 on a freshly cleaved crystal, then Au cold welding) that yields 4–18-layer Ta2NiSe5 flakes on Au and on Al2O3. They use polarized Raman scattering (the onset of the monoclinic Ag(m4)/Ag(m5) modes, the Fano asymmetry of B2g(o1), and the quasi-elastic intensity at 10 cm^-1) to track the 326 K transition. They find that 4L and 6L flakes on Au show a transition lowered by about 100 K and broadened, while flakes on Al2O3 stay close to bulk. From this they argue for an interfacial charge-transfer and screening effect that selectively suppresses excitonic fluctuations, and so for an excitonic contribution of at least about 100 K to Tc. Substrate control of a candidate excitonic insulator is a timely and attractive idea, and the fabrication protocol may be useful more widely. However, the step from "Tc is lower on Au" to "excitonic interactions are suppressed by charge transfer and screening" is not yet supported by the controls and analyses the paper presents.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The paper presents a new, solvent-free fabrication route for ultrathin, rippled vdW chalcogenides (Fig. 1C–E). The flakes have large lateral size (10–100 µm), and the method could be used for other materials.
- The Raman analysis is careful at the level of symmetry. It has bare-substrate references in both channels (Fig. 2F,G), the ac channel is chosen to suppress substrate signal, and the authors show that the orthorhombic-to-monoclinic distortion can be detected down to 4L.
- Three independent Raman markers (phonon onset and kinks, Fano −1/q, quasi-elastic intensity) are followed on the same flakes over a wide temperature range (Figs. 3, 4), and the structural and electronic Tc values from them agree reasonably well (Fig. 5).
- The 7L/Al2O3 and 18L/Au flakes recover the bulk Tc. This gives an internal check that the fabrication route by itself does not destroy the transition.
- The result bears on a debated question (excitonic versus lattice-driven transition in Ta2NiSe5) with a new kind of perturbation, and it is relevant to devices that place metal contacts on Ta2NiSe5.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. Each condition rests on a single flake, and the thickness series on the two substrates is not matched. The central comparison, 4L/Au against 4L/Al2O3, uses one flake of each (Figs. 3–5), and every other thickness is also a single sample (7L/Al2O3; 18L, 6L, 4L/Au). There is no 6L/Al2O3 or 18L/Al2O3 flake, and no thinner flake on either substrate. The paper therefore cannot separate flake-to-flake variation (strain from the cold-welding press, local damage, thickness misassignment) from the substrate effect. This matters all the more because 4L/Al2O3 already shows a 15% reduction (280 K) that the paper does not explain. The authors should measure at least two or three further flakes for each of 4L/Au and 4L/Al2O3, add matched thicknesses (for example 6L/Al2O3), and report the spread of Tc and of the width of the transition. Ideally they should also show a single flake that crosses from Au onto Al2O3, or regions of one flake on both substrates, so that the comparison is made within one sample.

2. Au and Al2O3 differ in much more than their conductivity, and the controls needed to isolate charge transfer and screening are missing. The two substrates are made differently. Au is thermally evaporated directly onto the cleaved Ta2NiSe5 surface, where hot Au atoms can react with Se or interdiffuse, and Au–chalcogen bonding is strong. Al2O3 is grown by ALD at 100–150 °C with TMA/H2O precursors, and is then capped with Au (Methods, pp. 16–17). The two interfaces will also differ in roughness, conformality to the ripples, adhesion, and the strain they impose through different thermal expansion on cooling to 100 K. Uniaxial strain is known to tune both Tc and the excitonic fluctuations in Ta2NiSe5 (ref. 17, from the same group). Without further controls, any of these could lower and broaden the transition in the interfacial layers. The paper also offers no direct evidence that charge is transferred: no doping level, no shift of the work function or of the bands, no XPS or ARPES of the interface, and no estimate of how much charge the Au/Ta2NiSe5 work-function difference would transfer. The authors should:
   - (a) add a spacer control, such as Au separated from the flake by a thin insulator (a few nm of ALD Al2O3 or hBN), which keeps the strain and mechanical boundary conditions but removes charge transfer and leaves screening;
   - (b) check for strain using the shifts of the Ag phonon frequencies at fixed temperature between Au and Al2O3, compared with the known strain coefficients;
   - (c) characterize the Au/Ta2NiSe5 interface chemically, for example by XPS or cross-sectional TEM, or at least argue quantitatively against Au–Se reaction;
   - (d) give an electrostatic estimate of the induced carrier density and its decay over layers.

   Without at least (a) or (b), "charge transfer and electrostatic screening… are the likely culprit" (p. 15) remains one hypothesis among several.

3. The gradient picture is not consistent with the paper's own onset temperatures, and it ignores the depth weighting of Raman scattering above a metal. The model says that only one or two layers next to Au are strongly suppressed, while the surface layers keep a Tc "closer to room temperature" (pp. 4, 13–14, Fig. 5B inset). If so, the highest-Tc component, which is the first kink, should stay near the 4L/Al2O3 or bulk value. Instead, the first kink sits at 250 K for 6L/Au and 220 K for 4L/Au (Fig. 3S,T). In 6L/Au, four or five layers are supposedly unaffected, yet the onset is 70 K below bulk. This suggests that the whole flake is shifted, not just a thin interfacial region.

   In addition, the tangential optical field has a node at a metal surface. The layers nearest Au are therefore the ones Raman scattering samples *least* efficiently. The very large intensity suppression on Au (×3, ×25 and ×50 scaling for 18L, 6L and 4L in Figs. 3–4) is consistent with this. The claim that "Raman scattering from the interfacial Ta2NiSe5 layers becomes detectable" (p. 13), and the inference that the interface effect is "confined to one or two layers" (p. 14), therefore need a quantitative basis. The authors should:
   - compute layer-resolved Raman weights with a transfer-matrix model for each stack (air/TNS/Au/Cr/SiO2/Si and air/TNS/Al2O3/Au/…) at 632.8 nm;
   - model the measured response as a weighted sum over layers with an assumed Tc(z) profile;
   - show that a profile confined to one or two layers reproduces both the onset temperatures and the broadening in Figs. 3S,T and 4N,O,S,T.

   If the data instead need a suppression across the whole flake, the interpretation should change.

4. The claim that Au weakens electron–hole interactions rather than electron–lattice coupling rests on an asymmetric comparison that is not robust. The argument on p. 15 has two parts. First, the quasi-elastic peak at 10 cm^-1 is "suppressed beyond detection" in 4L/Au. Second, the maximum of −1/q drops only by 17%, which is said to be explainable by temperature broadening. Both parts are weak as presented.
   - The 4L/Au spectra are about 50 times weaker than the reference flakes. Fig. 4T has visible scatter of a size comparable to the peak expected after broadening. A Rayleigh leakage feature at 15 cm^-1 was removed by interpolation in the Au samples only (p. 18). And 10 cm^-1 is close to the cutoff of a single-grating spectrometer. A quasi-elastic peak broadened over about 100 K and reduced in proportion would plausibly be undetectable at this signal-to-noise for purely instrumental reasons.
   - The statement that "This moderate reduction could be explained by a simple temperature broadening" is not demonstrated. The authors should convolve the 18L/Au (or 7L/Al2O3) curves of −1/q(T) and χ''(10 cm^-1, T) with the same Tc distribution inferred from Fig. 3. They would then show quantitatively that broadening accounts for −1/q but *not* for the loss of the quasi-elastic peak, and give error bars for the upper limit on the quasi-elastic amplitude.
   - A single Fano fit to a superposition of layers in different phases (orthorhombic and monoclinic, with B2g(o1) evolving into Ag(m2) and Ag(o1)* leaking into the 20–80 cm^-1 fit window, Fig. 4F) has no clear meaning for q. The fit procedure, the free parameters, the treatment of the background and of Ag(o1)*/Ag(m1), and the systematic uncertainties should be stated. The quoted "error bars smaller than the symbols" are statistical only.
   - Carrier doping and screening also suppress a hybridization/band-Jahn–Teller instability: changing the band filling and screening the interband Coulomb interaction both reduce the energy gained from the distortion. So a lowered Tc by itself does not show that the mechanism is excitonic. The paper should explain why a lattice-driven scenario would *not* respond in the same way, ideally with a model estimate. It should also engage substantively with ref. 60, where gate doping led to a different conclusion. At present this is a one-sentence note.

   Until these points are dealt with, the statements that the result "establishes an excitonic component" (p. 16) and that "the suppression of excitonic mechanisms lowers the 326 K Tc… by at least 100 K" (p. 15) are stronger than the data support.

B. Should address

5. Laser heating is not quantified, and it can mimic a lowered Tc. If the laser spot is hotter than the cryostat, the transition appears at a *lower* nominal temperature, which is exactly the effect claimed. With 1 mW at 632.8 nm on 4–6L flakes, the absorption and the heat sinking differ between the stacks: Au/Cr directly on the wafer versus 50–100 nm Al2O3 on Au. The Methods say that the Stokes/anti-Stokes ratio was checked, but no data are shown, the spot size is not given, and it is not said whether the check was made on each flake and at which temperatures. The authors should show the Stokes/anti-Stokes temperatures for each flake, or a laser-power series (for example 0.25, 0.5 and 1 mW) of the Ag(m4)/Ag(m5) onset on 4L/Au and 4L/Al2O3, and state the spot size and power density.

6. The extraction of Tc^avg and its uncertainty is ad hoc and partly internally inconsistent. On p. 10, Tc^avg for 6L and 4L on Au is set equal to the first-kink temperature (250 and 220 K), with ±40 K derived from the assumption that the separation between the two kinks is a universal 60 K. But in the gradient picture the first kink marks the *highest*-Tc layers, so an average should lie below it. The definition of the second kink is also not tied to any physical process that would justify the subtraction. The structural and electronic criteria differ as well (a kink at ± half the temperature step versus within 15% of the maximum of χ''(10 cm^-1)), and their results disagree by more than the quoted error for 18L/Au (320 versus 306±14 K). The authors should:
   - fit the onset of the integrated Ag(m4)+Ag(m5) intensity (an order-parameter-like quantity) for every flake with a model that includes a distribution of Tc, and extract the centre and width directly;
   - apply one criterion to all flakes;
   - qualify the abstract's "reduced by over 100 K", given that 326 − (220±40) does not exclude reductions well below 100 K.

7. Interpolation and smoothing can affect the "broadening" seen in the 2D maps. Figs. 3F–O and 4F–J are interpolated onto a 5 K grid and Gaussian-smoothed by 10–15 K in temperature (p. 18), with raw temperature steps of 10–50 K. Smoothing of this size is comparable to the broadening being claimed, and the second-derivative maps (Fig. 3K–O) amplify noise in the weak 6L and 4L/Au data (colour scales ±0.6 and ±0.3 against ±15). The kinks should be determined from the raw spectra by fitting each peak (Lorentzian position with uncertainty). The temperature steps should be stated for each flake, and the 2D maps should be presented as illustrations only, not used to judge the width of the transition.

8. Thickness determination and the referenced Supplementary Notes are not available. Layer counts (4L, 6L, 7L, 18L) rest on AFM (Supplementary Note 3), and the symmetry assignments and further structural analysis are in Supplementary Notes 1–2. None of these are included. For flakes on evaporated, rough, amorphous Au and on ALD Al2O3, a one-layer error at 4L is a 25% thickness error, which matters directly for the layer-dependence argument. The authors should provide AFM height profiles with substrate roughness for each measured flake, ideally with an independent check (optical contrast or the intensity of the interlayer shear/breathing modes), and include all Supplementary Notes.

9. The 4L/Al2O3 result is described inconsistently and not explained. The abstract says 4L/Al2O3 shows "nearly bulk-like properties" and the Discussion says "the absence of any notable interface effect from Al2O3". Yet Tc drops to 280–287 K (a 15% reduction), which is larger than the up to 9% reported for 5L on SiO2 (ref. 45). If Al2O3 has no interface effect, the 15% reduction must come from dimensionality or from the fabrication itself, and the same contribution must then be present in 4L/Au. The excitonic share attributed to Au should then be measured relative to 4L/Al2O3 (about 60 K), not to bulk (about 100 K). The authors should state clearly which baseline is used and adjust the "at least 100 K" claim accordingly.

10. The ambient history and degradation of the flakes are not documented. The 4L/Al2O3 control rules out degradation only if both flakes had the same exposure to air, laser and temperature cycling. The Methods do not say whether the measurements were made in vacuum throughout, how long each flake spent in air, whether flakes were thermally cycled, or whether the room-temperature spectra were reproduced after cooling. The authors should report this and show a 295 K spectrum taken before and after the temperature sweep for 4L/Au.

11. The temperature range above 300 K uses an unspecified setup. The Methods describe a cryostat "for measurements below 300 K", yet the data extend to 340–400 K and the key points near Tc lie between 300 and 340 K. The authors should state how temperatures above 300 K were reached and calibrated, and whether the calibration factor for the viewport and the optical alignment were the same across the two regimes. Mismatched setups could produce artificial kinks near 300 K.

C. Minor

12. Fig. 4 caption: the caption refers to dashed curves "in (H) and (J)", but the text (p. 11) refers to "Fig. 4, H and I", and the figure shows them in H and I.

13. Fig. 3 caption and text: the "second kink" (open triangles) is defined in the text, but the reason Ag(m4) hardens below it is not explained. The authors should cite or explain its origin, since it is used quantitatively.

14. Fig. 2C labels Ag(o2)* and Ag(o3)* as polarization leakage. The authors should quantify the polarizer extinction ratio and confirm it is the same for all flakes, since leakage of Ag(o3)* is used as the high-temperature marker in Fig. 3.

15. Fig. 5: the open circle for 4L/Au and its error bar (about 165–275 K) should be checked against the "220±40 K" in the text, and the diamond for 4L/Au should be marked explicitly as not determined.

16. p. 3: "exctonic" should be "excitonic". p. 16: "high-quality, vdW materials" has a stray comma.

17. The Fano function in Eq. (1) has no background term. If one was used in practice, say so, and state the fit range for each temperature.

18. The data availability statement ("will be made available online") should give a repository.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a substrate control that separates the two effects: Au with an ultrathin insulating spacer, and ideally a second metal with a different work function (for example Pt or Ti/Au). This would separate charge transfer from screening and from mechanical and chemical effects, and would turn the qualitative "Au versus Al2O3" contrast into a test of the mechanism.
2. Build a quantitative forward model that combines a transfer-matrix optical weighting with a layer-dependent Tc(z). Fit it jointly to the phonon-onset, Fano and quasi-elastic data for 4L and 6L on Au, so that the "one to two interfacial layers" statement becomes a fitted result with uncertainties.
3. Measure directly how the interface changes the electronic structure, for example with micro-ARPES or STM/STS on the top surface of thin flakes on Au versus Al2O3 (the dry, UHV-compatible protocol makes this possible). This would test both the gradient picture and whether the top layers stay gapped near room temperature.
4. Reframe the excitonic-share conclusion relative to the 4L/Al2O3 baseline. Discuss explicitly how a purely lattice-driven or hybridization mechanism would respond to interfacial doping and screening, and reconcile the result with the gate-tuning study (ref. 60).
