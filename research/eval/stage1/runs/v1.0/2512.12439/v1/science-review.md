A. Must address

1. Charge transfer and screening are inferred rather than shown, and non-electronic explanations for the Au effect are not excluded (pp. 14-16, Fig. 5B). The mechanism ("charge transfer and electrostatic screening ... are the likely culprit", p. 15) rests only on the contrast between Au and Al2O3. No measurement in the paper shows charge transfer: there is no carrier density, no core-level or work-function data, no band-edge or phonon-frequency shift, no estimate from the Au/Ta2NiSe5 work-function offset, and not even the sign of the transferred charge (Fig. 5B suggests electrons). As a result, "charge transfer" and "screening (mirror charges)" cannot be told apart. The two substrates also differ in much more than conductivity. Au is thermally evaporated directly onto the freshly cleaved surface, where hot Au atoms can react with Se, intercalate into the first vdW gap, interdiffuse or create defects; Au-chalcogen bonding is strong. Al2O3 is grown by ALD at 100-150 °C with TMA/H2O and then capped with Au (Methods, pp. 16-17). The interfaces also differ in roughness, conformality to the ripples and adhesion. Their mechanical boundary conditions differ too: the cold-welding press, and differential thermal contraction on cooling from 340 K down to 10-100 K. The wafer under each sample (SiO2/Si or sapphire, p. 16) is not stated. Ta2NiSe5 Tc and its excitonic fluctuations are known to be very sensitive to pressure and uniaxial strain (refs. 7, 14-17, including ref. 17 from the same group), and a strain explanation would have no special connection to excitons. The 4L/Al2O3 control rules out generic oxidation (p. 15), but not damage specific to the Au interface. The authors should: (i) compare the absolute Ag-mode frequencies of 4L/Au and 4L/Al2O3, and of each flake against bulk, at matched temperatures in the orthorhombic and monoclinic phases, and use known mode Grüneisen or strain coefficients to bound the strain; (ii) characterize the Au/Ta2NiSe5 interface chemically, by XPS of the Au-Se chemistry or cross-sectional STEM/EDX, or at least argue quantitatively against reaction and intercalation; (iii) give an electrostatic estimate of the transferred sheet density and its decay over layers, and relate it to the gate-induced densities in ref. 60 and the doping levels in K-dosing studies (refs. 26-29); (iv) state the wafer for each sample; (v) ideally, add a control that separates electrostatics from chemistry and strain. Possible controls are a monolayer hBN or a few-nm (about 1 nm) Al2O3 spacer between Au and the flake, which keeps the mechanical boundary conditions but removes charge transfer; Au deposited on top of a flake already on Al2O3; a substrate that screens without transferring charge (graphite) or a metal with a different work function (Pt, Ti/Au); or a Raman temperature series on the 2L/4L ribbons already obtained on non-evaporated Au(111)/mica (Fig. 1B), which tests a pristine Au contact. The paper should also state explicitly that the existing Al2O3 samples sit on Au/Cr below 50-100 nm of Al2O3, so they are effectively a thick-spacer control. Without at least the strain bound or a spacer control, the mechanism remains one hypothesis among several, and "manifesting the presence of excitonic interactions" (abstract) does not follow.

2. The conclusion that Au weakens electron-hole rather than electron-lattice interactions rests on an asymmetric and circular comparison (p. 15, Fig. 4). The argument has two parts. First, the quasi-elastic peak (QEP) at 10 cm⁻¹ disappears in 4L/Au (Fig. 4T) and is read as destruction of excitonic fluctuations. Second, the 17% drop in the maximum of -1/q is dismissed as "simple temperature broadening", which is not demonstrated. Both parts are weak. (a) The reasoning is circular. The microscopic origin of the QEP is contested (direct excitonic fluctuations, ref. 54, versus an exciton-coupled acoustic phonon, ref. 55). The paper uses the Au-induced suppression to "affirm" that the QEP is excitonic, and then uses the QEP to conclude that Au suppresses excitonic interactions. (b) Detection limits are not addressed. The 4L/Au and 6L/Au spectra are scaled by ×50 and ×25. A Rayleigh leak at 15 cm⁻¹ was removed by interpolation in the Au samples only (Methods, p. 18). The 10 cm⁻¹ point is even closer to the laser line on a single-grating instrument. Fig. 4T is flat at about 3.5 (×50), with scatter comparable to the peak expected after broadening. A QEP broadened over about 100 K at a signal 50 times weaker may simply fall below the noise or under stray light rather than be destroyed. (c) Broadening is applied to one marker and not the other. A distribution of local Tc would also smear and lower the χ''ac(10 cm⁻¹, T) peak; the 6L/Au data (Fig. 4S) already show a broad plateau. (d) The Fano asymmetry is not a clean measure of electron-lattice coupling. In an excitonic-insulator scenario the continuum it couples to is the electron-hole continuum itself, and a single Fano fit to a superposition of layers in different phases has no clear meaning for q. (e) Carrier doping and screening would also suppress a hybridization-gap or band-Jahn-Teller instability, since changes in band filling and screening of the interband Coulomb interaction both reduce the energy gained from the distortion. A lowered Tc by itself therefore does not show that the mechanism is excitonic. (f) Ref. 60, a gated, hBN-encapsulated study that gives a cleaner electrostatic-doping comparison, reaches a different conclusion, but the paper acknowledges this in one sentence (p. 16) without saying what that conclusion is or how the two results could be reconciled. The authors should: convolve the 18L/Au, 7L/Al2O3 or 4L/Al2O3 curves of -1/q(T) and χ''(10 cm⁻¹, T) with the Tc distribution inferred from Fig. 3, and show quantitatively that broadening accounts for the -1/q change but not for the loss of the QEP; give an upper limit on the QEP amplitude in 4L/Au, for example by injecting the scaled 4L/Al2O3 QEP into the 4L/Au noise; show raw, uninterpolated low-frequency spectra below 25 cm⁻¹ for all flakes, with the filter edge and stray-light background; consider integrating χ'' over a window (for example 8-20 cm⁻¹) instead of a single point; explain, ideally with a model estimate, why a lattice-driven or hybridization transition would not respond to interfacial doping and screening in the same way; and discuss ref. 60 substantively. Until then, "establishes an excitonic component" (p. 16) should be softened.

The headline magnitude, "reduced by over 100 K" and "the suppression of excitonic mechanisms lowers the 326 K Tc ... by at least 100 K" (abstract; p. 15), uses the wrong reference and is not supported by the stated uncertainty. Both statements use bulk 326 K as the reference. But 4L/Al2O3 already transitions at 280 K (15% below bulk), so part of the 4L/Au reduction is a thickness, encapsulation or fabrication effect unrelated to the conducting substrate. The substrate-induced shift at fixed thickness is 280 K versus 220 ± 40 K, about 60 ± 40 K. Even relative to bulk, the reduction is 106 ± 40 K, so the quoted ±40 K alone makes "at least" untenable. "At least" also assumes that Au removes excitonic correlations completely in the measured layers, which is not shown. The authors should quote the substrate effect as a shift at fixed thickness against the 4L/Al2O3 control with propagated uncertainties, state clearly which baseline is used, and revise the abstract and the estimate of the excitonic share (pp. 15-16). That share should be framed as an estimate whose bounds depend on the assumptions of the interface model.

4. The layer-resolved "Tc gradient" picture is contradicted by the paper's own onset temperatures, and it ignores the depth weighting of Raman scattering above a metal (pp. 4, 13-14, Fig. 5B inset). The model says that only the one or two layers next to Au transition far lower, while the layers away from it keep a Tc "closer to room temperature". If so, the first (onset) kink, which marks the highest-Tc layers, should stay near the 4L/Al2O3 (280 K) or bulk value. Instead it sits at 220 K for 4L/Au and 250 K for 6L/Au (Fig. 3S,T). In 6L/Au, four or five layers are supposedly unaffected, yet the onset is 70 K below bulk. This suggests the whole flake is shifted, which does not fit an effect "confined to one or two layers" (p. 14). With interlayer coupling in a 4L crystal, an independent layer-by-layer Tc is also not obvious. In addition, at 632.8 nm the tangential field has a node at a metal surface. The layers next to Au are therefore the ones Raman scattering samples least efficiently, not the ones that "become detectable in the overall signal" (p. 13). The roughly 50-100-fold weaker signal on Au (colour scales in Figs. 3G, I, J and 4I, J; the ×3, ×25 and ×50 scale factors for 18L, 6L and 4L) is consistent with this. The confinement to one or two layers is a deduction from the persistence of broadening, not a result. The authors should compute layer-resolved Raman weights with a transfer-matrix model for each stack (air/Ta2NiSe5/Au/Cr/SiO2/Si and air/Ta2NiSe5/Al2O3/Au/Cr/...). They should then model the response as a weighted sum of bulk-like layer spectra with an assumed Tc(z) profile, and show that a profile confined to one or two layers reproduces the onset temperature, the kink separation and the evolution of the Ag(m5) intensity and position, the Fano parameter and the quasi-elastic response for 4L and 6L on Au (Figs. 3S,T and 4N,O,S,T). Ideally this should be a joint fit giving the depth of the Au effect with uncertainties. If the data instead need suppression across the whole flake, the interpretation should change. Separately, they should rule out lateral inhomogeneity as the source of broadening (amorphous-Au roughness, partial contact, local strain), for example with Raman maps across each flake at a few temperatures.

5. Each condition rests on a single flake, and the thickness series is not matched across substrates. Five flakes are shown in total: 7L/Al2O3, 4L/Al2O3, and 18L, 6L and 4L on Au. The central contrast (4L/Au against 4L/Al2O3) and the 6L/Au result each come from one flake. There is no 6L or 18L flake on Al2O3, and no thinner flake on either substrate, so a thickness effect on Al2O3 (4L is already 15% lower) cannot be separated from the substrate effect at 6L. Flake-to-flake variation from local strain from the cold-welding press, interface and contact quality on amorphous Au, folds, local damage, laser position or thickness misassignment could plausibly produce Tc shifts of tens of kelvin, comparable to the effect claimed. The authors should measure at least two or three further 4L flakes (and ideally 6L) on each substrate, preferably from different exfoliation runs, report the spread in Tc and in transition width, and add thickness-matched flakes such as 6L/Al2O3. Ideally they should also show a single flake that crosses from Au onto Al2O3, so the comparison is made within one sample.

6. Laser heating and the effective sample temperature are not documented, and heating could by itself produce a lowered, broadened transition (Methods, p. 17). About 1 mW at 632.8 nm is used, and the Methods say that "the actual temperature" was checked from the Stokes/anti-Stokes ratio, but no ratios, inferred temperatures or power dependences are shown. The spot size and power density are not given, and it is not said whether the check was made on each flake and at which temperatures. Heating above the stage temperature makes the transition appear at a lower nominal temperature, and a temperature gradient across the spot and through the stack broadens it. These are the signatures attributed to the Au interface. Absorption in the flake, the local field, heat sinking and thermal boundary resistance all differ between the stacks (Au over a ~2 nm Cr layer directly on the wafer, versus an extra 50-100 nm of Al2O3), so the direction of the effect is not obvious and it bears directly on the 60 K substrate difference. The 18L/Au reference does not settle this, because absorption and heat spreading in 18L differ from those in 4L. The authors should show the Stokes/anti-Stokes temperature against stage temperature for each of the five flakes, especially 4L/Au and 4L/Al2O3; repeat the 4L/Au (and 4L/Al2O3) temperature series at reduced power (for example 0.25 and 0.5 mW) to show that the onset and width do not change; and state the spot size and power density.

B. Should address

7. The definition of Tc and Tc^avg is ad hoc and inconsistent across samples (p. 10, Fig. 3). For the sharp flakes Tc is the onset kink (solid triangle). For 6L and 4L on Au, the same onset kink (250 K and 220 K) is relabelled Tc^avg and given ±40 K, derived from the assumption that the separation between the first and second kinks is a universal 60 K (100 K versus 60 K). In a distribution of local Tc the onset kink marks the upper end, so an average must lie below it; if 220 K is the average, the onset criterion is being applied inconsistently. The extra separation of 40 K more naturally corresponds to a total spread of about 40 K (±20 K) centred below the onset. The assumption that the second kink sits at a fixed 60 K below Tc, and that any extra gap measures symmetric broadening, is not tied to any physical process. The structural and electronic criteria also differ (a kink with ± half the temperature step versus within 15% of the maximum of χ''(10 cm⁻¹)), and for 18L/Au they disagree by more than the quoted error (320 versus 306 ± 14 K). The authors should apply one criterion to all five samples. For example, they could fit the integrated Ag(m4)+Ag(m5) intensity (an order-parameter-like quantity), or the Ag(o3)*/Ag(m4) shift versus T, with a model that includes a distribution of Tc, and extract the midpoint and width directly, or define Tc^avg from the layer model of concern 4.

8. The way the kinks are extracted may itself broaden or shift the transition (Methods, pp. 17-18; Fig. 3F-T, Fig. 4F-J). The 2D maps are interpolated onto a 5 K grid and smoothed with a 10-15 K Gaussian in temperature, with raw temperature steps of 10-50 K. The second-derivative maps (Fig. 3K-O) are built from these smoothed maps and amplify noise in the weak 6L and 4L/Au data (colour scales ±0.6 and ±0.3 against ±15). It is not clear whether the peak positions in Fig. 3P-T, and hence the kinks and the "broadening", come from raw spectra or smoothed maps. Smoothing of this size is comparable to the claimed differences and Tc uncertainties. The kink criterion also relies on a "small but abrupt" shift of the leaked Ag(o3)* mode comparable to the 1.3 cm⁻¹ point spacing, and the 4L/Au kink is described as "less defined". The authors should extract peak positions and intensities from Lorentzian/Fano fits to the raw, unsmoothed spectra at each measured temperature, with fit uncertainties; state the temperature steps for each flake; and present the 2D maps as illustrations only, not for judging the width of the transition.

9. The Fano fits lack detail and realistic uncertainties (Eq. 1; Fig. 4K-O). Eq. 1 has no background term. The 20-80 cm⁻¹ window also contains the leaked Ag(o1)* mode near 35 cm⁻¹ (Fig. 4F), Ag(m1) at low temperature, and on Au the region near the interpolated 15 cm⁻¹ Rayleigh artefact. Below Tc, B2g(o1) evolves into Ag(m2). The caption states that error bars are "smaller than the symbols" even for the ×50-scaled 4L/Au spectra, which presumably reflects statistical fit errors only. The authors should state whether a background was included, how Ag(o1)* and Ag(m1) were handled, which parameters were free, and the fit range at each temperature. They should give uncertainties that include systematic variation with the fit window and background. The 17% reduction in the maximum of -1/q, on which concern 2 rests, should be compared with 4L/Al2O3 (about 0.44) rather than with 18L/Au.

10. The 4L/Al2O3 result is described inconsistently and its 15% Tc reduction is not explained (abstract; pp. 13, 15). The abstract says 4L/Al2O3 shows "nearly bulk-like properties", and the Discussion says there is an "absence of any notable interface effect" from Al2O3 and that the sample "serves as a control to exclude a degradation effect". Yet Tc drops to 280-287 K, a 40 K (15%) reduction, larger than the reduction of 9% or less reported for 5L on SiO2 (ref. 45). The authors should explain the 40 K reduction, compare it concretely with ref. 45, and align the wording of the abstract and Discussion.
