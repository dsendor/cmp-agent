A. Must address

1. The superconducting volume fraction is never measured, so the claims of "bulk", "spatially uniform" and "optimal 3D" superconductivity are untested, and so is the picture in which superconductivity lives in the non-magnetic regions. The data also conflict with the paper's own percolation argument. The in situ ac susceptibility in Fig. 2b appears to be normalised to −1 at every stress. Full diamagnetic shielding measures screening by a connected path, which a thin percolating network or a surface shell can provide; it is not a volume fraction. At 0.09 GPa, V_M at base temperature is about 0.84 (Figs. 2d, 4b), so only about 16% of the sample is non-magnetic, and V_M is still about 0.75 just below Tc,mid. Yet χ_AC already shows a sharp, complete transition with Tc,mid ≈ 27–28 K (Fig. 2b), roughly 80% of the final enhancement. The text (p. 7) says that a non-magnetic fraction of about 20% at 0.1 GPa "is likely insufficient for percolation", and that Tc saturates only once 1 − V_M approaches 40%. That does not match the 0.09 GPa data. As it stands the data cannot distinguish (a) superconductivity confined to the non-magnetic regions (the authors' picture), (b) superconductivity coexisting microscopically with static stripe order, and (c) filamentary superconductivity. Cases (a) and (b) lead to opposite conclusions about competition, and (b) and (c) both undercut the picture of uniform d-wave patches in the non-magnetic regions. The authors should report the absolute (unnormalised) shielding signal, or the normalisation procedure and raw signal, at each stress. They should measure the superfluid density and superconducting volume fraction with field-cooled TF-μSR above Hc1 (or the field-cooled diamagnetic shift) at several stresses, for example 0, 0.09 and 0.23 GPa, which their cell and beamline already allow, and test whether 1/λ² tracks 1 − V_M. They should also rewrite the percolation argument so that it is consistent with the 0.09 GPa data.

2. The zero-stress and finite-stress susceptibility curves in Fig. 2b were measured in different geometries and under different conditions, so the "elevenfold enhancement of 3D superconductivity" is not established (Section II, Fig. 2b, Methods A). The text says the ambient curve was measured ex situ, before mounting, in a dc field of 0.5 mT parallel to the CuO2 planes, "such that the induced shielding currents flow between the layers", and that this is what makes it sensitive to 3D superconductivity. The in situ ac excitation under stress is "predominantly along the c-axis". That drives in-plane shielding currents, which 2D superconductivity can carry, and the authors' own resistivity puts 2D superconductivity at about 20–25 K at zero stress (Figs. 3e, 4a). Part of the jump from Tc,mid ≈ 3 K at 0 GPa to about 28 K at 0.09 GPa may therefore come from switching from an interlayer-sensitive to an in-plane-sensitive measurement, or from mounting strain, rather than from restored 3D coherence. The same problem affects the "3D SC" region of Fig. 4a and the "Tc,3D" line of Fig. 1d. The authors should show in situ ac susceptibility at 0 GPa in the stress cell, with the same coil and excitation, after cooldown with the sample glued. They should specify the coil geometry and the field direction relative to the crystal axes, state which curve in Fig. 2b comes from which measurement, and give the normalisation (for example to the zero-stress low-temperature value with a demagnetisation correction). They should justify identifying the in situ Tc,mid with the 3D transition, for example by showing that the in situ midpoint at zero stress matches the dc H ∥ ab value of about 3 K. Ideally they should also measure with the field in the plane under stress, or measure c-axis resistivity under stress.

3. The stress in the μSR/susceptibility cell is unlikely to be uniform, and a distribution of local stress would by itself produce the partial loss of magnetic volume that the paper interprets as intrinsic phase coexistence. The crystal is a 7 × 5.1 × 0.3 mm³ plate glued with Stycast to a Ti holder, with only a 4 × 5.1 mm² area (about 4 mm free length) exposed between the jaws (Fig. 2a, Methods A). The free length is shorter than the width and the plate is thin. In this geometry the stress near the epoxy-bonded ends, at the free edges and in the centre is expected to differ strongly, and gradients across the thickness are likely; a 0.3 mm plate under compression can also bend or buckle. The quoted stress is force over the nominal 1.53 mm² cross-section (480 N / 1.53 mm² = 0.32 GPa), using a force-sensor calibration (1 μV ≈ 30 N) taken from earlier work on different samples (Refs. 6, 41), with no stated uncertainty; no stress value anywhere has an error bar. A spread of local stress would give regions that lose static order and others that keep it with nearly unchanged T_so and B_int (Figs. 2d, 4c), which is the central μSR observation. It would also explain why the V_M(T) curves at 0.16–0.23 GPa do not saturate at low temperature and have long tails up to about 35–40 K (Fig. 2d), and it could account for part of the apparent difference between the stress scales of the μSR cell and the FC100 cell. If so, the factor-2.5 reduction of V_M, the coexistence of LTT and LTLO domains and the percolation argument (p. 7) would reflect regions at high and low stress rather than an intrinsic mixed static/dynamic stripe state. The authors should give a finite-element calculation of the stress and strain distribution for this exact sample, holder and epoxy geometry (including glue thickness), weighted by the area the muon beam sees through the hematite masks, and quote the fraction of the illuminated volume within, say, 20% of the nominal stress. They should check for bending, estimate the stress uncertainty, and discuss whether the fractional V_M could reflect a stress distribution rather than intrinsic phase coexistence. Ideally they should measure strain directly (strain gauge or digital image correlation) or check the result with a thinner, longer sample.

4. Suppression of the LTT phase at x = 0.125 and the formation of an "LTLO" phase are inferred only from the height of a resistive hump; no structural measurement under stress is presented. The abstract and conclusion state that stress suppresses the LTT phase and stabilises an LTLO phase, and that "suppression of the LTT state is essential" (p. 7); Fig. 1d labels the whole 0.23 GPa phase diagram "LTLO". The only evidence for x = 0.125 is the size of the normal-state resistivity peak (Figs. 3b, 3e). This is not a clean structural measure, for several reasons. Near 1/8 in LBCO the LTT transition coincides with charge-stripe ordering, so a smaller hump is equally consistent with weaker charge-order pinning or a smaller charge-ordered volume in a structure that is still LTT, with a shift of T_LTT, or with a change of transport anisotropy under stress. The peak in Fig. 3b sits at about 35–40 K, well below T_LTT (about 55 K), and its height is set both by the resistive upturn below T_LTT and by the superconducting-fluctuation downturn; since stress raises the superconducting onset to about 46 K, the hump is cut off by superconductivity and ΔR_LTT (Figs. 3d–f, 4b) would fall even if the LTT fraction were unchanged. The paper does not show whether the kink at T_LTT itself shifts or vanishes. There is also a tension in the data: Tc,mid saturates near 35 K by about 0.15–0.23 GPa (Figs. 2b, 4a), while the resistive peak at x = 0.125 is still 50–70% of its ambient value at those stresses and vanishes only near 0.5 GPa (Fig. 3e), which argues against bulk 3D superconductivity requiring full LTT suppression. The authors should provide direct structural evidence at x = 0.125 under comparable stress, for example the LTT-specific (100)/(010) superlattice reflections by X-ray or neutron diffraction, or compare quantitatively with the stress-dependent XRD at x = 1/8 in Ref. 30 (and their own diffraction at x = 0.135). At a minimum they should track the T_LTT anomaly in dR/dT against stress, discuss explicitly why Tc,mid saturates while the hump is still large, and otherwise phrase the conclusion in terms of the resistive anomaly rather than a structural phase.

5. Data from two different stress apparatuses, and apparently different crystals, are put on one stress axis without cross-calibration, and the paper describes the sample basis inconsistently. The Introduction (pp. 2–3) says μSR, ac susceptibility and resistivity were "performed on the same crystal under uniaxial stress". The Methods describe μSR and susceptibility on a 7 × 5.1 × 0.3 mm³ crystal in a piezoelectric cell with stress from a force-sensor calibration, and resistivity on separately mounted crystals, of unstated dimensions, in a Razorbill FC100 in a PPMS. Fig. 4a overlays T_so and Tc,mid from the μSR cell with Tc,zero from the FC100, and Fig. 4b plots V_M and the LTT peak height on a common σ axis and calls them "directly correlated". Nominal stress (force over cross-section) can differ substantially from the stress the sample carries, because of epoxy compliance, different free lengths and aspect ratios, and differential thermal contraction between Ti, epoxy and sample. The apparent tracking in Fig. 4b, and the convergence of Tc,zero with Tc,mid at about 0.15 GPa in Fig. 4a, may therefore partly reflect calibration rather than physics. The authors should state unambiguously which crystal was measured in which cell, with dimensions and growth batches; give the resistivity-sample dimensions, epoxy thickness and free length, and the stress uncertainty for those samples; and provide a common reference for the two stress scales, for example Tc,mid, the resistive Tc or the resistive hump measured on one crystal in both cells, or a direct strain measurement (strain gauge, capacitive displacement or X-ray lattice parameter) in each. They should include an estimate of stress-transmission efficiency, for example from finite-element modelling, and justify plotting the two data sets on one axis.

6. The title's claim that x = 1/8 "hosts optimal superconductivity", and the statement that stripe-related interactions enhance pairing, rest on a doping comparison made at mismatched stresses, with mixed probes, on one crystal per doping. In resistivity, x = 0.125 was taken to 0.56–0.58 GPa, but x = 0.115 and 0.135 only to 0.23 and 0.29 GPa (Figs. 3d, 3f). At matched stress (about 0.2–0.3 GPa), Fig. 3 gives Tc,zero of about 33–34 K for x = 0.125 against about 31 K (x = 0.115) and 30 K (x = 0.135), a margin of 2–3 K, comparable to plausible sample-to-sample and mounting spread, and the x = 0.125 curve is still rising. For susceptibility, Tc,mid ≈ 35 K for x = 0.125 here is set against about 30 K for x = 0.115 and 0.135 from earlier papers (Refs. 6, 19), on different crystals, possibly different growth batches, and with a different stress calibration. Even without stripe-related physics, the underlying LBCO Tc dome would be expected to peak near x ≈ 0.125–0.15. The authors should extend the x = 0.115 and 0.135 resistivity to 0.5–0.6 GPa in the same cell, or compare all dopings at matched stress with the same Tc definition; base the doping comparison on bulk (susceptibility) Tc; measure at least one further sample per doping and give error bars that reflect sample-to-sample variation; and present the pairing-enhancement interpretation as a hypothesis.

B. Should address

7. The extraction of V_M from weak-TF μSR in the presence of a large cell background is not documented, although the factor-2.5 drop (0.97 → 0.38) is a primary result. Only about 50% of muons stop in the sample (Methods A). The rest stop in the hematite masks, which depolarise quickly and so look "magnetic" in wTF, or in the Ti holder and epoxy, which are non-magnetic and precess at the applied field. V_M = 1 − P_TF(0,T) (Eq. 5) is correct only after the background is subtracted, yet V_M reaches about 0.97 at 0 GPa, so a background must have been fixed in some way that is not described. Methods D says "baseline asymmetry shifts were corrected by allowing P_TF(t) to be adjusted for each temperature", which suggests a temperature-dependent free parameter that can move V_M. Any change of the background split or stopping fraction with stress (sample, mask or epoxy displacement, cell deformation, beam overlap) would appear as a change of V_M. The authors should give the fit model including the background term, the background fraction and how it was determined (for example from a run above T_so at each stress or with a dummy holder), whether it was fixed across temperature and stress, and which parameters in Eqs. (3)–(5) were free. They should show raw (not A0-normalised) wTF asymmetries above and below T_so at 0 and 0.23 GPa, check that V_M from the ZF fits (Eq. 3) agrees with the wTF values, and state whether the 0 GPa μSR point was measured in the cell after mounting.

8. The headline Tc numbers mix 2D and 3D, resistive and magnetic criteria, and the claim of "the highest bulk Tc reported in the 214 family" (p. 7, Fig. 4d) is not supported as stated. The abstract's "from 5 K to 37 K under 0.5 GPa" combines the ambient 3D susceptibility onset (the text gives Tc,mid = 3 K) with the in-plane zero-resistance temperature at 0.56 GPa, which the authors themselves describe as reflecting the 2D transition (p. 5). The highest bulk Tc,mid actually measured is about 35 K at about 0.23 GPa, below optimal LSCO, because susceptibility was not measured at higher stress. The 46 K "onset" at 0.56 GPa has no stated criterion; in Fig. 3b the downturn begins on the shoulder of the residual hump, and unstressed LBCO-1/8 already shows a resistive drop near 40 K from 2D correlations, so the onset depends on how the normal-state background is defined. Fig. 4d compares it with LSCO data from a single 1989 study (Ref. 32), with no matching of criterion or sample form, and the record claim ignores other 214 materials with Tc at or above about 40 K: oxygen-intercalated La2CuO4+δ (about 40–45 K) and compressively strained LSCO films (about 45–50 K). The authors should quote stress-matched values with stated criteria (3D Tc,mid from χ; Tc,zero and onset from ρ, each with its stress), keeping 3D and 2D values separate in the title, abstract and text; define the onset criterion (for example the intersection with an extrapolated normal-state background or a fixed percentage drop) and apply the same one to the LSCO reference data, stating whether those were single crystals or polycrystals; in Fig. 4d use consistent symbols for LBCO under stress and state which stress is plotted for each doping; measure χ_AC near 0.5 GPa if possible; and otherwise restrict "bulk" to susceptibility (and ideally superfluid-density) results, soften "highest in the 214 family", and cite the relevant literature.

10. The zero-stress reference states are uncertain and suggest residual mounting strain or a different criterion. In Figs. 3d–f the zero-stress Tc,zero is about 21 K (x = 0.115), about 23 K (x = 0.125) and about 20 K (x = 0.135), and Fig. 4a puts x = 0.125 at about 25 K. The 1/8 sample thus has the highest Tc,zero of the three at "0 GPa", contradicting its description as the lowest-Tc composition, and its value is well above the in-plane zero-resistance temperatures usually reported for unstressed LBCO-1/8 (about 10–16 K). For x = 0.115 and 0.135 the "Peak (%)" scale is normalised to a tensile point rather than to zero stress (Figs. 3d, 3f), and Fig. 3 shows small tensile values (−0.03, −0.01 GPa), which suggests the nominal zero is not strain-free. Differential thermal contraction between sample, epoxy and holder can apply stress at nominally zero force, and a different composition or resistance criterion would also shift the values; any of these affects the comparison along the stress axis. The authors should show ρ(T) for each crystal free-standing or before mounting, state the criterion for Tc,zero (threshold or noise floor), explain how zero stress was established after cooldown in each cell, and discuss the uncertainty in the stress zero.

11. Reproducibility, reversibility and the stress protocol are not documented. Each result appears to rest on one crystal per doping and per technique. There is no evidence that Tc, V_M or the resistive hump recover when stress is released, so plastic deformation, cracking (which could itself contribute to a resistive drop), or epoxy creep or slipping at 0.5–0.6 GPa cannot be ruled out. The paper does not say at which temperature the stress was changed (stress applied above T_LTT selects twin and LTLO domains differently from stress applied at base temperature) or whether the crystals were thermally cycled between stress points. The authors should state the protocol and the number of samples per doping, show at least one stress-release (return-to-zero) measurement after the highest stress with any hysteresis, and ideally reproduce the key x = 0.125 result on a second crystal.

12. Several interpretive statements go beyond what is measured. "Spin-stripe order remains long range" (abstract, p. 4): coherent μSR oscillations show a well-defined local-field distribution, not a correlation length, which requires neutron or X-ray diffraction under stress. The conversion of static stripes into "dynamically fluctuating" ones in the non-magnetic regions is inferred from the absence of signal ("invisible to μSR on its characteristic timescale"), which is equally consistent with the stripes simply melting. The claims that stress "enhances pairing", acts "primarily at the level of phase coherence" through restored interlayer Josephson coupling, and shifts the system from a "putative PDW-dominated regime" to a uniform d-wave state are not tested by any measurement of the pairing scale, gap, anisotropy or pairing symmetry. The title speaks of the "stripe state" and the introduction promises to track "the evolution of the charge stripe order", but charge order is not measured; the resistive hump is an indirect proxy. The authors should either add discriminating data, for example the stress dependence of the relaxation rate of the non-magnetic fraction or of λ_L/λ_T, LF-μSR as a sign of slow fluctuations, c-axis resistivity under stress, the ratio of resistive to susceptibility transitions (loss of the 2D/3D separation as a PDW signature) or a diffraction correlation length, or recast these points as hypotheses, remove "demonstrate" from the abstract, and separate clearly in the discussion what is measured from what is inferred.
