Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors vary the longitudinal width W_sc of the superconducting contacts in five co-fabricated symmetric Al/InAs planar Josephson junctions (W_sc = 0.3, 0.6, 0.9, 1.2 µm and an unetched "W_sc → ∞" device). They report that the critical current follows I_c^∞(1 − exp(−W_sc/ξ)) with ξ ≈ 586 nm, close to a dirty-limit estimate of 570 nm. They also find that the peak diode efficiency grows with W_sc, that the field B_η of maximum diode efficiency is linear in 1/W_sc and saturates for W_sc ≳ 2ξ, and that the field B_* of maximum I_c depends strongly on gate voltage. Using contact width as a control parameter to separate diode-effect mechanisms is a useful and fairly new idea in a field where several mechanisms usually coexist. In its present form, though, the paper is mostly a phenomenological survey. It rests on one device per width, and its mechanistic conclusions (orbital versus spin-orbit) stay qualitative.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- A systematic, single-chip variation of a geometric parameter (W_sc) that is rarely varied on purpose. All devices share the same heterostructure, junction length (80 nm) and transverse width (4 µm).
- A careful protocol to suppress artifacts from the out-of-plane field: the Fraunhofer extrema of I_c+ and I_c− are tracked separately over B_⊥ at each (B_∥, V_g) point (Eq. 1, Fig. 1d–f). Switching currents for both polarities are measured by sweeping out from zero bias, so retrapping hysteresis does not enter. There is a θ = 0 control in which η vanishes (Fig. 1h).
- A simple, testable phenomenological picture: the exponential suppression of I_c (Eq. 2) and flux threading the area W_sc·d (Eq. 4). The order-of-magnitude estimates in Eq. 6 are internally consistent (I checked the values 13 mT, 4 mT and 690 mT, and 0.8Δ).
- Raw data and analysis scripts are deposited on Zenodo.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. **There is one device per contact width, and device-to-device spread is never characterised.** Every trend in the paper (Fig. 3b, Fig. 4, Fig. 5b, Fig. 5c) is drawn from five junctions, one per W_sc. InAs/Al planar junctions on one chip commonly differ in I_c, R_n, transparency and diode efficiency by 10–30% from local variations in the semiconductor surface, the Al etch and the gate dielectric. The paper itself shows non-monotonic behaviour that may be this spread. In Fig. 5b, max η is about 10% at W_sc = 0.9 µm but only about 8.5% at 1.2 µm. In Fig. 3c the V_g = 0 values of B_* are non-monotonic (about 22, 9, 8, 7 and 13 mT). The conclusions that "both the critical current and the diode efficiency increase with contact width" and that B_η "saturates" therefore cannot be told apart from sample variation. The authors should do one of two things. Either measure at least a second junction for some widths (ideally all, or at least 0.3 µm and 1.2 µm) and show the spread as error bars, or, if no further devices exist, give normal-state resistances R_n and I_cR_n products for all five devices to show that the I_c trend is not a transparency or R_n trend. In that case they should also state clearly in the abstract and conclusions that each width is represented by one device.

2. **The quoted carrier density, and so the "agreement" between the fitted ξ and the dirty-limit ξ, is inconsistent within the paper.** The coherence-length section uses n = 6×10¹² cm⁻² and ℓ = 130 nm, giving v_F ≈ 3×10⁶ m/s, ξ_BCS ≈ 2.5 µm and ξ ≈ 570 nm. The Methods instead report n = 8.14×10¹¹ cm⁻² and ℓ_e = 147 nm from a van der Pauw sample. With the Methods density, v_F ≈ 1.1×10⁶ m/s, ξ_BCS ≈ 0.9 µm and ξ ≈ 370 nm, which no longer agrees with the fitted 586 nm. The paper does not say whether the higher density is the density under the Al, a gated value, or a different wafer. The headline interpretation, that I_c(W_sc) measures the induced coherence length of the ABS, rests on this quantitative agreement. Two further points weaken it. Δ is taken as the bulk BCS value of the Al film (1.764 k_B T_c), not the induced gap. The relevant mean free path is the one in the InAs under the Al, which is not measured. The authors should state which density and mean free path apply to the proximitized region under the contacts and how they were obtained, reconcile the two sets of numbers, and propagate the uncertainties in n, ℓ and Δ into a range for the predicted ξ.

3. **The exponential I_c(W_sc) suppression has untested alternative explanations, and the fit is anchored on a device of different geometry.**
   - (i) Inverse proximity effect. A 10 nm Al strip only 0.3 µm wide sits on a large proportion of normal InAs. Its gap and superfluid density may be suppressed more than in wide contacts, which would lower I_c for a reason other than ABS leakage past the contact edge.
   - (ii) Kinetic inductance and phase gradients in the narrow leads. The T-shaped geometry (Fig. 1a) feeds the current along 4 µm long, 10 nm thick strips. For W_sc = 0.3 µm that is about 13 squares of thin Al, whose kinetic inductance can be comparable to Φ₀/I_c. This makes the current non-uniform along the junction and lowers the measured I_c. It could also contribute to the asymmetries that make B_+ ≠ B_−. The authors note that kinetic inductance is "expected to be non-negligible" but do not estimate it.
   - (iii) The fit in Fig. 3b takes I_c^∞ from the W_sc → ∞ device. That device has no S–N interface near the junction and very different screening. Its data also needed separate smoothing (Supplementary Information). In the plot of 1/ln(I_c^∞/(I_c^∞ − I_c)) versus 1/W_sc it sits at the origin by construction, so in practice ξ is set by four points that all depend on that single normalisation.

   The authors should take three steps. First, estimate the sheet kinetic inductance of the 10 nm Al (from R_□ and T_c) and the phase drop along each lead at I_c. Second, report the induced gap of each device, for example from multiple Andreev reflection features or from the temperature at which I_c vanishes, to exclude a width-dependent gap. Third, show that the fitted ξ is robust when I_c^∞ is left free rather than fixed by the W_sc → ∞ device. Temperature dependence of I_c(W_sc) would strengthen the case considerably, because the ABS-leakage picture predicts how the suppression changes as ξ changes.

B. Should address

4. **The linear fit of B_η against 1/W_sc (Fig. 5c) does not appear to match Eq. 4, and the orbital interpretation is not discriminating.** Eq. 4 predicts B_η = Φ/(W_sc·d), a line through the origin. The fitted line in Fig. 5c appears to have an intercept of roughly 20–25 mT. With slope Φ₀ × 0.036 nm⁻¹ ≈ 75 mT·µm, a line through the origin would give about 250 mT at W_sc = 0.3 µm, not the plotted 270 mT. Because Φ and d enter only as a product, the fit cannot determine either one, and the quoted Φ/Φ₀ = 0.36–0.61 follows from the assumed d. The text also states that the expected signatures of an orbital mechanism, a sign change at Φ = Φ₀ and Φ₀-periodic oscillations, are not observed. It mentions the alternative finite-size resonance condition 2q(2W_sc + W_n) = (2n+1)π/2, which gives B_η ∝ 1/(2W_sc + W_n) rather than 1/W_sc. The authors should state the fit function and its free parameters, including any offset, and justify an offset physically if one is used. They should also fit both the 1/W_sc and 1/(2W_sc + W_n) forms, compare their quality, and say plainly which mechanisms the data can and cannot exclude. A numerical BdG or tight-binding calculation of the actual geometry, with finite W_sc, vector potential, Rashba SOC and Zeeman terms, would make the mechanistic discussion quantitative rather than suggestive.

5. **The gate dependence of B_* does not isolate spin-orbit coupling.** By the authors' own Eq. 3, B_* increases both with increasing α (through k_so) and with decreasing transparency τ. E_T, k_F and possibly g* also change with V_g. The statement that the gate dependence "lends credence to a spin-orbit coupling based interpretation" is therefore not established by Figs. 3c and 4. The authors should provide independent gate calibrations on the same chip or wafer: R_n(V_g) and an estimate of τ(V_g), for example from excess current or I_cR_n, and α(V_g) from weak antilocalization (the group's ref. 46 suggests such data exist). They should then compare the measured B_*(V_g) quantitatively with Eq. 3 for at least one device.

6. **The "V_g < 0" points are taken at different gate voltages for each device, and the gate lever arm may depend on W_sc.** In Fig. 4 the negative-gate points lie between about −4 V and −3 V depending on the device, and Figs. 3c and 5c merge these into a single "V_g < 0" series. The Al contacts screen the gate, so the effective density change in the junction for a given V_g may depend on W_sc. Comparing B_* and B_η across widths at nominally equal gate voltage could then compare different densities. The authors should give the actual V_g for each point in Figs. 3c and 5c, explain why the values differ (for example, pinch-off), and present the comparison against a gate-independent quantity such as R_n or the Hall density, if available.

7. **The sign and W_sc dependence of B_* are not explained.** Eq. 3 contains only positive quantities, yet Fig. 3c and Fig. 4 show negative B_* (down to about −5 mT) at negative gate voltage. The text says Eq. 3 is derived for W_sc → ∞ and declines to compare across widths. However, the observed W_sc dependence of B_* is one of the paper's stated results, called "nontrivial" without further comment. The authors should explain what a negative B_* means physically, given the definition B_* = argmax I_c+ = −argmin I_c−. They should also discuss whether the W_sc dependence of B_* is consistent with the same finite-size and orbital physics invoked for B_η, or whether it may reflect self-field or residual out-of-plane effects that scale with lead width.

8. **The θ = 0 null control and the field-alignment error are shown for one device only.** Fig. 1h shows η ≈ 0 for B_∥ parallel to the current, for a "representative device" whose W_sc is not stated. Flux focusing, the misalignment-induced B_⊥ and screening all depend on the lead geometry. The control matters most for the W_sc → ∞ device and for the narrowest device, where B_η reaches about 270 mT. There a 2° misalignment produces about 9 mT out of plane, many Fraunhofer periods. The authors should state which device is shown in Fig. 1h, provide θ = 0 traces for all widths (in the SI if needed), and show how the out-of-plane offset field that tracks the central lobe varies with B_∥ for each W_sc. This would confirm that the B_⊥ tracking works equally well in all devices.

9. **Error bars do not include switching-current stochasticity, and the smoothing of the W_sc → ∞ data is not quantified.** In Methods, the uncertainty of I_c± is the RMS residual of a local fit to the Fraunhofer lobe, and η errors are propagated "assuming zero covariance". Switching currents are stochastic. Diode efficiencies of a few percent need repeated switching events to establish their uncertainty. The I_c+ and I_c− values also come from the same sweep, so their errors may be correlated. The authors should state how many switching events or sweeps enter each point, report the width of the switching distribution, and show that the smoothing applied to the W_sc → ∞ device does not shift its extrema or bias max η (16.5%, the largest value in Fig. 5b).

10. **There is no temperature or out-of-plane-field dependence to support the coherence-length interpretation.** All data are taken at a base temperature of about 30 mK. Since ξ ∝ √(ħD/Δ) and the induced gap changes with temperature, measuring I_c(W_sc), and ideally B_η(W_sc), at two or three temperatures would test whether the length scale extracted from Fig. 3b behaves like a coherence length. The B_⊥ period of the Fraunhofer pattern versus W_sc, which the authors already have, would independently test the effective-area assumptions behind Eq. 4 (flux focusing scales with lead width). The authors should report the Fraunhofer periods for all five devices.

C. Minor

11. The device in Fig. 1 is called "representative", but its W_sc is not given. Please state it, so that Figs. 1d–h can be compared with Figs. 3a and 5a.
12. The definition of I_c(W_sc) is unclear. Section 2.2 uses the maximum over B_∥ (max I_c+(B_∥)), while the Fig. 3b axis label reads "max|I_c±|". Please state explicitly whether the B_∥ = 0 value or the B_* value is used, and that the positive and negative branches are averaged.
13. Fig. 5b has no error bars, although the Fig. 5 caption describes the η uncertainty. Please add them, since the non-monotonic 0.9 µm and 1.2 µm points are central to concern 1.
14. Fig. 1d–f use a broken current axis, and the white crosses are hard to read against the colour scale. A one-dimensional inset of I_c±(B_⊥) with its local fit would show the extraction procedure more clearly.
15. The reference list has many formatting problems: missing spaces ("inJosephson", "AndreevMolecules"), stray "$\pi$" in ref. 12, and "type: article" in ref. 1. Several arXiv-only citations (refs. 7, 9, 12, 13) have since been published.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a second device at the two extreme finite widths (0.3 µm and 1.2 µm), together with R_n and I_cR_n for all junctions, so that the trends with W_sc can be separated from device-to-device variation.
2. Reconcile the carrier densities and mean free paths in the main text and the Methods, and recompute the dirty-limit ξ with a stated uncertainty, using the induced gap rather than the bulk Al gap.
3. Run a numerical simulation of the real geometry (finite W_sc leads, 80 nm junction, Rashba SOC, Zeeman and orbital vector potential) to predict I_c(W_sc), B_η(W_sc) and B_*(V_g). This would turn the qualitative mechanism discussion into a quantitative comparison.
4. Use independent gate calibrations (Hall density, weak antilocalization α, junction transparency) to separate the α and τ contributions to B_*(V_g) in Eq. 3.
