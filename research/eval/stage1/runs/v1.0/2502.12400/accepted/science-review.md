A. Must address

1. One device per contact width; device-to-device spread is never characterised. Each W_sc (0.3, 0.6, 0.9, 1.2 µm and the unetched W_sc → ∞ device) is represented by a single junction, so every trend in the paper (Fig. 3a, 3b, Fig. 4, Fig. 5a–c) rests on five junctions with no spread shown. InAs/Al planar junctions on one chip commonly differ in I_c, R_n, transparency and diode efficiency by 10–30%. Sources include the actual junction length W_n (an 80 nm gap set by wet etching, to which I_c is very sensitive), interface transparency, local density, Al etch undercut or damage, the semiconductor surface and the gate dielectric. The data already show non-monotonic behaviour that may be this spread. In Fig. 5b, max η is about 10% at W_sc = 0.9 µm but about 8.5% at 1.2 µm, and the text concedes it "does not follow a clear exponential trend". In Fig. 3c, the V_g = 0 values of B_* are also non-monotonic (about 22, 9, 8, 7 and 13 mT). Yet the abstract states that "both the critical current and the diode efficiency increase with contact width" and that B_η "saturates". The Methods mention several cooldowns but do not say whether I_c, max η, B_η and B_* were reproduced across them. The authors should measure at least a second junction for some widths, ideally the extremes 0.3 µm and 1.2 µm, or show that the same device gives the same values after a thermal cycle, and show the spread as error bars. They should report R_n, I_cR_n, and the excess current or another transparency measure for every device, together with the measured W_n of each, so that the W_sc trend can be separated from variation in transparency or W_n. They should state whether the key data come from one cooldown, and add error bars to Fig. 5b. If no further devices exist, they should state in the abstract and conclusions that each width is represented by one device, and qualify the claim about η unless the trend can be shown to exceed device-to-device scatter.

2. The agreement between the fitted ξ = 586 nm and the dirty-limit coherence length rests on parameters that the paper gives inconsistently. The coherence-length section (pages 2–3) uses n = 6×10^12 cm^−2 and ℓ = 130 nm "from magnetotransport data", giving v_F ≈ 3×10^6 m/s, ξ_BCS ≈ 2.5 µm and √(ξ_BCS ℓ) ≈ 570 nm. The Methods report n = 8.14×10^11 cm^−2, ℓ_e = 147 nm and mobility 9.86×10^3 cm²/Vs from van der Pauw measurements on the same wafer. Those values give v_F ≈ 1.1×10^6 m/s, ξ_BCS ≈ 0.9 µm and √(ξ_BCS ℓ) ≈ 370 nm, well below the fitted 586 nm. A density of 6×10^12 cm^−2 and v_F = 3×10^6 m/s are also unusually high for an InAs 2DEG. The paper does not say whether the higher density is the density under the Al, a gated value, or from a different sample. Further, the estimate uses the bulk Al gap (Δ = 1.764 k_B T_c ≈ 250 µeV) rather than the smaller induced gap Δ* in the proximitised 2DEG, which changes ξ substantially. It is also not stated which medium's coherence length is relevant: the proximitised InAs under the Al, whose mean free path is not measured, or the 10 nm Al film itself, which would be much shorter. Because the quantitative match is the main evidence that I_c(W_sc) reflects the Andreev-bound-state coherence length, a headline conclusion, the authors should state which density, mean free path and gap apply under the Al and where each comes from (for example gated magnetotransport, or tunnelling or MAR spectroscopy of Δ*). They should correct or explain the order-of-magnitude difference in n, and propagate the uncertainties in n, ℓ and Δ into a range for the predicted ξ.

3. Kinetic inductance and current injection along the narrow Al strips are an untested alternative explanation for I_c(W_sc), and possibly for B_η(W_sc). The contacts are 10 nm thick, 4 µm long strips of width W_sc fed from a T-shaped stem (Fig. 1a). Such a film has a sheet kinetic inductance of order 10 pH/□. For W_sc = 0.3 µm (about 13 squares along the junction) this gives a phase drop along the junction of order a radian at I ≈ 2 µA, comparable to Φ₀/I_c; for 1.2 µm it is about four times smaller. A phase gradient along the junction makes the current non-uniform, lowers the measured I_c and shifts the Fraunhofer pattern. The authors themselves attribute B_⊥+ ≠ B_⊥− to "the magnetic field induced by the slightly asymmetric critical current density". The gradient scales as 1/W_sc, so it could mimic the "coherence-length" suppression of I_c in Eq. 2 and contribute to the 1/W_sc scaling of B_η. The paper says kinetic inductance is "expected to be non-negligible" but mentions it only in passing, in the discussion of the single-mode estimates, and does not estimate it. The authors should report the normal-state sheet resistance of the Al and, from it and T_c, estimate the kinetic phase drop along each lead at I_c. They should state whether the two contacts are fed from the same end or opposite ends of the junction. They should use the Fraunhofer shapes already measured (lobe periods, lifting of minima, the B_⊥+/B_⊥− offset as a function of W_sc) to bound the current non-uniformity, and compare Eq. 2 with this alternative functional form.

4. A width-dependent induced gap, or edge damage, could also produce the I_c(W_sc) suppression and is not excluded. A 10 nm Al strip only 0.3 µm wide sits on a large proportion of normal InAs. Inverse proximity or finite-size effects may suppress its gap and superfluid density more than in wide contacts, lowering I_c for a reason other than ABS leakage past the distal Al edge. Damage at the etched Al edge, which is a larger fraction of a narrow strip, could act similarly. Eq. 2 is a one-parameter model, so these alternatives cannot be distinguished by the fit alone. The authors should report the induced gap of each device, for example from MAR features or from the temperature at which I_c vanishes, to exclude a width-dependent gap, and discuss edge damage. A comparison with a microscopic calculation, such as a BdG or tight-binding model of the T geometry with a bare 2DEG beyond the contacts, would strengthen the link between the fitted length and ξ.

B. Should address

5. The 1/W_sc scaling of B_η does not single out the orbital mechanism, and no quantitative estimate matches the data. The paper notes that any finite-momentum mechanism has zeroes of η set by the resonance condition 2q(2W_sc + W_n) = (2n + 1)π/2. Each of q_M, q_orb and q_RZ is linear in B_∥, so all three predict B_η ∝ 1/(2W_sc + W_n). The single-mode estimates (about 13 mT Meissner, about 4 mT orbital, about 690 mT Rashba–Zeeman) bracket but do not match the observed 90–270 mT. The orbital finite-q estimate in Eq. 6 has no W_sc dependence at all, yet the W_sc dependence in Fig. 5c is attributed to orbital flux. The expected orbital signatures, a sign change at Φ = Φ₀ and Φ₀-periodic oscillations of η, are not observed. The sign changes of η at 400–600 mT correspond to several Φ₀ rather than one for every device; for example W_sc = 0.9 µm with d = 17 nm gives about 3.7 Φ₀ at 0.5 T. The Conclusion's statements that the data "invite an orbital interpretation" or are "consistent with an orbital mechanism" are therefore not supported over the alternatives. The authors should fit both the 1/W_sc form (Eq. 4) and the resonance form 1/(2W_sc + W_n), compare their quality, and say plainly which mechanisms the data can and cannot exclude. Better, they should compare quantitatively with a multimode BdG or tight-binding calculation of the actual geometry (finite W_sc, W_n = 80 nm, 4 µm width) that includes Rashba, Zeeman and orbital vector-potential terms and reproduces both the scale and the slope of B_η. Otherwise they should state that the mechanism setting B_η is not identified and soften the mechanistic language in the abstract and Conclusion.

6. The linear fit in Fig. 5c is not Eq. 4, and the fitted quantities are not independent. Eq. 4, B_η = Φ/(W_sc d), is a line through the origin in B_η versus 1/W_sc. The plotted line has slope ≈ 74–75 mT·µm, matching the legend "Φ/Φ₀ = 0.036 nm⁻¹ × d", but an apparent intercept of about 20–30 mT at 1/W_sc = 0. A line through the origin with that slope would give about 250 mT at W_sc = 0.3 µm, not the plotted 270 mT. Because Φ and d enter only as a product, the quoted Φ/Φ₀ ≈ 0.36–0.61 simply restates the assumed d = 10–17 nm and is not an independent test of Φ < Φ₀. The flux area W_sc·d is also not justified; the geometry of Fig. 2 could equally suggest (2W_sc + W_n)·d or a ξ-limited width. The authors should state the fit function and its free parameters, including any offset, explain a finite intercept physically (it may point to the resonance form of concern 5), and show the fit parameters with uncertainties, a fit through the origin, and residuals. They should justify the choice of flux area. Reporting the B_⊥ Fraunhofer period for all five devices, which they already have, would independently test the effective-area assumption behind Eq. 4, since flux focusing scales with lead width.

8. The extraction of ξ from I_c(W_sc) in Fig. 3b needs more transparency and an uncertainty. The ordinate 1/ln[I_c^∞/(I_c^∞ − max I_c)] equals ξ/W_sc only if I_c^∞ is known. 9. The gate dependence of B_* is attributed to Rashba α and transparency τ, but neither is measured. The right-hand axis of Fig. 4 asserts that α increases and τ decreases with V_g. The α trend is cited from ref. 46, which used a different sample, and the τ trend is not supported by data. By Eq. 3, B_* rises both with α (through k_so) and with falling τ, and k_F, E_T and possibly g* also change with V_g; V_g also strongly changes I_c (Fig. 1g). Since B_* ∝ k_so/k_F = αm*/(ħ²k_F), it falls as density rises unless α rises faster. The statement that the gate dependence "lends credence to a spin-orbit coupling based interpretation" is therefore not established by Figs. 3c and 4. The authors should provide independent calibrations on the same chip or wafer: gate-dependent density, and τ(V_g) or E_T for each device (from R_n, I_cR_n, excess current or MAR), and α(V_g) from weak antilocalisation on a Hall bar with the same gate stack. They should compare B_*(V_g) quantitatively with Eq. 3 for at least one device, and write out in the supplement the fit to "Eq. (1) of ref. 8" that yields B_*, with its free and fixed parameters.

10. The sign and W_sc dependence of B_* are not explained. Eq. 3 contains only positive quantities, yet Figs. 3c and 4 show negative B_* at negative gate voltage: about −5 mT for 1.2 µm and about −2 mT for 0.9 µm. Given the definition B_* = argmax I_c+ = −argmin I_c−, the physical meaning of a negative value needs explaining; it may be a sign reversal like that of ref. 8. The text says Eq. 3 holds only for W_sc → ∞ and declines to compare across widths, yet the W_sc dependence of B_* is one of the stated results, called "nontrivial" without comment. At V_g = 0 it is non-monotonic, with a minimum near 0.6 µm and a rise at 0.3 µm. The authors should discuss the negative values and whether the W_sc dependence of B_* reflects the same finite-size and orbital physics invoked for B_η, or self-field or residual out-of-plane effects that scale with lead width.

11. Gate voltages are not matched across devices, and the gate's effect may depend on W_sc. The "V_g < 0" series in Figs. 3c, 4 and 5c mixes different voltages for each device (roughly −4, −3.5 and −3 V in Fig. 4), and the 0.3 µm device has no V_g = +10 V point. Because I_c pinches off near −5 V (Fig. 1g), small offsets in V_g correspond to large changes in density near depletion. The Al contacts also screen the gate, so the lever arm may depend on W_sc. If the gate acts on the bare 2DEG beyond the distal edge of the Al strips, it also tunes the Andreev reflection that sets the W_sc dependence. Comparing devices at mismatched or nominally equal V_g may therefore compare different densities, weakening the conclusion that B_* depends on both W_sc and V_g. The authors should list the exact V_g for each point, explain why the values differ and why the 0.3 µm device lacks +10 V data, state which regions the gate covers, and present the comparison against a gate-independent quantity such as density, R_n or normalised I_c(V_g)/I_c(0).

12. Max η is compared at very different fields, which confounds its W_sc dependence. η peaks at about 90 mT for wide contacts and about 270 mT for W_sc = 0.3 µm. At higher B_∥, Zeeman and orbital pair breaking suppress the induced gap and I_c (Fig. 3a), so the smaller max η of narrow devices may reflect where the peak sits on the field axis rather than an intrinsic geometric reduction of nonreciprocity. The authors should compare η at fixed B_∥, as a function of B_∥W_sc, or after normalising by I_c(B_∥)/I_c(0), and discuss whether the W_sc dependence of max η in Fig. 5b survives.

13. Error bars do not capture switching-current stochasticity. I_c± are single switching events from a dc ramp, and their uncertainty is the RMS residual of a local fit around the Fraunhofer extremum, propagated to η "assuming zero covariance" (Methods). This does not capture switching stochasticity, premature switching, sweep-to-sweep reproducibility or drift, and asymmetric switching histograms can produce or mask a few-percent η. I_c+ and I_c− come from the same sweep and fitting procedure, so their errors are likely correlated, and zero covariance may misstate the error on η. The authors should state how many sweeps or switching events enter each point, report the switching-distribution width at a representative field, give the ramp rate and filtering, and check that η is unchanged when the median of repeated switching events is used.
