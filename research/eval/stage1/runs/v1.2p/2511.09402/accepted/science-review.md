**A. Must address**

1. **Uniaxial strain or another extrinsic symmetry lowering is not excluded as the source of the "chiral-nematic" signal.** The distortion used to describe the data, k_F(θ) = k_F0(θ) + η cos(2θ + φ) with φ = −π/6 (Fig. 2e; p. 8), is a two-fold (l = 2, nematic) deformation whose principal axis lies 15° away from both the σv and σv' mirror lines. Any in-plane uniaxial or shear strain along a non-high-symmetry direction produces exactly this symmetry class: three unequal |q| values along A–H_I, A–H_II and A–H_III and a cyclic "handedness". Sources include cleave-induced strain, a step or terrace gradient, differential thermal contraction against the sample plate, and local strain around Ti clusters. The effect is small, 1.4–2.2% of |Q_Bragg| (Supplementary Table 1), so a modest strain acting on the large electronic nematic susceptibility reported for AV3Sb5 could account for it. The global affine transformation that forces the Bragg peaks onto a perfect hexagon (Methods; Figs. 2b, 4d) removes the lattice signature of a real strain from the images but not the electronic response to it; the analysis may therefore discard exactly the evidence that would identify strain. Domains of opposite handedness (±15° director, Fig. 2g) are also what strain varying on a ~100 nm scale would give, and the T-matrix argument (Supplementary Figs. 18–20) addresses defects but not strain. The authors should: report, for every map, the raw uncorrected Bragg vectors, the affine-correction parameters and the implied strain tensor (magnitude and principal-axis angle); show whether the sign and size of η and φ track the strain axis or are independent of it, including in the opposite-handedness regions of Fig. 2g; use DFT to estimate the strain needed to produce the observed η and compare it with the measured lattice distortion; and report φ for every domain and doping, since a director pinned at ±π/6 everywhere would favour an intrinsic order and a scattered φ would favour strain. Maps from several independently mounted crystals with the director angle reported for each, or a strain-tuning experiment, would make the intrinsic interpretation much stronger.

2. **The "parent state" claim and the onset T* ≈ 167 K are not measured.** The title and abstract present the chiral-nematic Fermi liquid as the parent of the chiral CDW and of superconductivity, emerging at T* above T_CDW, and Fig. 4f draws a flat T* line above T_CDW at all doping levels (with a related dashed curve in Fig. 1b). Yet all QPI is taken at 0.4 K, plus about 4 K for the superconducting comparison; Ti doping is used as a stand-in for temperature. T* is obtained by converting a mean δ-pocket energy splitting of 14.4 meV at x = 0.15 directly to a temperature (apparently E/k_B with no prefactor), and then compared with T_CDW ≈ 90 K of the undoped compound. The conversion assumes an unstated, unjustified ratio between a band-splitting scale and a transition temperature (mean-field ratios alone change it by a factor of order unity), it mixes compositions, and the splitting itself depends on how q-differences are converted to energy through the renormalized velocities. The resistivity shows no anomaly at T* (p. 8), and the 130 K torque anomaly (ref. 28) is on a different composition, is a bulk probe, and is numerically inconsistent with 167 K. Suppressing the CDW by doping does not show that the x = 0.15, 0.4 K state exists above T_CDW in the undoped compound. The authors should measure the q1/q2/q3 anisotropy at elevated temperatures at x = 0.15 (for example 20, 50 and 100 K, ideally up to and beyond where it vanishes), or measure x = 0 between T_CDW and the claimed T*, or use a bulk probe on the same crystals sensitive to rotation of the principal axes away from the mirrors (polarization-resolved optical birefringence, elastoresistance, polarized Raman in the Eg channels). They should give the formula and assumptions behind the 14.4 meV → 167 K conversion. If no data are added, the title, abstract, statements such as "already present above the CDW transition", the T* estimate, and the T* lines in Figs. 1b and 4f should be presented as hypotheses, with Fig. 4f labelled as schematic and the measured boundaries (Fig. 1b, ref. 29) distinguished from the conjectured ones.

**B. Should address**

3. **Measurement artifacts are not excluded at the level a percent-scale effect requires.** The distinction between "nematic" and "chiral-nematic" rests on peak-position differences of about 0.01–0.03 |Q_Bragg| (Figs. 2c, 3c, d), comparable to the plotted error bars, which include a pixel term ε_pix. For a 70–100 nm field of view one FT pixel is about 0.5–1% of |Q_Bragg|, so the signal is a few pixels. A global affine correction removes only linear distortion; nonlinear piezo creep, thermal drift along the slow-scan axis, fast/slow-scan asymmetry, residual non-orthogonality and spatially varying distortion within the field of view all survive it, and each has a fixed orientation relative to the scan frame, so it can mimic a handedness. Tip anisotropy (a multi-atom or asymmetric tip) can redistribute FT intensity between directions, shift apparent peak positions in line cuts, and distort the apparent tilt of the q3 triangles. The opposite-handedness region (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Fig. 1) is the key control, and the sign changes between compositions in Fig. 4g also argue against a fixed instrumental handedness, but Fig. 2g was recorded several hundred nanometres away at a different setpoint (1000 pA, 0.3 mV lock-in versus 500 pA, 0.5 mV), and the paper does not say whether the same tip, scan angle, session and field-of-view size were used. The authors should: image the same area at two or three scan-frame rotations (for example 0°, 60°/30° and 90°) and with opposite fast-scan directions, showing that φ stays locked to the lattice; show the distortion survives a deliberate tip change, and ideally show opposite handedness with the same tip; repeat the analysis with a local (Lawler–Fujita-type) drift correction; state the FOV, pixel number and q-pixel size for every map (including in the captions of Figs. 1, 2 and 4) and express the distortion in q-pixels; calibrate the residual distortion floor on a quantity that should be symmetric (for example the q1 ring, uncorrected Bragg-peak shapes, or a reference such as the Au(111) surface-state ring measured with the same scanner and pipeline); and ideally image a domain boundary between opposite handedness within one field of view, where tip and drift are common to both.

4. **The data do not yet show statistically that all mirrors are broken, and η and φ are imposed rather than fitted.** A nematic that keeps one mirror makes two of the three A–H_m directions equivalent, so the chiral claim requires that all three pairwise differences in q1, q2, q3, d12, d13, d23 and the velocities be significant. The line profiles in Fig. 2c, d carry no error bars; in Fig. 3c, d the error bars of the three directions overlap over much of the energy range. The phase φ = −π/6 appears to be chosen ("used for our QPI data analysis", p. 8), the value of η is not given, and the simulated QPI (Fig. 2f) is compared only qualitatively ("very well captured"), with no quantitative comparison of simulated and measured d12, d13, d23 or q_i(θ). The authors should give uncertainties for every q, test pairwise significance, fit η and φ directly to the extracted q(E) or to the full QPI map with confidence intervals (stating the number of data points and free parameters), compare simulated and measured q-vector positions along each A–H_m direction with residuals, show that φ is many standard deviations from the mirror-preserving values (φ = nπ/3; see concern 6), state whether a pure nematic (φ = 0) plus uniform strain or a C3-breaking term fits equally well, and show the same result in independent fields of view and samples.

5. **The causal link between the chiral-nematic QPI and the CDW chirality at small x is asserted, not demonstrated.** The abstract states that the residual chiral QPI in CDW samples demonstrates "that the CDW-triggered exotic states descend from the chiral-nematic Fermi liquid", and the 2.6 : 1.2 : 1.0 intensity ratio of the 2a0 × 2a0 CDW peaks at x = 0.05 (Fig. 4c) is cited as evidence of CDW handedness. Coexistence of the two does not establish parentage; one would expect the CDW handedness to be locked to the QPI handedness. FT peak intensities of 2a0 peaks in AV3Sb5 are known to depend on tip and setpoint. The authors should show, across several domains of each handedness at x = 0 or 0.05, that the sense of the CDW intensity ratio flips together with the QPI chirality; state how the intensities were obtained (integrated or peak value, background subtraction); and give the ratio's uncertainty across fields of view, tips and setpoints.

6. **The symmetry analysis of the order parameter is incomplete, and the Methods contain errors.** In a hexagonal lattice the Landau free energy of a single two-component E2g nematic order parameter contains an allowed cubic (Z3, ∝ cos 3α) term that pins the director to σv or σv', preserving one mirror. A director at an intermediate angle such as φ = −π/6 (principal axis at 15°, halfway between the mirror types) therefore needs a second, independent order parameter that breaks all vertical mirrors (A2g-like; the authors propose an intra-unit-cell sublattice charge order, Eqs. 2–5) or an external field such as strain (concern 1). The paper should state which is assumed, what it predicts, what sets the 15° offset, and whether the same φ is found in all dopings and domains (reported per dataset in Supplementary Table 1 and Figs. 3–5, 16). Separately, the Methods say mirror symmetry survives only for φ = 0, ±π, but cos(2θ + φ) with φ = ±π/3 or ±2π/3 is symmetric about a σv' or σv line; with Eqs. (3)–(5), φ = π/3 gives n1 = n3 ≠ n2, so mirror-preserving configurations occur at every φ = nπ/3. The statement that for φ = ±π/2 "the nematic component vanishes and the charge order becomes purely chiral" is inconsistent with n1 = n0 ≠ n2 ≠ n3, which breaks C3; in this parametrization any δn ≠ 0 is nematic and φ only sets the angle between director and mirrors. The authors should correct these statements and restate the claim as an E2g director at a non-symmetric angle together with whatever additional order parameter is required.
Raised by: r1, r3

7. **Structural or dopant-induced symmetry breaking is not excluded, and sample characterization is thin.** The proposed microscopic origin is unequal charge on the three V sublattices (Supplementary Fig. 10b), and Ti substitutes on exactly these sites. Preferential sublattice occupation, short-range Ti ordering, or Ti alignment along a step or growth direction would produce the same symmetry breaking structurally rather than electronically. The T-matrix simulations (Supplementary Figs. 18–20) address random point defects with "representative" strong-scattering parameters, not correlated occupancy, and the statement that no correlated defect population is visible (Supplementary Fig. 13) is qualitative. The Ti concentrations are nominal or cited (ref. 29). The authors should tabulate the sublattice assignment of Ti features in the topographs (for example Fig. 1d) for each field of view and test whether occupancy is equal across sublattices; quantify the Ti pair-correlation function and orientational distribution in the maps used for QPI; state the range of scattering strengths and sublattice choices explored in the T-matrix study; report whether diffraction on x = 0.15 crystals shows any monoclinic or orthorhombic distortion; give the measured composition, local Ti density per map, and transport or susceptibility confirming the absence of CDW and the Tc at x = 0.15; and show that the residual Cs adatom arrangement left after sweeping with the tip (Methods) has no preferred orientation correlated with φ.

8. **Sample statistics, reproducibility and domain structure are not reported.** The paper says the chirality is reproducible across doping, energy and termination (Discussion; Supplementary Figs. 3–7, 14, 16) but does not state how many crystals, cleaves, tips and fields of view were measured at each x, how many showed each handedness, whether any region showed no distortion, or how large the domains are. The domain picture rests on one other region "several hundred nanometers away" (Fig. 2g) recorded at a different setpoint, and on handedness changing sign between compositions (Fig. 4g). The authors should provide a table listing, for each map, x, sample, termination, tip, scan angle, setpoint, field of view, φ (with uncertainty) or the ordering of d12/d13/d23, and handedness. Large-area or multi-field mapping showing domain size and a domain wall, ideally showing that the director rotates by about 30° rather than an arbitrary angle, would substantially strengthen the claim of a spontaneous electronic order.

9. **The velocity and mass-renormalization analysis needs uncertainties, justification of its approximations, and reconciliation with the small distortion.** Velocities are quoted to six significant figures without uncertainties (for example v1,F = 4583.84, 5107.30, 4121.17 meV/|Q_Bragg| and v_DFT = 6074.72; Supplementary Table 2), yet the chirality argument relies on their orderings. The fit window "away from the kink" is not given, nor the sensitivity to it. The α-velocity spread is about 24% and the δ-pocket masses range from 1.45 to 2.33 (about 60%), far larger than would normally follow from a Pomeranchuk deformation of about 2% of |Q_Bragg|. The α-band ordering (v^II > v^I > v^III) has the opposite cyclic sense to the δ-band ordering (v^I > v^II > v^III), which is not discussed though both are offered as evidence of one handedness. For the δ pockets, q2 and q3 connect inequivalent pockets not related by inversion, so v_F ≈ 2v_s holds only "to leading order in the chiral-nematic distortion" (p. 6), yet the resulting direction dependence of m*/m (1.53, 1.95, 2.33) is of the same order as that distortion. The reference velocities come from VCA-DFT without spin–orbit coupling at fixed kz = π, and a Ti-induced chemical-potential shift moves the δ band relative to the van Hove point near L, changing v_DFT. The authors should give fit windows and uncertainties for all v_s and m*/m, test the significance of the inter-direction differences, round to meaningful precision, quote velocities in physical units (meV·Å), quantify the error of the v_F ≈ 2v_s approximation (for example by computing dq/dE from the distorted model Fermi surface of Fig. 2e), explain the opposite cyclic orderings, and discuss whether a single cos(2θ + φ) term can produce anisotropies of this size; if not, the velocity anisotropy should not be presented as independent confirmation of the same order parameter.

10. **The chiral-nematic superconducting-state conclusion is overstated.** The claim that superconductivity "emerges directly" into a chiral-nematic SC ground state with the same handedness rests on the qualitative resemblance of subgap Bogoliubov QPI to the normal-state pattern (Supplementary Figs. 8, 9, 17). That shows the quasiparticles that pair live on a distorted Fermi surface, as expected for any anisotropic band structure; it does not show that the superconducting order parameter breaks mirror symmetry or constrain the pairing symmetry. Chirality is said to be seen "both below (0.4 K) and above (4 K)" Tc = 3.5 K, but 4 K is barely above Tc. The gap at x = 0.15 is a fraction of a meV, so the lock-in modulation (0.3–0.5 mV in the main-text maps) and the 650 mK electron temperature should be reported for the B-QPI data and compared with the gap, to show the subgap maps are dominated by Bogoliubov quasiparticles. Convincing evidence would be a same-area, same-tip comparison of subgap B-QPI with normal-state QPI with superconductivity suppressed above Hc2 (the 11 T magnet is available), with a quantitative comparison of the q-vector misalignments; a gap anisotropy Δ(k) along mirror-related directions or a field- or temperature-dependent change in B-QPI distinct from normal-state QPI; or vortex-core shapes reflecting the same handedness. Otherwise the claim should read "the normal-state anisotropy persists into the superconducting state".

11. **Whether the effect is a surface or bulk property is not addressed.** STM probes the Sb- (and Cs-) terminated surface of a crystal cleaved at 13 K; surface reconstructions, incomplete Cs coverage and subsurface Ti layers can lower the local symmetry. A bulk "Fermi liquid" phase underlying the bulk CDW needs either a bulk probe at x = 0.15 (torque magnetometry, elastoresistance, polarization-resolved optics, or comparison with the bulk measurements of ref. 28 on doped samples) or an argument that the surface layer reflects bulk symmetry. The Cs-terminated data (Supplementary Fig. 14) help; the authors should state whether the handedness is the same on both terminations in the same region and discuss surface effects explicitly.

12. **"Chirality" is used in a sense that differs from the literature the paper links to, and is defined only late.** Only in the Discussion (p. 8) do the authors say "chirality" means breaking all vertical mirrors without time-reversal breaking. The proposed state preserves inversion (Methods), so with σh (untested) the point group is at most C2h, achiral in three dimensions; the handedness is a planar property of the in-plane director, for which a nematic director at a non-high-symmetry angle suffices. The abstract and introduction, however, connect it to the "chiral CDW" (refs. 19, 20, 24), whose handedness is different and tied to TRS breaking and field-switchable chirality, and to "chiral superconductivity", which readers will take to mean TRSB pairing. The authors should define the term in the abstract and introduction, state the full point group of the proposed state, and explain how it would connect to the 3D chirality and TRSB claimed for the CDW phase.
