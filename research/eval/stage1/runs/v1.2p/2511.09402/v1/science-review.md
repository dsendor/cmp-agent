A. Must address

1. Measurement and analysis artifacts are not excluded at the size of the effect. Where: Figs. 2a–d, 3a, 3c–d, 4d; Supplementary Table 1; Methods. The claimed chiral distortion is 1.4–2.2 % of |Q_Bragg|. For the roughly 80–90 nm field of view of Fig. 1d–g, one Fourier pixel (2π/L ≈ 0.08 nm⁻¹ against |Q_Bragg| ≈ 13 nm⁻¹) is about 0.5–0.6 % of |Q_Bragg|, so the effect is only 2–4 FT pixels. Piezo calibration error, creep, scanner non-orthogonality, nonlinear thermal drift over a multi-hour dI/dV map, layer-to-layer drift within the multi-energy data set of Fig. 3, and an asymmetric or multi-apex tip can all shift FT features anisotropically by about 1–3 %. The Methods apply a single affine transformation to the Bragg peaks "to restore a perfectly symmetric lattice", and state this only for Figs. 2b and 4d; it is not said whether the maps behind Fig. 2a/2c, Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 1 were corrected. An affine correction removes only uniform distortion, and once the Bragg peaks are forced to be symmetric they can no longer serve as an internal symmetry reference. In Fig. 3c the q1 data for the three directions differ near E = 0 by about 0.01 |Q_Bragg|, while the horizontal error bars are about 0.005 |Q_Bragg|, and no uncertainties are given for any q_i^m, d12, d13 or d23. The opposite-handedness region (Fig. 2g, Extended Fig. 1) argues against a fixed scanner distortion, but it was taken several hundred nanometres away at a different set point (1000 pA, 0.3 mV against 500 pA, 0.5 mV), and it is not said whether the tip or scan angle was the same, so it does not exclude a tip or scan-frame origin. Why it matters: every symmetry-breaking claim in the paper rests on these few-pixel differences. What to do: (i) state the field of view, pixel count and q-resolution of every map and express the effect in FT pixels; (ii) report the raw Bragg-peak positions, the affine-correction parameters and the residual Bragg-peak asymmetry for each data set, show the raw FT next to the symmetrized one, and compare forward and backward scans; (iii) give fit uncertainties for every q_i^m and d_ij and show that the three directional values differ beyond them; (iv) apply a full Lawler–Fujita drift correction rather than only an affine one; (v) repeat the same area with the fast-scan axis rotated (for example by 60° or 90°) and, separately, after a deliberate tip change, and show that the handedness follows the crystal and not the scan frame or the tip; (vi) state whether Fig. 2a and Fig. 2g used the same tip and scan angle, and ideally image a boundary between opposite handedness within one field of view with one tip; (vii) ideally, run the identical pipeline on a reference surface known to be C6/C3 symmetric (for example Au(111)) with the same scanner and show that no comparable "chiral" asymmetry appears.

2. The observed symmetry cannot be told apart from extrinsic strain, or from an ordinary nematic distortion with a director at a generic angle. Where: Fig. 2e–f, Fig. 2g, Discussion (cos(2θ+φ) model, Extended Fig. 6a), Methods (affine transformation). The model used, k_F(θ) = k_F⁰(θ) + η·cos(2θ+φ) with φ = π/2 (i.e. −η·sin 2θ), is a pure l = 2 two-fold distortion whose principal axes lie at 45° and 135° to the crystal direction, away from the mirror lines θ = nπ/6. Any C2-preserving perturbation along a generic in-plane direction has exactly this symmetry: it breaks all vertical mirrors, keeps inversion (point group C2h), gives three unequal q_i along the three A–H_m directions, and has two mirror-related "domains". A residual uniaxial strain from cleaving, from differential thermal contraction against the holder or epoxy, or from local Ti inhomogeneity does this without any electronic instability; a shear of order 0.5 % could plausibly move pocket boundaries by the reported 1.4–2.2 % of |Q_Bragg|. Opposite handedness a few hundred nanometres away also fits a director set by random local strain. The affine correction discards the lattice distortion that would reveal such strain. Why it matters: the headline claim is a spontaneous, intrinsic correlated (Pomeranchuk) state, and the symmetry argument alone cannot separate it from strain-pinned anisotropy. What to do: (i) extract the local strain tensor (magnitude and principal axis) from the uncorrected Bragg peaks or Lawler–Fujita displacement fields in every field of view, and estimate the residual strain that drift cannot account for; (ii) compare the strain axis with the fitted director and test for correlation; (iii) compile director angle and handedness over many fields of view, cleaves and mountings, including whether the local Bragg peaks differ in the opposite-handed domain; (iv) use DFT under strain (or the tight-binding model) to estimate what strain magnitude and angle would reproduce the observed q-vector distortions, and compare it with the measured strain; (v) explain physically and experimentally what distinguishes "chiral-nematic" from "nematic with a generic director"; (vi) if possible, apply deliberate uniaxial strain and show how the handedness responds.

3. The central claim that the chiral-nematic Fermi liquid is a parent state forming at T* ≈ 174 K above T_CDW is not measured. Where: Title, Abstract, Discussion ("T* ~ 174 K"), Fig. 4f. All QPI data are taken at 0.4 K, with a brief comparison at 4 K (Figs. S2–S3), far below any proposed T*. T* is obtained by converting an average energy splitting of about 15 meV (Supplementary Table 1) directly through E = k_B T*; no mean-field ratio or other relation is given that would let a zero-temperature band splitting set an ordering temperature. Fig. 4f draws a T* line that was not measured. A low-temperature anisotropy at x = 0.15 also does not show that the same order exists above T_CDW at x = 0. The torque result of Ref. 35 (about 130 K) gives context but is not evidence from this work. What to do: measure the temperature dependence of the QPI anisotropy at x = 0.15 (or 0.27), for example at 4, 20, 50, 100 K and above, track the fitted distortion η(T) (or the q_i^m differences) and show where it vanishes; if possible, probe x = 0 across T_CDW ≈ 94 K. Otherwise reduce the title, abstract and phase diagram to a low-temperature observation and describe T* as a speculative scale.

6. The phenomenological model is imposed rather than fitted, and has internal gaps. Where: Fig. 2e–f, Discussion, Extended Fig. 6. The value of η is never given, φ = π/2 is "used for our QPI data analysis" rather than fitted, and the agreement between Fig. 2f and Fig. 2a is judged by eye. The text writes η·sin(2θ) in one place and η·cos(2θ+φ) with φ = π/2 in another; these differ by a sign, so the handedness convention is ambiguous. One η is applied to both the α (Sb p) and δ (V d) pockets, although the proposed sublattice charge order would couple differently to the two orbitals. The model only shifts Fermi momenta by about 2 % of |Q_Bragg|, yet the extracted Fermi velocities differ by up to about 45 % between directions (v2,F = 1554 against 1074 meV/|Q_Bragg|), and no mechanism is given for that. In Landau theory for the hexagonal two-component E2g nematic order parameter, the cubic (three-state Potts) term pins the director to high-symmetry directions, so a director locked at a generic angle needs a microscopic reason or an explicit symmetry-breaking field; the same applies to three unequal V sublattice densities (Extended Fig. 6b). What to do: fit η and φ freely (per pocket or jointly) to the measured q_i^m(θ, E) in all three directions with uncertainties; show quantitatively that η = 0, a pure nematic (φ = 0 or a multiple of π/6) and a C6-symmetric model are rejected; use one sign convention; explain or acknowledge the velocity anisotropy; and address the Landau pinning argument.

7. The velocities and mass renormalizations are given without uncertainties, with spurious precision, and rest on untested assumptions. Where: Fig. 3c–d, pp. 7–9, Supplementary Table 2. Velocities are quoted to six significant figures (for example 4928.84, 5115.95, 4350.17 and 3037.36 meV/|Q_Bragg|) with no errors, yet the chirality conclusion (v^III < v^I < v^II, etc.) rests on 5–20 % differences, and the energy error bars and q scatter in Fig. 3c are comparable to the separation between directions. The linear-fit window is not given, and curvature outside it is attributed to an electron–phonon kink without analysis, though the window choice sets the slope. v_F = 2v_s is exact only for inversion-related scattering (q1); for q2 and q3 it assumes equal and antiparallel velocity projections at both end points, which the authors note fails in the distorted state, so the δ-band ratios m*/m_DFT = 1.63–2.35 carry an unknown systematic error. The claim that the δ-band renormalization "reinforces" symmetry breaking holds only if it has the same C2 pattern as the k_F distortion. The "Fermi liquid" label is asserted rather than tested. 8. The Bogoliubov QPI does not establish a "chiral-nematic superconducting ground state". Where: Abstract ("direct transition from the parent state to a chiral-nematic superconducting state"), Discussion (p. 11), Extended Fig. 5, Fig. S4. Subgap or near-gap B-QPI inherits the normal-state Fermi-surface geometry, so if the normal state is distorted the B-QPI will be too, whether or not the gap function breaks mirrors. The U-shaped gap gives no information on angular gap anisotropy. With a fully gapped spectrum the subgap QPI should be weak, and the origin of the subgap signal used in Extended Fig. 5b (residual normal carriers, impurity bound states, broadening) is not discussed; the electron temperature (650 mK) and the subgap energy relative to Δ are not compared. What to do: show a direction-dependent gap magnitude or coherence-peak position (energy- or phase-resolved B-QPI near Δ, or gap maps with a C2 pattern at a non-symmetric axis); otherwise restate the result as "the superconducting state forms out of / coexists with the distorted normal-state Fermi surface".
