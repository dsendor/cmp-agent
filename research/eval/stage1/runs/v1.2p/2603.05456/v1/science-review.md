A. Must address

1. The polarization dependence cannot tell magnetic-dipole emission from electric-type second-order emission, and no measurement discriminates between them. In point group 3̄ the in-plane axial vector (M_x, M_y) and the in-plane response to E⊗E both belong to E_g (Table S1). At the (0001) surface the symmetry drops to point group 3, where an in-plane polar vector (a surface rectification polarization or shift/photocurrent, J_x ∝ a(E_x² − E_y²) + b(2E_xE_y)) belongs to E as well. Surface electric-dipole rectification, bulk electric-quadrupole rectification, photon drag (proportional to the photon wavevector), anisotropic photocurrents, and electric-quadrupole radiation from the very intra-cell quadrupoles invoked in SI Fig. S6b therefore all give the same normal-incidence form S ∝ cos(2φ − 2δ), with δ material-dependent and not locked to a mirror because 3 has no mirrors. The fourfold |S| pattern with a non-universal δ (Figs. 2d,e; Eq. 3 and Eq. IV.64) is thus not specific to M_y. The two arguments used on p. 2 to exclude a surface contribution are not decisive: (i) "would not track magnetic order" is undercut by the paper's own data, since the pattern is fully present at 200 K (about three times T_c) with amplitude about 85% of the maximum (Figs. 2e,f), and a surface or bulk rectification can be temperature dependent through band gap, absorption and carrier lifetime, which change across T_c in Cr2Ge2Te6 through magneto-elastic and band effects; (ii) a sublinear, saturating fluence dependence is generic for photocurrent and rectification emitters through absorption saturation, band filling, carrier screening and heating (see concerns 9 and 10). As it stands the identification of the radiating channel rests on symmetry that does not distinguish the candidates. The authors should add measurements that do: emission versus applied in-plane and out-of-plane magnetic field, with field reversal, at 7 K and above T_c, using the magnet of the OptiCool cryostat they already used; emission in transmission versus reflection from a thin exfoliated flake (a sheet electric-dipole current and a sheet magnetic dipole radiate with opposite relative sign forward and backward); emission from opposite faces, several thicknesses, a fresh versus aged or capped surface, or a different pump photon energy, to separate surface from bulk; detection of both THz polarization components (an in-plane magnetic dipole predicts an E_y ∝ sin(2φ − 2δ) channel in quadrature with the measured one, though this constrains only the tensor form); and a reference emitter (ZnTe, or a non-magnetic or isostructural crystal) under identical conditions to fix absolute polarity and the detection-chain response.

2. The link to ferromagnetism and to the localized Cr³⁺ moments is not demonstrated; the only observable is far-field THz emission. The title and abstract claim "manipulation of ferromagnetism" and that the emission is "directly observe[d]" magnetic dipole radiation, but no measurement shows a change of the Cr magnetization, its direction, or its relation to the ordered state. The signal persists at nearly full strength at 80 K and 200 K (Figs. 2e,f), so even within the authors' picture most of the effect is a light-induced magnetization of a paramagnet. The itinerant nonequilibrium spin density would itself radiate as a magnetic dipole without involving the Cr moments, and the statement that the field "pull[s] [the magnetization] completely into the plane" (p. 5, Fig. 4d) is a model output, not an observation. The authors should add a direct magnetic probe of the pumped state with the same pump polarizations: time-resolved Kerr or Faraday rotation or ellipticity, or time-resolved magnetic linear dichroism, showing δM_y(t) ∝ cos(2φ − 2δ) with the same δ. They should also measure the emission against applied field (the model predicts a specific dependence on H_dc, Eq. V.33), consider a comparison with isostructural CrSiTe3 (T_c ≈ 33 K) to test whether any temperature feature moves with T_c, and plot the THz amplitude together with M(T) and χ(T) from Figs. S1/S2. Without such evidence the "manipulation of ferromagnetism" framing should be narrowed.

3. The 45° interpretation rests on a net, single-domain out-of-plane magnetization and on the static M rather than the pump-induced change, neither of which is established. Fig. S2 shows a soft ferromagnet with vanishing coercivity and no remanence, so at zero field the 1.9 mm (×√2) spot should cover many up/down domains with net ⟨M_z⟩ ≈ 0. The paper does not say whether any field was applied in the 45° (OptiCool) measurements. The pump-induced δM_z from tilting toward +y is odd in the sign of M_z and cancels between domains, leaving only the even δM_y, so the "frozen easy-axis component" that competes with M_y (p. 6) and the |M_y − M_z| interference that explains the polarity reversal and rotated two-fold pattern (Figs. 3b,c, 4d,e) would be absent. Moreover, THz emission is set by the pump-induced ΔM(t) (or its time derivatives), not the static M(I_0): Fig. 4d plots full static |M_z|, |M_y| and |M_y − M_z|, with M_z starting at 1 at zero fluence, so the minimum of the green curve comes from subtracting a large equilibrium M_z that does not itself radiate. Recomputed with ΔM_y and ΔM_z = M_z(I_0) − M_z(0), which have opposite signs, and with the 45° radiation projection and Fresnel factors, the destructive interference and minimum may not survive. The authors should state the field and magnetic history of the measurement, repeat the 45° fluence series with the sample saturated at +B_z and at −B_z (the M_z-derived part should reverse sign, and the pattern rotation should follow the sign of M_z), repeat at 45° above T_c (the sign reversal should vanish there if M_z competition is essential), and recompute Fig. 4d using ΔM with the correct geometric weights.

B. Should address

4. The temperature dependence does not support, and may argue against, a ferromagnetism-mediated emission, and it is not compared with the model. In Fig. 2f the signal rises from about 0.4 at 5–7 K to about 0.85 near 175–180 K, and the 200 K pattern in Fig. 2e is the largest. The text says it "decreases gradually with cooling and then undergoes a more rapid reduction upon crossing T_c" (p. 2), but within the error bars (about ±0.1–0.15, origin unstated) there is no clear feature at T_c; the amplitude is roughly flat from about 40 K to above T_c and the steep drop occurs below about 30–40 K, well inside the ordered phase. The petal orientation is identical at 7, 80 and 200 K, so the ordered moment, which lowers the magnetic point group from 3̄' to 3̄ (SI p. S-17), leaves no trace; magnetization-odd contributions allowed below T_c should be discussed and bounded. The model does not reproduce the trend in the regime measured: at low fluence the in-plane susceptibility is largest at and below T_c and falls as 1/(T − T_c/(1+κ)) above it (Eqs. V.20, V.23; Fig. S7b, which predicts η_z diverging at T_c), so the Curie–Weiss response at 200 K should be about ten times smaller than at 80 K, yet the measured amplitude is not smaller. The rise with temperature appears only at I_0 = 5 T_c0 (Figs. S8b, S9 right), a deep-saturation regime, while the temperature series uses 100 µJ/cm², which lies in the near-linear part of Fig. 4c. The authors should show with an explicit fluence-to-I_0/T_c0 map that 100 µJ/cm² is in the model's high-fluence regime or reconcile the trend otherwise; add a model curve to Fig. 2f at the same fluence; define the plotted quantity and the error bars; add finer polarization maps across T_c; explain why the change is concentrated far below T_c; and extend the temperature dependence to room temperature.

5. There is no quantitative estimate of the Edelstein–Zeeman field or of the magnetization change needed to radiate the measured field. The simulations use I_0 = 0.005 T_c0 and 5 T_c0 and χ_x,∥ = χ_z,xx = 0.5 (T_c0)⁻¹ (pp. S-20, S-21) with dimensionless fluence axes; the saturation and temperature trends require H^EZ of order 5 T_c0 (about 30 meV, effective fields of order 100 T). The authors should use a calibrated E_THz (see concern 18) to estimate the transient ΔM (in µ_B per Cr) and its rise time needed to radiate it as a magnetic dipole from the excited volume (spot size, absorption depth at 1.2 eV, pulse duration, collection optics); estimate the corresponding H^EZ in tesla and compare it with the anisotropy field of Cr2Ge2Te6 and with J_sd times a plausible photoinduced spin density at 0.1 mJ/cm²; and calibrate the theory's fluence axis against experiment. If H^EZ must be comparable to the exchange scale, that should be stated and justified. Without this the mechanism stays qualitative, and the magnitude may instead point to an electric-dipole source (concern 1).

6. A static equilibrium model is used to describe sub-picosecond dynamics. The modelling minimizes a free energy, or solves the self-consistent Eq. V.9, for a static H^EZ (Eq. 4; Fig. 4; Figs. S7–S9) and identifies the emitted amplitude with the equilibrium M. The radiated field is a roughly single-cycle transient of about 0.5–1 ps (Figs. 2c, 3b,d) and depends on d²M/dt², while H^EZ exists only during the coherence window of a 160 fs pulse. Local Cr moments respond through precession and damping, with magnon, FMR and anisotropy frequencies in the GHz to sub-THz range, and through longitudinal dynamics; neither reaches the adiabatic equilibrium assumed, and "pulling the magnetization completely into the plane" (p. 5) needs a coherent rotation that a 100–200 fs torque cannot complete. The model also gives no account of the waveform or spectrum. The authors should solve a time-dependent model (LLG, Landau–Lifshitz–Bloch or Bloch equations driven by H^EZ(t) with the experimental pulse envelope), derive the emitted waveform and spectrum and compare them with Figs. 2c and 3d (including the 0.3–1.5 THz content), and show that the polarization, fluence and waveform trends survive; or argue explicitly why the adiabatic limit applies and why the amplitude should track a static M(I_0).

7. The fluence-driven rotation and polarity reversal at 45° have simpler generic explanations that are not excluded. A two-fold pattern that rotates by 90° and passes through a minimum follows from interference between a polarization-independent term A and the anisotropic term B·cos(2φ − 2δ) whenever A and B have different signs or scale differently with fluence. Candidates for A include photo-Dember or a surface-field current along z (which radiates p-polarized light at oblique incidence), ultrafast demagnetization of M_z (polarization-independent and nonlinear in fluence), or a term linear in fluence such as surface rectification added to a saturating term. The main text dismisses photo-Dember as "largely isotropic", but an isotropic term is exactly what this interference needs. The spectral content also changes with fluence (Fig. 3d): the peak moves from about 0.4 THz at 0.1 mJ/cm² to about 1 THz at 0.75 mJ/cm² and the waveform becomes more oscillatory, whereas in the model both M_y and M_z are driven by the same H^EZ(t), so only their relative amplitude should change and the spectrum should stay fixed; a shifting spectrum points to two sources with different time responses. Fig. 3c shows only two fluences with sparse points, plotted without sign. The authors should show signed polarization maps at several fluences through the minimum (about 0.1–0.4 mJ/cm²), fit them to A(F) + B(F)·cos(2φ − 2δ) or the full Eq. 3 at oblique incidence including the E_z terms, report how the coefficients depend on fluence and temperature (including above T_c), decompose the waveforms into a fluence-dependent sum of two fixed temporal components, and show that the results follow the M_y/M_z competition rather than a sum of independent contributions.

9. The saturating fluence dependence (Fig. 4c) is taken as the signature of Heisenberg magnetization near saturation (p. 6), but it has standard non-magnetic explanations: absorption or band-filling saturation at the near-resonant 1.2 eV transition, Coulomb screening of the THz-generating field by photocarriers, and heating (concern 10). The authors should measure the absorbed fluence (pump transmission or reflection) against incident fluence, measure a reference quantity such as the transient reflectivity amplitude or the carrier density by optical-pump/THz-probe, plot the THz amplitude against absorbed fluence or carrier density, and show that the emission saturates at a different fluence or with a different temperature dependence than those quantities.

10. Heating, steady-state and transient, is not quantified, although the paper claims a non-thermal mechanism and draws conclusions relative to T_c ≈ 67 K. The normal-incidence data use 50 kHz (25 kHz after chopping) and up to 330 mW average power on a 1.9 mm spot in an exchange-gas cryostat at a nominal 7 K, where van der Waals crystals conduct heat poorly; the 45° data use up to 90 mW at 3 kHz in a different cryostat. Per pulse, an absorbed fluence of about 0.3 mJ/cm² deposited within a penetration depth of a few tens of nm gives an energy density of order tens of J/cm³, which literature heat capacities suggest is comparable to or larger than the enthalpy needed to heat Cr2Ge2Te6 from 7 K to above T_c. Heating could blur the T_c feature in Fig. 2f, produce the saturation in Fig. 4c through loss of magnetization and the non-monotonic behaviour in Fig. 4e, and means the effective spin temperature is not the 7 K assumed in the model (T = 0.104 T_c0, Fig. S9). The authors should give the absorbed fluence and penetration depth at 1.2 eV; estimate the per-pulse and steady-state temperature rise; test steady-state heating by varying the repetition rate or chopping at fixed pulse energy (e.g. from the shift of a T_c-related feature); state the effective temperature for Figs. 2f and 4; show the low-fluence pattern is unchanged after high-fluence exposure; and explain what beyond the polarization dependence supports "non-thermal".

12. The microscopic theory is a generic toy model, does not treat the ferromagnetic phase, and is not compared with established mechanisms. The SI Hamiltonian (Eq. III.3, Fig. S5) is a two-site Dirac/Rashba model with opposite-sign SOC on inversion partners stacked along z, C_n symmetry about z and unspecified t'1, t'2 and α̃. It shows χ^(2) ≠ 0 but gives neither its magnitude, sign, δ, nor the ratio χ_z,xx/χ_x,∥ that the 45° interpretation needs. The real 1.2 eV transitions involve Te-p/Cr-d manifolds on a honeycomb lattice with C3 Cr sites; the intraband dipole-current terms involve Fermi-surface contributions that an insulator at low temperature lacks; and the hidden-spin-texture argument relies on Kramers degeneracy, which is lifted below T_c by exchange splitting. There, polarization-dependent absorption by spin-polarized bands, photoinduced magnetic anisotropy and the inverse Cotton–Mouton effect all give polarization-dependent torques on M from linearly polarized light. J_sd, the photocarrier density and the coherence lifetime (regularized by δ⁺ → 0) set H^EZ but none is estimated. The authors should discuss how the Edelstein–Zeeman field differs from these established mechanisms in symmetry and experimentally; explain why the dynamic intra-cell quadrupoles (SI Sec. IV.B.4) do not themselves radiate comparably; and estimate χ^(2) for the real band structure (e.g. DFT with SOC, as in Refs. 6–8, or a tight-binding model fitted to Cr2Ge2Te6), or present the theory explicitly as a symmetry-allowed proof of principle with the magnitude constrained by experiment.

13. Fresnel factors and refraction at 45° are not included. 17. The polarization purity of the pump inside the cryostat is not verified.
