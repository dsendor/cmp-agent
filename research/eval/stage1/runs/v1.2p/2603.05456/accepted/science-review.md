A. Must address

1. **The 45° interpretation needs a single-domain out-of-plane magnetization, but the field history and domain state are never given and no field-dependent data are shown.** The explanation of Fig. 3b–d and Fig. 4d, e (pages 4–6) rests on a competition between the light-induced in-plane M_y and a "frozen" equilibrium easy-axis M_z, with E_THz ∝ (M × n). Bulk Cr2Ge2Te6 is a soft ferromagnet. Below Tc in zero field it forms stripe or bubble domains, so up and down M_z average to near zero over a pump spot of tens to hundreds of micrometres. The M_z contribution, and the interference that is said to produce the polarity reversal, 90° rotation and spectral-weight minimum, would then largely cancel. The Methods mention a magneto-optic cryostat, yet the paper never applies a field and does not say whether the sample was zero-field-cooled or field-cooled. Without field data, the link between the emission and the ferromagnetic order rests only on a modest kink in Fig. 2f and on this unverified two-component picture. The authors should state the magnetic history. They should repeat the 45° fluence series after field cooling in +B_z and −B_z and under ±B along c: the M_z-derived component must reverse sign with the field while the light-set M_y component must not, and the fluence at which the pattern reorients should shift with field, which the Landau model ties to r0, r_z and g. They should also show how an in-plane and an out-of-plane field change the normal-incidence signal, below and above Tc.

2. **Heating is not excluded, although "non-thermal" is a headline claim, and it could produce the fluence-driven reorientation at 45°.** The polarity reversal and 90° rotation at 45° occur between 0.1 and 0.75 mJ/cm² at 7 K (Fig. 3b–d), with fluences up to 1.5 mJ/cm² (Fig. 4e) of above-gap 1.2 eV photons. Absorption within roughly 10–100 nm deposits tens of J/cm³, and the lattice heat capacity of Cr2Ge2Te6 is small at 7 K, so the excited volume could be heated transiently, or cumulatively depending on the repetition rate, toward or above Tc = 66 K. The repetition rate, spot size, absorbed fluence and absorption depth are not given, and no temperature rise is estimated. Transient or steady heating, ultrafast demagnetization of M_z, or a heating-induced change of anisotropy would suppress the M_z-related contribution and leave the in-plane one. At 45°, M_z radiates into the detected p-polarization, so a polarization-independent thermal or demagnetization term A added to a cos 2φ term from M_y gives a twofold pattern that rotates by 90° and passes through a minimum as the relative weight of A changes. That reproduces Fig. 3c, d and 4e without an Edelstein-generated competition. The same heating could also contribute to the saturation at normal incidence (Fig. 4c). The paper's argument that a change in magnetization magnitude alone cannot give non-monotonic behaviour does not address this offset scenario. The authors should report the repetition rate, spot size and absorbed energy density; estimate the transient and average lattice and spin temperatures at each fluence relative to Tc; decompose the 45° data at each fluence into isotropic and cos 2φ / sin 2φ parts; and, crucially, show the 45° fluence series above Tc (for example at 80 K), where a reversal caused by competition with ordered M_z must vanish. A temperature series at fixed fluence across Tc at 45°, and a check that the crossover fluence in Fig. 4e does not shift with base temperature or repetition rate as heating would predict, would further test this.

B. Should address

3. **The paper does not show that the Cr ferromagnetic moments, rather than the photoexcited carriers, are the radiating magnetization.** The title, abstract and Discussion claim "manipulation of ferromagnetism" and "femtosecond-to-picosecond manipulation of magnetic order". The only observable is far-field THz emission during the first ~2 ps: a single-cycle transient with spectral weight at 0.3–1.5 THz (Fig. 3d) and no feature at the Cr2Ge2Te6 magnon or FMR frequency, which lies in the GHz range. The emission pattern and amplitude are essentially unchanged across Tc (Fig. 2e at 80 K and 200 K; Fig. 2f is largest in the paramagnetic phase). The nonequilibrium spin or orbital density of the photocarriers (the nonlinear Edelstein δM of Eq. 1 itself) would radiate as a magnetic dipole with the same φ dependence without any rotation of the Cr moments, and the paper itself describes the magnetic degrees of freedom as "slower". The authors should separate carrier magnetization from Cr magnetization driven by H^EZ with a direct probe of the ordered moment, such as time-resolved, pump-polarization-dependent MOKE or Faraday rotation at the same fluences (feasible in the existing setup), the XMCD they propose, or at least a static Kerr measurement before and after pumping, showing a change in the ordered moment that outlasts the pulse. At minimum they should estimate the Cr response time. Without such a probe, the headline claim should be narrowed to THz emission from a light-induced nonequilibrium (nonlinear Edelstein) magnetization, with ferromagnetic involvement inferred only from the 45° data.

4. **Surface electric-dipole, bulk electric-quadrupole and photon-drag emission are not excluded experimentally.** The arguments against surface rectification (page 2–3) are that a surface χ(2) would not track the magnetic order, would scale linearly with fluence, and would change the pattern. None is decisive. Surface depletion fields, carrier densities and surface χ(2) in a semiconductor are temperature dependent, and Fig. 2f shows a smooth crossover with most of the signal well above Tc rather than an order-parameter-like onset. Saturation is generic (see concern 10). The authors themselves note on page 3 that the surface "has no mirror symmetry ... to lock the surface dipole emission to the crystal axes", so an in-plane polar response of point group 3 at the surface (surface optical rectification, shift or injection currents, P_x ∝ a(E_x² − E_y²) + b(2E_xE_y) with arbitrary phase) gives exactly the same |cos(2φ − 2δ)| pattern as Eq. (3). In the bulk, linear photon drag (in-plane current ∝ q_z E⊗E, allowed in trigonal symmetry) and electric-quadrupole rectification (which Fig. 1 itself invokes) also give a cos(2φ − 2δ) response. At 1.2 eV the penetration depth is likely only tens of nm, so bulk and surface are not cleanly separated. The magnetic-dipole interpretation therefore rests on the trends with temperature and fluence. The authors should add discriminating tests: (a) rotating the sample azimuth at fixed pump polarization, compared with rotating the polarization at fixed sample, to tie the lobes to the crystal axes and report δ; (b) detecting both orthogonal THz polarization components (only the component along a is measured), since Eq. (2) also predicts an in-plane M_x (M± terms) and point group −3 fixes how the full in-plane vector rotates with φ, with a definite phase relation and sign alternation between lobes; this would also show that the response follows bulk −3 symmetry rather than strain, step edges or a particular termination; (c) front- versus back-face illumination, or transmission versus reflection, which separates magnetic-dipole from electric-dipole emission and mechanisms odd in the light wavevector (photon drag) from those even in it (E⊗E → M); (d) thickness dependence; (e) incidence-angle dependence, which differs between magnetic-dipole, quadrupole and surface-dipole radiation; and (f) a non-magnetic, isostructural or same-point-group layered reference crystal measured under identical conditions.

5. **The temperature dependence argues as much against magnetic control as for it.** Fig. 2e–f and page 2 report that the emission increases with temperature: the spectral weight is largest in the paramagnetic phase, roughly flat from ~70 to 200 K, about twice its 7 K value near 150 K, and falls only below ~50 K. Ordering thus suppresses rather than produces the emission. In the model of Eq. (4), with r_z ∝ T − Tc, the in-plane magnetization induced by a fixed H^EZ above Tc scales with the paramagnetic susceptibility, which should peak near Tc and fall roughly as 1/(T − Tc), crossing over below Tc to the transverse susceptibility set by the anisotropy. The data show no feature near Tc and the opposite trend at high temperature. The text says the emission "tracks the magnetic ordering" (page 3), but the "more rapid reduction upon crossing Tc" in Fig. 2f is hard to see within the error bars, and the account of the crossover is qualitative. The authors should compute the temperature dependence predicted by their mean-field model and overlay it on Fig. 2f; extend the data above 200 K, for example to room temperature; measure the fluence dependence at several temperatures above and below Tc, since the model predicts a saturation fluence that tracks r0(T); consider temperature-dependent non-magnetic factors such as the absorption edge, carrier lifetime and THz absorption; explain how a mechanism requiring a magnetic susceptibility gives its largest signal where the susceptibility is smallest; and, if the 200 K signal comes from a non-magnetic channel, show how it is separated from the 7 K signal. The title claim should be framed in light of the effect existing without ferromagnetic order.

6. **Inverse Cotton-Mouton and photo-induced anisotropy are not ruled out as the coupling to the ordered moment.** Below Tc, a response δM ∝ M E E (inverse Cotton-Mouton) is allowed for linear polarization in a magnetized crystal, as is a photo-induced change of the magnetic anisotropy. Either would give polarization-dependent emission that changes at Tc. The introduction dismisses these channels on symmetry grounds, but in the ordered phase they are not symmetry-forbidden. The authors should state and test the specific evidence for a time-reversal-even, interband-resonant α(2) origin of the ordered-phase response as opposed to these M-linear channels, for example by field reversal (concern 1; the inverse Cotton-Mouton term reverses with M) and pump photon-energy dependence (concern 13).
Raised by: r3

7. **A static free-energy minimization is compared with a radiated field set by the time derivative of M.** The Landau model of Eq. (4) minimizes F(M) under a constant H^EZ; it has no time scale, damping or equation of motion. It assumes the Cr magnetization follows H^EZ adiabatically on a sub-picosecond time scale, which is not justified for precessional dynamics in a weakly anisotropic magnet. Magnetic-dipole emission is proportional to ∂²M/∂t² (or ∂M/∂t in the near field), so the rise time, decay time and spectral content of δM(t) set both amplitude and waveform. The waveform and spectrum change markedly with fluence at 45° (Fig. 3d: the spectral peak moves from ~0.5 THz at low fluence to ~1 THz at 0.75 mJ/cm²), which the static picture cannot capture and which the paper does not discuss; the spectral-weight comparison in Fig. 4c, e implicitly assumes the shape is fixed. The authors should model the dynamics, for example with LLG or time-dependent mean-field equations driven by a pulsed H^EZ(t) set by the 160 fs pump envelope and the carrier lifetime, and predict the waveform and spectrum; or explicitly justify why spectral weight should be proportional to the static |M_y| or |M_y − M_z|. They should also discuss the fluence-dependent spectral shift.

Fig. 4e has six points, one (1.5 mJ/cm²) with a large error bar, and the minimum near 0.2 mJ/cm² rests on few points. If spectral weight is an integral of |E(ω)|², it is quadratic in the emitted field, while the model quantity |M_y| is linear in it. The authors should define spectral weight and compare quantities that scale the same way; list all parameters with values and sources, including in the Fig. 4 caption; state how many parameters are fitted relative to the number of data points; overlay the model on Figs. 2f, 4c and 4e in physical units; and either fit the normal-incidence fluence, 45° fluence and temperature data with one shared parameter set or soften "quantitatively".

9. **Conventional mechanisms at 45° incidence need fuller treatment.** Page 4 dismisses photo-Dember and surface-field emission as "largely isotropic with respect to pump polarization". At 45°, however, s- and p-polarized light are absorbed differently through the Fresnel coefficients, so any z-directed surface current acquires a twofold φ dependence. Competition between such a term, which saturates with carrier density, and a polarization-dependent rectification term could also produce a polarity reversal and pattern rotation with fluence. The meaning of φ relative to the plane of incidence and the crystal axes, and how much E_z the pump carries, are not stated. The authors should state these, measure the polarization dependence of the absorbed fraction at 45°, estimate the photo-Dember or surface-field contribution, and show that their two-component decomposition is not reproduced by these terms.

10. **The saturation at normal incidence has simpler explanations.** Page 2, point (ii), argues that the sublinear fluence dependence in Fig. 4c is incompatible with a χ(2) mechanism and reads it as Zeeman-like saturation of M_y. But saturable absorption, Pauli blocking or band filling, screening by dense photocarriers, two-photon absorption or a fluence-dependent carrier lifetime all make second-order emission saturate at mJ/cm² fluences. The authors should measure the fluence dependence of absorbed power or transient reflectivity over the same range; if the THz amplitude saturates while the excited carrier density stays linear, that supports the magnetic interpretation. Ideally they should also compare with the saturation of a non-magnetic reference.

11. **The superposition test in Fig. 3d is under-specified.** The 0.4 mJ/cm² trace is compared with E_0.1 + E_0.75 without stated weights. Under the model both the M_y and M_z contributions change with fluence, so an unweighted sum of the two endpoint traces has no obvious reason to reproduce the intermediate one, and the agreement may be partly coincidental. The spectral-weight minimum is also near 0.2 mJ/cm² (Fig. 4e), not 0.4. The authors should state whether any scaling, time shift or normalization was applied and give a residual or goodness-of-fit measure; show the same test at other intermediate fluences (0.15, 0.2 mJ/cm²); and ideally decompose all fluence traces globally (for example by SVD or a fit to two fixed temporal basis waveforms with fluence-dependent weights), showing that two components explain the full series and that the extracted weights follow the model M_y(I0) and M_z(I0) of Fig. 4d.

12. **The size of the induced magnetization and of the Edelstein-Zeeman field is not estimated.** The paper does not estimate the δM (in μB per Cr or as a fraction of Ms) needed to produce the measured several-V/cm field via the magnetic-dipole radiation formula, given Ms and the absorption depth; nor the H^EZ needed to rotate M away from the easy axis against the anisotropy field, as implied by the observed saturation; nor a microscopic estimate of J·χ(2) (Eq. 5) or the nonlinear Edelstein susceptibility at 1.2 eV from the band structure. Order-of-magnitude numbers would show whether the Cr moments can plausibly be rotated "completely into the plane" (Fig. 4a, d and text) at sub-mJ/cm² fluence, and whether the carrier spin density alone could account for the signal (concern 3).
