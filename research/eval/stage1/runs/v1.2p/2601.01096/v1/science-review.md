A. Must address

1. Nothing in the data separates excitonic pair breaking from ordinary defect states in a hybridization-gapped (band) insulator or semiconductor, so the central claim and the Conclusion are circular. Every observation offered as evidence would also appear for a vacancy with donor/acceptor or charge-transition levels in a non-interacting two-band insulator with a static (for example lattice- or phonon-driven) hybridization of the same size. These observations are: (i) a pair of in-gap peaks with mixed electron–hole character; (ii) energies that depend on the defect site and on intra- and inter-band scattering (Fig. 2d); (iii) peaks that shift with tip–sample distance (Fig. 2e); (iv) identical E_e and E_h spatial profiles (Fig. 3g,h), which could be two charge-transition levels of one localized orbital; and (v) bonding/antibonding splitting in dimers (Figs. S10, S11). In the two-band model of Supplementary Sec. II, Δ0 is a fixed, non-self-consistent parameter (Δ0 = 35 meV in Fig. 2f; 45 and 18 meV in Fig. 4d; an imposed tanh profile Δ(x) in Fig. 4f). With a fixed Δ the mean-field EI Hamiltonian is formally identical to a band insulator with interband hybridization Δ, where a scatterer with J1 and J2 terms gives in-gap bound states just as well. Reproducing an asymmetric pair of peaks (Fig. 2f, Figs. S6–S8) is therefore not evidence that the gap is excitonic. Whether the Ta2Pd3Te5 gap is excitonic or structural is exactly the open question of refs. 6, 21–23, and defect states in a topological insulator (Ta2Pd3Te5 has been discussed as a quantum spin Hall and topological excitonic insulator, refs. 22, 25) are a further alternative. The Conclusion's statement that the results "confirm that the probed insulating gap at impurity-free region arises from the excitonic pairing" assumes what it sets out to show. The authors should identify at least one observable that the excitonic scenario predicts and a single-particle defect-level scenario does not, and test it. Options: a self-consistent calculation in which the order parameter is suppressed around the vacancy, predicting a gap-edge depression and healing length to be compared with dI/dV maps of the local gap edge near the defect; a dependence on coupling sign or order-parameter phase that a fixed hybridization lacks; a DFT supercell calculation of the Te vacancy in a non-excitonic Ta2Pd3Te5 slab, showing whether in-gap levels at the observed energies and with the observed orbital shapes arise without any order parameter; the evolution of the states as the gap closes on warming toward the transition; the dependence on electron–hole imbalance; a check of the bound-state decay length against the excitonic coherence length ħv/Δ, set against the measured 0.6 nm and 2.5 nm widths; and an explicit calculation of what the same model gives for a hybridization-only insulator with the same gap, showing which features it fails to reproduce. Failing such a test, the title, abstract and Conclusion should present pair breaking as one consistent interpretation ("consistent with") rather than a demonstration.

2. The reported "subgap" energies are inconsistent with the authors' own gap. In Fig. 1d the gap Δ ≈ 85 meV is defined from a hole-side onset near −35 mV to an electron-side coherence peak near +50 mV. Yet the most probable resonance separation for V_Te3 is 2δ_m = 104 meV, with histogram tails to about 120 meV (Fig. 2d), which exceeds the full gap; and the hole component E_h of V_Te2 is mapped at −45 meV (Fig. 3f, 3g,h) and appears near −45 mV in Fig. 2c, outside the stated hole edge. In Fig. 2c the defect spectra also show a hard, nearly zero-conductance region between the two resonances and, beyond them, no remnant of the host's +50 mV peak or hole-side onset. This lineshape looks more like a locally shifted or renormalized gap with enhanced edges than like bound states inside an intact gap, whereas pair-breaking (YSR-type) states must lie at |E| < Δ with the host coherence features still visible outside them; this is the central premise of the title. The authors should define the gap edges quantitatively (criterion, threshold, spatial averaging); overlay defect and defect-free spectra on the same absolute scale over a wide bias range (±200 mV); report E_e, E_h and the local gap edges measured next to each defect (relevant given the inhomogeneity of Fig. 4); plot E_e and E_h, not only 2δ, against those edges for every defect in Fig. S3; and explain how a 104 meV separation fits in an 85 meV gap, or revise the gap definition.

3. Tip-induced band bending (TIBB), voltage division and tip-induced charging have not been ruled out as the origin of the tip "tunability", and several observations point toward them. The abstract says the energies are "continuously tunable by tip electric field", but Fig. 2e (and Fig. S5) and Fig. 4e change the setpoint current at fixed V_b = 0.1 V, up to 8 nA and 90 nA (junction resistance down to about 1 MΩ). This mainly changes tip height, and with it the local field, TIBB, the charge state of the defect, and possibly local heating and tip–sample forces. In a narrow-gap, low-carrier-density material, a Te-vacancy donor/acceptor level in a double-barrier geometry generically gives resonances at both polarities (resonant tunnelling plus tip-induced ionization) that shift and broaden with setpoint exactly as in Fig. 2e. The modelling instead treats the tip as a change of J1 and J2, finds that |J1,2| must decrease on approach, which the authors themselves call "unexpected" (p. 4), and resolves this post hoc with an opposite-sign tip potential. The lower-energy states that appear only above a threshold of ≈25 nA (Fig. 4e) are especially suggestive of a charging transition. The authors should: separate field and distance by measuring at fixed tip height with different setpoint biases (including both polarities) and at fixed current with different setpoint biases; give I(z) curves or the apparent barrier height so I_t can be converted to tip height; show whether the gap edges and coherence-like peak of a defect-free region shift over the same I_t sequence (they should under TIBB, not under purely local pair breaking); look for charging rings or bias-dependent contrast in dI/dV maps around vacancies; give peak energies extrapolated to I_t → 0; estimate TIBB from the gap, carrier density and tip work function, and show that an electrostatic shift of a localized level cannot reproduce the observed shifts; and analyse the 25 nA threshold in the same way. The abstract's "tip electric field" wording should then be reconciled with what these controls show.

B. Should address

4. The model parameters are tuned panel by panel and are not tied to the measured gap or band structure, so the model comparison is only qualitative. The measured gap is about 85 meV (p. 2), but the simulations use Δ0 = 35 meV and μ = 12.25 meV (Fig. 2f), Δ0 = 45 and 18 meV (Fig. 4d), and Δ0 = 30 meV with x_c = 80 b0 ≈ 30 nm (Fig. 4f). J1 changes sign between the higher-energy (J1 = −15 meV) and lower-energy (J1 = +20 meV) cases of Fig. 4d; this sign sets the intensity asymmetry (Fig. S6) but has no physical justification. It is not stated whether the model's bands are one-dimensional, how they disperse, whether they reflect Ta2Pd3Te5's quasi-1D, multi-orbital, spin-orbit-coupled band structure and proposed Dirac/topological character (refs. 6, 22), or how a Te vacancy maps microscopically onto intra- and inter-band potentials. With Δ0, μ, J1 and J2 free per panel, agreement with lineshapes is weak evidence. The authors should state which parameters are fixed by independent data (DFT/ARPES bands, gap and doping from the clean-region spectrum) and which are fitted; explain the factor of about 2 between Δ0 and Δ; justify the J1 sign change and the 30 nm healing length; and show that one parameter set, with Δ0 and μ fixed from adjacent defect-free spectra and only the claimed-to-vary quantity (J1, J2, tip potential or local Δ) changed, reproduces all vacancy types, both regions and the tip-height series, reporting which features it fails to capture.

5. The temperature dependence (Fig. 2g) does not connect the subgap states to the excitonic order. The data cover only 1.6–20 K, whereas the gap is about 85–100 meV and excitonic order is reported to persist to about 10² K (refs. 5, 6), so the order parameter should be essentially constant over this range. The text nevertheless invokes the "thermal suppression of excitonic gap", without any defect-free spectra at 20 K showing such suppression, and attributes the faster-than-Fermi-convolution decay to "local suppression of the excitonic gap by tip potential and/or the quantum pairing poison". That explanation is not specific to an EI (refs. 32, 33 concern Majorana/Andreev quasiparticle poisoning); phonon-assisted or inelastic broadening of a localized level, thermally activated charge fluctuations of a donor level, changes in tip state or setpoint with temperature, drift, or a tip temperature different from the sample's would do the same. The authors should show defect-free spectra at each temperature with the same tip; give fitted peak widths and energies against T with uncertainties, quantifying the claimed shift "toward the gap center", which is not obvious in Fig. 2g; quantify the extra broadening against lock-in and instrumental resolution; and ideally extend the measurements to temperatures where the gap measurably changes, plotting subgap energies against the simultaneously measured local gap (a pair-breaking state should track Δ(T); a defect level need not).

6. The type-I/type-II classification and the "secondary pair breaking" interpretation of the lower-energy states (Fig. 4) are under-supported. The regions are defined by a shift of about 20 meV in a single "characteristic normal-state" feature near +120 to +130 meV (Fig. 4b, arrows) whose origin is not identified, and reading that shift as a change in electron–hole imbalance rather than in local band bending is not tested by any other measure. It is also unclear that the gap center lies below E_F as stated: edges at about −35 and +50 mV in Fig. 1d place it above E_F. The lower-energy states require I_t ≳ 25 nA (up to 90 nA in Fig. 4e), where tip forces, heating and TIBB are substantial, and their threshold appearance and low "surviving temperature" (Fig. S12) are typical of tip-induced charging. The Δ(x) = Δ0 tanh(x/x_c) profile with x_c = 80 b0 (Fig. 4f) is put in by hand, forcing the gap to zero at the impurity. The authors should report the local gap magnitude, edges and center in type-I and type-II regions from many spectra (a locally reduced order parameter should give a smaller gap); show the I_t dependence of a defect-free type-II spectrum up to 90 nA; provide bias- and height-dependent dI/dV maps around a type-II defect to test for charging rings; state how many defects of each type were measured in each region and how many type-II defects show the lower-energy states; demonstrate the correlation with local doping across a field of view, for example by mapping the characteristic-state energy alongside impurity-state energies; and justify or self-consistently derive the Δ(x) profile and its length scale.

7. The Te-vacancy identification rests on topography alone. The defects are assigned as surface Te vacancies on chains Te1–Te4 from their appearance in cluster-decorated-tip images (Figs. 2a,b, S1, S2), where they appear as "elongated protrusions", which is unusual for a missing chalcogen atom. No DFT-simulated STM images, formation-energy considerations or controlled creation/annealing are given, and the absence of Te5 vacancies is not explained. Since the configuration dependence of 2δ (Fig. 2d) is central to the "coupling-tunable" argument, the chemical identity and site assignment matter. The authors should add simulated topographs (including the defect-induced LDOS) for V_Te1–V_Te4 and for competing candidates (antisites, substitutions, interstitials, Pd or Ta vacancies, subsurface defects, adsorbates), or controlled creation/annealing experiments, and explain why no Te5 vacancies are seen.

8. The defect dependence of 2δ is neither quantified nor specific to pair breaking. The text argues that the vacancy-dependent 2δ (Fig. 2d) "highlights their origin from the e–h pair-breaking states", but bound-state energies of any defect depend on its local configuration. The distributions overlap strongly (V_Te4 and V_Te2 differ by only 7 meV in 2δ_m with widths of order 20 meV), so "statistically distinct ranges" needs a quantitative test, and because the peak energies depend on I_t (Fig. 2e, concern 3) the histograms are meaningful only at a common setpoint and comparable tip. The claimed ordering V_Te2 → V_Te4 → V_Te1 → V_Te3 as one of coupling strength has no microscopic support. The authors should report the setpoint, the number of vacancies per type, the number of tips and cleaves, and the Gaussian widths and 2δ_m with uncertainties; apply a statistical test; state whether the Fig. S3 spectra are at a common setpoint or corrected for it; say whether the spread within a class correlates with the type-I/II environment of Fig. 4; and link the ordering to computed vacancy potentials or orbital weight on each chain.

9. The "locked" spatial profiles of E_e and E_h are not a distinctive signature, and their analysis is under-specified. On p. 5 the collapse of the 10 mV and −45 mV linecuts onto one curve (Fig. 3g,h) is taken as "locked as expected for pair-breaking subgap states". For impurity states in superconductors (ref. 19), however, the particle (u) and hole (v) components generically have different spatial profiles and oscillation phases, while a single localized defect level seen at both polarities would give identical profiles. It is not stated whether the curves were normalized before overlaying, and Fig. 3g shows clear differences in the tails beyond ±0.3 nm. The authors should plot the calculated u and v profiles from the model (Fig. S8) on the same axes as the data, state the normalization, and explain why identical profiles are expected here when they are not in the superconducting analogue.

10. The spatial maps may contain setpoint-normalization artifacts. The maps in Fig. 3d–f and linecuts in Fig. 3g,h were presumably taken in constant-current mode at a fixed setpoint. With strong in-gap weight at the defect, setpoint normalization alone can suppress the 60 meV map at the defect, producing the "spectral reversal" read as spectral-weight transfer, and can make the E_e and E_h profiles look alike. The authors should state the setpoint and normalization used, show constant-height maps or maps at different setpoint biases, and present the raw linecuts so that the claimed locking is not a normalization artifact.

11. Reproducibility across samples and tips is not documented. The paper does not say how many crystals, cleaves and tips the key observations (Figs. 2e, 2g, 3, 4e) come from, or whether resonance energies for a given vacancy type agree between them, although tip state clearly affects these spectra (tip decoration is used deliberately). 12. The dimer evidence is generic and rests on single examples. Splitting into bonding/antibonding-like states for one dimer at 1.3 nm (Fig. S10) but not one at about 2 nm (Fig. S11) is expected for any overlapping localized states (cf. ref. 36, Mn in GaAs), so it does not specifically probe "quantum pair-breaking states". The crystal axis along which each dimer is separated is not given, which matters given the 0.6 nm versus 2.5 nm anisotropy of Fig. 3g,h. The authors should show several dimers at several separations and orientations (along b versus c), report the splitting against distance, compare it with the anisotropic decay lengths of Fig. 3g,h, and compare the measured splitting with a two-impurity calculation in the same model.

13. Several claims go beyond the data. 14. The non-magnetic character of the vacancy is assumed, not tested. The model assumes a non-magnetic impurity in a singlet EI, but a Te vacancy may carry a local moment depending on its charge state. Spectra in an applied magnetic field (Zeeman splitting or shift of E_e and E_h) would test this and bear directly on the singlet-versus-triplet question the authors raise. If no field is available, the authors should at least discuss the expected charge and spin state of the vacancy.
