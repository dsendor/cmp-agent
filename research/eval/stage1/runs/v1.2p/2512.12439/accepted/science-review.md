A. Must address

1. Only one flake per condition, so the central comparison is not shown to be reproducible. Each of the five conditions (7L/Al2O3, 4L/Al2O3, 18L/Au, 6L/Au, 4L/Au) appears to rest on a single flake measured at a single spot. The headline contrast, 4L/Al2O3 (sharp, 280–287 K) against 4L/Au (broad, about 220 ± 40 K), is therefore between two flakes made by different processes (Figs. 3, 4, 5). Thin-flake Tc values are known to scatter: the authors themselves cite 309 K for 8L on SiO2 and modest gate-induced shifts in thin flakes. Their own Al2O3 data show that Tc depends steeply on thickness (320 K at 7L, 280 K at 4L), so a one-layer error in thickness, or a region locally thinner inside the laser spot, could move Tc by tens of kelvin. Flake-to-flake variation in strain (Tc is strongly stress-sensitive, Ref. 16), wrinkles, tears, defect density, or ALD and evaporation conditions could also produce shifts and broadenings of tens of kelvin. A 40 K versus 150 K difference from two flakes therefore cannot be separated from sample variation. The authors should state how many flakes of each kind were prepared and measured; measure at least two or three further flakes for each of 4L/Au and 4L/Al2O3 (ideally also 5–6L on each substrate), preferably from different exfoliation runs; and show the spread of the onset and width of the transition in Fig. 5. If possible, they should also measure a single flake that spans both substrates, for example a flake on a laterally patterned Au/Al2O3 substrate or one partly covered by Al2O3 before Au deposition, with Raman line scans across the boundary. This would remove flake-to-flake variation from the comparison entirely.

2. Strain and interface chemistry are not excluded as the cause of the lowered and broadened Tc on Au. The paper attributes the whole difference between the substrates to screening and charge transfer from a metal (pp. 14–15), but the two interfaces are made in very different ways. On the Au side, Au is thermally evaporated at room temperature directly onto the Se-terminated surface. It conforms to the surface ripples and binds more strongly, which the authors themselves invoke (p. 4), and hot Au adatoms could intermix with, bond to or react with the first Se layer. On the Al2O3 side, the flake sees 50–100 nm of ALD Al2O3 grown from TMA/H2O at 100–150 °C, a heated, chemically reactive process that could itself passivate or modify the first layer, with Au further below. The two stacks also impose different interfacial strain and different thermal-expansion mismatch on cooling, which can be inhomogeneous and temperature dependent. Because Ta2NiSe5 is very sensitive to uniaxial pressure and strain (Refs. 14–17), a substrate-imposed strain is a direct alternative explanation for both the suppression and the broadening, and an interfacial strain decaying away from the substrate would produce the same vertical Tc gradient attributed to screening. The sharp 4L/Al2O3 transition rules out bulk oxidation (p. 15), but not an Au-specific chemical or strain effect. The authors should: compare the frequencies and linewidths of strain-sensitive phonons (for example the orthorhombic Ag(o) modes in the aa channel) well above Tc (340–400 K) and well below Tc across all five flakes, to bound any relative strain; discuss interfacial strain explicitly; add a control that keeps the Au and its mechanical environment but blocks electronic contact, for example Au evaporated onto 1–2 nm of ALD Al2O3, an evaporated dielectric, or a monolayer hBN spacer, and/or a non-metal deposited the same way as Au; and, if possible, characterize the interface (XPS, or cross-sectional TEM/EDX) to show that the interfacial layer stays chemically intact. Without these, "charge transfer and electrostatic screening ... are the likely culprit" (p. 15) is one hypothesis among several.

3. The data do not separate excitonic from electron-lattice mechanisms, and the quantitative decomposition of Tc goes too far. The conclusions that Au mainly "weaken[s] electron-hole interactions" rather than electron-lattice coupling, that excitonic mechanisms account for up to about 150 K of Tc with about 180 K left to the lattice (p. 16), and that the results "confirm the existence of excitonic effects" (abstract, Discussion, Conclusions) rest on four untested assumptions: (a) the disappearance of the quasi-elastic peak in 4L/Au means excitonic fluctuations are destroyed rather than buried in noise (see concern 6); (b) the 16% drop of the maximum of −1/q (0.38 against 0.45) means the electron-phonon coupling is unchanged, although q also depends on the continuum's density of states and its coupling, both of which change directly with doping and screening, so the Fano parameter does not isolate the electron-lattice coupling strength; (c) the interfacial layers lose their excitonic contribution completely, whereas in the mixed-phase picture those layers do not reach a monoclinic ground state within the measured range, so "suppression by up to 150 K" is at most a bound on the perturbation of those layers, not a measure of the excitonic share; and (d) the excitonic and lattice contributions to Tc add linearly, which is not true in general. Electron doping and metallic screening also suppress a purely hybridization-, band-Jahn-Teller- or phonon-driven instability, as in electron-doped CDW systems, because they move the Fermi level away from compensation and screen interband matrix elements, so a Tc reduction under charge transfer is expected in both pictures. Strain or chemistry (concern 2) would also lower Tc without being specifically excitonic. The authors should either support the attribution with a model calculation (for example a minimal two-band model with phonon coupling and a screened interaction, or a Landau/mean-field model with exciton-lattice coupling) showing that screening and doping suppress the two mechanisms differently and predicting the size of the Tc shift, or substantially soften the abstract, Discussion and Conclusions to "consistent with" and drop or qualify the numerical 150 K / 180 K split.

B. Should address

4. The optical depth sensitivity near a metal substrate is not accounted for, which undermines the vertical-gradient interpretation. The Discussion (pp. 13–14) assumes the Raman susceptibility "averages over all layers" and needs the signal from the one or two layers next to Au to be a substantial part of the total. But at 632.8 nm the standing-wave field has a near-node at an optically thick Au surface, so for a 2.5–4 nm flake the layers next to Au should be weighted far less than the top layer, in both excitation and collection. Flakes on Al2O3 (50–100 nm above Au) sit in a very different standing-wave pattern. The ×25–×50 weaker ac signals of 6L and 4L on Au compared with the Al2O3 flakes (scale factors in Figs. 3e and 4e versus ×3 in Fig. 3c; colour bars 0.57 against 52.6) are consistent with this. Two consequences follow: the interfacial layers that supposedly have Tc suppressed by about 150 K may contribute little to the spectrum, so the low-temperature end of the transition (about 180 K) is poorly constrained; and if those layers are weakly weighted, the observed broadening needs either a larger perturbed region or a different cause. The authors should give a transfer-matrix calculation of the layer-resolved Raman weight (incident × scattered field intensity) for each stack, including the Al2O3/Au stack beneath the Al2O3 flakes; use it as the layer weights in the toy model of Supplementary Note 4; and show that the measured lineshapes are consistent with the extent of the interface region they propose.

5. The vertical Tc gradient is inferred rather than tested, and the gradient model is not shown to fit the data better than alternatives. Lateral inhomogeneity within the laser spot would also produce a broadened, averaged transition: thickness steps or wrinkles, bubbles, strain from the roughness of the polycrystalline or amorphous Au, or Au–flake contact varying across the flake. The spot size is not given and no spatial map is shown. The gradient picture also has internal tensions. If one or two interfacial layers are perturbed, the mixed fraction is about 2/4 for 4L but only about 2/6 for 6L, yet both flakes show the same ±40 K width and similar kink spacing. The model assumes the layers far from Au have Tc "closer to room temperature" (p. 13), but the dominant top layer of 4L/Au begins its transition at about 220–260 K (first kink, Fig. 3t), well below 4L/Al2O3 (280 K), which suggests suppression across the whole flake rather than a gradient. It is also unclear which Tc the toy model assigns to the top layers of 4L/Au: bulk, the 4L/Al2O3 value, or a free parameter. The authors should give the spot size and show Raman maps, or at least spectra at several positions on the 4L/Au and 6L/Au flakes at temperatures inside the broadened range, and check whether the width depends on position; explain how the equal 4L and 6L widths fit the gradient picture; state the Tc assigned to the top layers; show that the toy model (Supplementary Note 4) reproduces Fig. 3t and Fig. 4o quantitatively with physically motivated weights (see concern 4); and present a 3L or 5L dependence or at least discuss why a gradient fits better than uniform suppression plus lateral spread. The abstract's description of the effect as "atomic-scale" should be stated as an inference from the 4L/6L comparison, with its assumptions, rather than as a measured fact.

6. The absence of the quasi-elastic peak in 4L/Au may be a sensitivity limit. The statement that the quasi-elastic peak is "suppressed beyond detection" and that this "affirms" its excitonic origin (p. 15) uses non-detection (p. 12) as positive evidence. But Fig. 4t shows χ''(10 cm^-1) at ×50 scaling, the ac signal of 4L/Au is 50–100 times weaker than that of 4L/Al2O3, and in exactly the 6L and 4L Au datasets a Rayleigh leakage peak at 15 cm^-1 was removed by interpolation (Methods), close to the 10 cm^-1 reading and the filter edge. The authors should show the raw, uncorrected low-energy spectra for the 4L and 6L Au flakes with a noise estimate; give an upper bound on the quasi-elastic amplitude normalized to an internal phonon reference in the same spectrum (for example the integrated Ag(m2) or B2g(o1) intensity); show that a peak of the expected relative size would have been detectable at the achieved signal-to-noise; and demonstrate that the Rayleigh removal does not affect the 10 cm^-1 point.

7. Sample temperature and laser heating are not quantified, and the high-temperature setup is not described. The Methods say the actual temperature was checked from Stokes/anti-Stokes ratios at about 1 mW, but no values are given. The two stacks differ in absorption (metal mirror against 50–100 nm Al2O3 over Au) and in thermal anchoring: a few-layer flake on amorphous ALD Al2O3 is much more thermally isolated than one in direct contact with Au. A heating offset of 10–30 K that differs between flakes would shift the apparent Tc by an amount comparable to the 40 K suppression of 4L/Al2O3. The Methods also describe a cryostat only "for measurements below 300 K"; it is not stated how the 300–400 K spectra were taken, or whether a change of setup or window calibration occurs near 300 K, exactly where the 7L/Al2O3 and 18L/Au transitions (about 320 K) lie. The authors should report the Stokes/anti-Stokes temperature against nominal temperature for each flake (at least the two 4L flakes); show the power dependence of a Tc marker (for example the Ag(m5) intensity near Tc) for 4L/Al2O3 and 4L/Au; give the spot size; describe the high-temperature setup and show that the data join continuously across 300 K; and state that the Bose correction of Eq. (2) uses the calibrated temperature.

8. The transition width is defined in an ad hoc way. The ±40 K broadening for the 6L and 4L flakes on Au (pp. 9–10) comes from the spacing between the first and second kinks in the Ag(m4) position: 100 K on Au versus 60 K for the sharp flakes, giving (100 − 60) = 40 K. This assumes the second kink lies a fixed, intrinsic 60 K below Tc (a spacing taken from only three flakes) and that its extra separation maps directly onto the distribution of Tc. Nothing physical ties the second kink to Tc by a fixed offset, and the offset could itself depend on doping or strain. The kinks in Figs. 3s and 3t are also described as "broadened and less defined", on peak positions quantized at 1.3 cm^-1. A more robust measure is the temperature dependence of the integrated intensity of Ag(m4) and Ag(m5) in the ac channel, which scales with the square of the monoclinic order parameter. The authors should fit it (and/or −1/q(T)) with an order-parameter form convolved with a Gaussian distribution of Tc, applied the same way to all five flakes, report the fitted centres and widths with uncertainties, and justify or drop the "second kink" argument.

9. Temperature smoothing could create or exaggerate the broadening. The 2D maps are resampled on a 5 K grid and smoothed with a Gaussian of 10–15 K standard deviation in temperature (Methods, p. 19), comparable to the 10 K measurement spacing near Tc and to the widths of the sharp transitions. Fig. 3k–o are second derivatives of these smoothed maps, and it is not clear whether the peak positions in Fig. 3p–t come from the smoothed maps or the raw spectra. For the noisy 4L/Au data, smoothing combined with a second derivative can turn a weak, noisy onset into an apparently gradual one. The authors should state whether Fig. 3p–t uses raw or smoothed spectra, extract peak positions from fits to the raw spectra at each measured temperature (with uncertainties) and show them alongside Fig. 3p–t, and state whether the same smoothing was applied to every sample.

10. The electronic Tc for 4L/Au is not measured independently. Because the quasi-elastic peak is undetectable in 4L/Au, Tc^avg ≈ 220 ± 46 K is obtained by shifting the 6L/Au −1/q curve by about 30 K and borrowing the 6L width (p. 12). This point is then plotted in Fig. 5b as a second, independent electronic estimate and supports the statement that the structural and electronic transitions agree, which is partly circular for this flake. The authors should either extract a Tc and width for 4L/Au directly from −1/q(T) (for example its inflection point and width) with a stated criterion also applied to all other flakes, or mark this point clearly as derived in Fig. 5b and leave it out of the argument that the structural and electronic transitions agree.

11. Charge transfer is asserted but neither estimated nor measured. Neither the amount nor the sign of charge transfer from Au is estimated, and the Au work function (polycrystalline, on Cr) is not compared with that of Ta2NiSe5 (about 5.4 eV, cited). No doping-related observable is shown: no phonon frequency or line-asymmetry shift at high temperature relative to Al2O3, no change in the electronic background, no XPS/UPS core-level or valence-band shift, no Kelvin-probe measurement. Without at least an order-of-magnitude estimate of the transferred carrier density, set against the doping levels at which K dosing or substitution suppress Tc, charge transfer cannot be weighed against screening, yet it is part of the argument for an excitonic mechanism. The authors should give such an estimate (for example from the work-function difference and an interfacial capacitance) and any available Raman, XPS/UPS or Kelvin-probe evidence. A second metal with a different work function (for example Pt, which the authors propose) would test the proposed doping direction.

12. The 40 K suppression in 4L/Al2O3 is unexplained, and the thickness series is not matched. The text calls 4L/Al2O3 "moderately perturbed" with no notable interface effect, yet Tc drops by about 40 K between 7L and 4L. 15. The structural Tc of the sharp samples relies on a leakage mode. Tc is defined by a kink in the position of Ag(o3)*, a leakage of an aa mode caused by polarizer misalignment (pp. 7, 9). The authors should confirm the Tc values with a criterion based only on intrinsic ac modes, for example the onset temperature of the Ag(m5) intensity, for all five flakes.
