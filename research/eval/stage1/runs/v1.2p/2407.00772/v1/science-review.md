A. Must address

1. The 1/√F scaling of the window-iii rise time does not, on its own, show excitonic correlations above Tc, and the fit cannot tell it apart from other forms (Fig. 4a,b,e; pp. 9–10; abstract; Discussion p. 11). The claim of short-range excitonic correlations in the normal state rests entirely on the fluence dependence of the "initial response time" of Se peak ② (window iii): about four fluences at 23 K (0.2, 0.6, 1.8, 2.9 mJ/cm²) and four at 300 K (0.2, 0.8, 1.5, 1.9 mJ/cm²), fitted to τ ~ a/√F + τ0 with a fixed exponent and a free offset τ0 = 26 fs, attributed ad hoc to the "time needed for creating mobile Se 4p holes". The argument runs: τ follows the plasmon period, the plasmon frequency goes as √n, and n ∝ F. Several problems remain. (a) Alternative explanations: a faster build-up of Se 4p holes near the valence-band top at higher excitation density is generic in any semimetal or narrow-gap semiconductor, which TiSe2 is above Tc. Electron–electron thermalisation and the cascade of hot carriers created far from E_F by the 500–1000 nm pump, density-dependent screening of the free-carrier plasma, band-gap renormalisation, state filling and Pauli blocking, saturation of the probed window, or a fluence-dependent shift of t0 absorbed into the rise parameter would all shorten the rise without excitons. (b) The fit is not discriminating: with two free parameters over about one decade of fluence, and a trend set largely by the single 0.2 mJ/cm² point at each temperature (about 150–190 fs, with error bars of roughly ±20–30 fs), 1/√F cannot be separated from 1/F, logarithmic, linear-decreasing or saturating forms; the offset τ0 in particular removes most of the distinction. The data also scatter: the 300 K points are non-monotonic (about 65 fs at 1.5 mJ/cm², about 87 fs at 1.9 mJ/cm²), and the 23 K point at 2.9 mJ/cm² (about 81 fs) lies above the curve. (c) The plasmon picture itself predicts a temperature difference: the screening density in a semimetal is the total density n0(T) + αF, not the photoexcited density alone, and the equilibrium carrier density changes substantially across the CDW transition and with temperature. The 23 K and 300 K curves should therefore differ; that they collapse onto one curve argues against, not for, the mechanism invoked. (d) Rough estimates suggest plasmon periods well below 50 fs at the photoexcited densities involved, so the 100–190 fs scale is not obviously consistent with plasmon-mediated exciton breaking. The phonon-dominated windows (i, iv) give a fluence-independent time, but they are not a control for free-carrier kinetics in a phonon-free window. The authors should: report fits with and without τ0 and compare competing functional forms with a goodness-of-fit or information criterion; fit the 23 K and 300 K data separately and report prefactors and offsets with uncertainties; add fluences, especially below 0.6 mJ/cm², where the forms differ most; give a quantitative estimate of the photoexcited (and total) carrier density and plasmon period at each fluence and compare it with the measured times, as in ref. 40; provide a null measurement in which excitonic correlations are absent or much weaker, analysed identically (an isostructural non-CDW compound such as TiS2 or ZrSe2, a metallic 1T dichalcogenide, electron-doped or Cu-intercalated TiSe2, or TiSe2 well above 300 K if reachable); and, if feasible, use the TDDFT already in the paper to simulate the rise time of this feature at several fluences in a model without excitonic interactions. Without such controls, "direct time-domain evidence for incoherent excitonic correlations" (abstract) should be softened to "consistent with".

B. Should address

2. Window iii is used both as a reporter of long-range order (Fig. 3e) and as a probe blind to long-range coherence (Fig. 4), and the two uses conflict. On p. 9 the Se peak ② transient amplitude "shows a clear onset right at Tc" and Fig. 3e is described as "quasi-constant above Tc"; this differs from the ARPES observables in Fig. 1e,f, which show a fluctuation tail above Tc. On p. 10 the same feature is said to be "agnostic about the existence of long-range phase coherence among the excitons" because it reflects localized dipole transitions, which is what justifies reading the 300 K fluence dependence as short-range correlations. If window iii were sensitive to local excitonic correlations persisting above Tc, its amplitude should show a tail above Tc as the replica weight does; if its amplitude is set by long-range order, the paper must explain why its kinetics would be insensitive to that order. The authors should reconcile the two readings with a concrete model of what the window-iii amplitude and rise time each measure, plot Fig. 3e and Fig. 1e,f on a common normalised temperature axis, and show how amplitude and rise time behave together versus temperature at fixed fluence (see concern 6).

3. The order-parameter-like onset in Fig. 3d,e may partly reflect spectroscopic and extrinsic effects rather than the proposed Se 4p hole mechanism (Fig. 3; Fig. 1o–q; pp. 8–9). The temperature scan is a single delay (200 fs) in a fixed energy window at fixed incident fluence (0.2 mJ/cm²). (a) Phonon phase: window ii carries a strong 6 THz A1g oscillation (period about 167 fs, Fig. 2d), so the 200 fs value samples a phonon phase, while the phonon amplitude, frequency and damping change across Tc. (b) Rising edge: at 0.2 mJ/cm² the window-iii rise time is about 150–190 fs (Fig. 4e), so 200 fs is still on the rising edge, and any temperature dependence of the rise time or of time zero during the ramp feeds directly into Fig. 3e. (c) Edge shift: the window-iii transient is derivative-like (negative lobe near 53.2 eV, positive near 53.4 eV at 25 K, Fig. 3c), integrated over a fixed 0.29 eV window (53.04–53.33 eV), while the static peak ② shifts by about 40 meV between 25 and 310 K (Fig. 1p); a fixed-window integral of a derivative-shaped signal is very sensitive to such a shift, and a larger magnitude at 25 K could reflect a sharper static peak rather than "additional Se 4p holes created via the breaking of condensed excitons". (d) Absorbed fluence: the optical constants of TiSe2 in the 1.2–2.5 eV pump range change across Tc, so the absorbed energy density, and every transient amplitude, may change at Tc for non-specific reasons. The authors should show full delay traces at several temperatures across Tc (e.g. 25, 150, 190–200, 210, 250, 300 K) and plot fitted incoherent and phonon amplitudes (or the plateau amplitude) versus temperature; fit the Se ② transient lineshape to peak shift, broadening and amplitude change, or integrate in a window that tracks the edge; normalise the amplitudes to the absorbed fluence at each temperature using measured or literature reflectivity and transmission; and show that a non-specific, carrier-dominated window does not display the same onset.

4. The "absence" of a static signature is not established at a sensitivity comparable to the transient measurement (abstract; pp. 5–6; Fig. 1j–l, o–q). The static XAS and XPS data are reduced to fitted peak positions with roughly 10–20 meV scatter, while the transient effect is an integrated transmission change of order 10⁻³ in specific windows. Changes in lineshape, width or integrated intensity, especially in the broad Ti 38–53 eV continuum that the paper later finds most sensitive, are not analysed, and no sensitivity estimate is given. Fig. 1o also appears to show a slope change in the 150–200 K range. The headline contrast ("equilibrium measurements … showed no observable signals") is thus partly a contrast between analyses rather than between techniques. The authors should show static difference spectra A(E,T) − A(E,300–310 K), normalised like the transient data and integrated over windows ii and iii in the format of Fig. 3d,e with the noise floor stated; report widths and integrated intensities versus temperature; show Ti 3p and Se 3d XPS widths and asymmetry across Tc; and fit or differentiate the peak-position curves for slope changes at Tc. If the static integrated windows also show an onset, the claim that time resolution is required must be revised.

5. Sample heating and the true temperature of the pumped spot are not quantified (Methods, pp. 18–20; Figs. 3–4). The samples are free-standing flakes under 100 nm thick (about 400 µm wide) on Cu TEM grids, pumped at 1 kHz with up to 2.9 mJ/cm², in a cryostat without radiation shielding, at a base temperature of about 20 K. Thermal contact through a TEM grid is poor. The Methods state that pre-time-zero signals from "millisecond cumulative heating" were subtracted and "do not change the conclusions", but this is asserted, not shown. A heating offset would shift the apparent Tc in Fig. 3d,e and make the "23 K" and "300 K" labels in Fig. 4 uncertain, particularly across the fluence series where heating increases with fluence; a 23 K high-fluence spot sitting above Tc would undermine the below/above-Tc comparison in Fig. 4e. In addition, the pump penetration depth at 750 nm is comparable to or smaller than the flake thickness, so the transmission probe averages over a depth-graded excitation, complicating any scaling with incident F. The authors should estimate the steady-state and per-pulse temperature rise (absorbed energy, heat capacity, conduction to the grid), for example using the static peak shifts in Fig. 1o–q as an in-situ thermometer on the pre-time-zero spectra; show the pre-time-zero signal versus temperature and fluence; check that the Fig. 3 onset does not shift with fluence or between warming and cooling ramps; and give the excitation depth profile and discuss its effect on the fitted rise times.

8. Fluence calibration and the assumed proportionality n ∝ F are not justified, and the fluence regime of Figs. 2–3 is ambiguous (pp. 7–8, 10; Methods p. 18). With a 3.4 fs pulse, 1.7–2.9 mJ/cm² corresponds to peak intensities of order 10¹² W/cm², where multiphoton absorption and saturation are plausible and would break the linearity at the heart of the √n argument. Thresholds taken from the literature (refs. 14, 30–34) were measured at different wavelengths, thicknesses and geometries, so comparing incident fluences is not meaningful. The text calls 0.2 mJ/cm² "commensurate with a fluence regime where the CDW order and excitonic correlations are close to be fully suppressed" (p. 8) and 1.7 mJ/cm² an order of magnitude above the melting threshold (p. 7); it is then unclear whether the 0.2 mJ/cm² data in Fig. 3 are in the perturbative regime needed to read Fig. 3d,e as a probe of equilibrium order. 9. The calculations supporting the Ti "many-body continuum" assignment are underspecified and used only qualitatively (pp. 5–6; Methods p. 20; Fig. 1g; Supplementary Note 1, Fig. S3). The broad 38–53 eV feature is assigned to valence excitations accompanying the core transition, based on TDDFT and ref. 22. The delayed, broadened M2,3 edge of early 3d metals is also commonly attributed to 3p–3d exchange and multiplet effects (giant resonance with super-Coster–Kronig decay), in which case window-ii changes would reflect local 3d occupation or screening rather than CDW-related valence excitations. The Methods describe only ground-state PBE DFT; missing are the TDDFT kernel, whether Ti 3p is treated as valence, propagation parameters, core-hole and spin–orbit treatment, and whether the 24-atom CDW cell is relaxed or experimental, with what distortion and resulting gap. PBE is known to misdescribe the TiSe2 band overlap and does not by itself stabilise the gap seen in ARPES. No calculated spectrum is compared with Figs. 2–3, nor is it shown whether TDDFT reproduces the sign reversal of the continuum between phases. The authors should describe the TDDFT in the Methods, discuss the multiplet alternative, test sensitivity to the functional (hybrid or DFT+U), and, if feasible, compute the change in absorption in windows ii and iii between normal and CDW structures or for a photoexcited distribution.

10. The novelty relative to prior XUV transient absorption on TiSe2 is not delineated (Introduction; abstract; Discussion p. 11). Ref. 22 (Heinrich et al., PRX 2023) reported electronic and structural CDW fingerprints in XUV transient absorption of 1T-TiSe2 at the same Ti and Se M edges, and the present paper relies on it for the static temperature shift and the many-body continuum assignment; ref. 23 reported temperature-dependent hybridisation in core-level spectroscopy. The claims of "the first demonstration where an attosecond core-level technique … can simultaneously probe low-energy phase transitions" and that core-level spectroscopies are "not traditionally regarded as an appropriate probe for long-range order" overlap with this work. Transient optical and photoemission probes are also well known to show order-parameter-like onsets at Tc in TiSe2. The authors should state precisely what ref. 22 observed (including any temperature dependence across Tc, amplitude-mode or Ti-edge signatures) and what is new here (few-fs resolution, simultaneous edges, temperature scans through Tc, normal-state fluence dependence), explain what element-specific core-level data add beyond optical and photoemission probes, and moderate the priority claims.

12. The inference that excitons drive the short-range in-plane order goes beyond the data (pp. 5, 9, 11). The paper frames the question as whether short-range order above Tc is "purely phononic" or excitonic, and the Discussion concludes that exciton correlations are "instrumental in forming the short-range 2D order in-plane before the long-range CDW is formed". Even accepting the 1/√F argument, the data show at most that some local electron–hole correlation exists above Tc; the measurement has no momentum or structural sensitivity and cannot tie these correlations to the 2×2 short-range order, nor show that they drive rather than accompany lattice fluctuations. The authors should link the correlations to the short-range order (e.g. a temperature dependence of τ0 or the prefactor tracking diffuse-scattering intensity) or restrict the conclusion.
