A. Must address

1. The headline "elevenfold enhancement of 3D superconductivity" compares two measurements made in different field geometries, so the 3D character of the stress-induced transition is not established. Section II (p. 3) and Fig. 2b say the 0 GPa curve was measured before mounting, in a dc field of 0.5 mT parallel to the CuO2 planes, "such that the induced shielding currents flow between the layers", and that this geometry is what makes it sensitive to 3D superconductivity (Tc,mid ≈ 3 K). All finite-stress curves are in situ ac data with the excitation "applied predominantly along the c-axis" (Methods A). That geometry drives in-plane shielding currents, which 2D superconductivity can also carry, and the authors' own resistivity puts 2D superconductivity near 20–25 K at zero stress (Figs. 3e, 4a). Part of the jump from 3 K at 0 GPa to about 27–28 K at 0.09 GPa may therefore come from switching from an interlayer-sensitive to an in-plane-sensitive measurement (and from mounting strain) rather than from restored 3D coherence. The same problem affects the "3D SC" region of Fig. 4a and the "Tc,3D" line of Fig. 1d. The authors should measure in situ ac susceptibility at 0 GPa in the stress cell, with the same coil and excitation, after cooldown with the sample glued; specify the coil geometry and field direction relative to the crystal axes; state which curve in Fig. 2b comes from which measurement and how each is normalised; and justify identifying the in situ Tc,mid with the 3D transition, for example by showing that the in situ zero-stress midpoint matches the dc H ∥ ab value of about 3 K, or by measuring with the field in the plane, or c-axis resistivity, under stress.

2. The superconducting volume fraction is never measured, and the data at 0.09 GPa contradict the paper's own percolation picture, in which superconductivity lives in the non-magnetic (LTLO) regions. The in situ susceptibility in Fig. 2b appears normalised to −1 at every stress; full diamagnetic shielding measures screening by a connected path and can come from a thin percolating network or surface layer, so it is not a volume fraction. At 0.09 GPa, V_M at base temperature is about 0.84 (Figs. 2d, 4b), so only about 16% of the sample is non-magnetic, and V_M just below Tc,mid is still about 0.75; yet the sample already shows a sharp, complete diamagnetic transition with Tc,mid ≈ 27–28 K, roughly 80% of the final enhancement. The text (p. 7) states that a non-magnetic fraction of about 20% at 0.1 GPa "is likely insufficient for percolation" and that Tc saturates only when 1 − V_M approaches 40%. The data cannot currently distinguish superconductivity confined to non-magnetic regions, superconductivity coexisting microscopically with static stripe order, and filamentary superconductivity; these lead to opposite conclusions about competition and undercut the picture of uniform d-wave patches and the claims of "bulk" and "spatially uniform" superconductivity. The authors should report the absolute (unnormalised) shielding signal, or the normalisation procedure with a demagnetisation correction, at each stress; measure the superfluid density and superconducting volume fraction with field-cooled TF-μSR above Hc1 (or the field-cooled diamagnetic shift) at several stresses (for example 0, 0.09 and 0.23 GPa), which their cell and beamline allow, and test whether 1/λ² tracks 1 − V_M; and rewrite the percolation argument so that it is consistent with the 0.09 GPa data.

3. Suppression of the LTT phase at x = 0.125, and the formation of an "LTLO" phase, are inferred only from the height of a normal-state resistive hump, with no structural measurement under stress, yet the abstract and conclusion state that LTT suppression is "essential" for optimal superconductivity and that the enhanced Tc lives in a stress-induced LTLO phase (p. 7). In LBCO near 1/8 the LTT transition coincides with charge-stripe ordering, so a smaller hump (Fig. 3b,e) is equally consistent with weaker charge-order pinning or a smaller charge-ordered volume inside a structure that is still LTT, a shift of T_LTT, or a change in transport anisotropy under stress. The hump in Fig. 3b sits at about 35–40 K, well below T_LTT (about 55 K), and its height is set by both the upturn below T_LTT and the superconducting-fluctuation downturn; since stress raises the onset to about 46 K, the hump is cut off by superconductivity and ΔR_LTT would fall even if the LTT fraction were unchanged. The data also show a tension with the claim: Tc,mid saturates near 35 K by about 0.15–0.23 GPa (Figs. 2b, 4a), while the x = 0.125 hump is still 50–70% of its ambient value at those stresses and vanishes only near 0.5 GPa (Figs. 3e, 4b), which argues against bulk 3D superconductivity requiring full LTT suppression. The authors should provide direct structural evidence at x = 0.125 under comparable stress (for example the LTT-specific (100)/(010) superlattice reflections by X-ray or neutron diffraction) or compare quantitatively with the stress-dependent X-ray diffraction at x = 1/8 in Ref. 30; at minimum track the T_LTT anomaly in dR/dT against stress; discuss explicitly why Tc,mid saturates while the hump is still large; and otherwise phrase the conclusions in terms of the resistive anomaly rather than the structural phase.

B. Should address

4. Stress inhomogeneity in the μSR/susceptibility cell is not considered, and on its own it could produce the fractional magnetic volume that the paper interprets as intrinsic phase coexistence. The crystal is a 7 × 5.1 × 0.3 mm³ plate glued with Stycast 2850FT to a Ti holder, with only a 4 × 5.1 mm² area exposed between the jaws (Fig. 2a; Methods A), so the free length is shorter than the width; in this geometry stress near the epoxy-bonded ends, the free edges and the centre differs strongly and the thin plate can bend or buckle. The stress is quoted as force over the nominal 1.53 mm² cross-section (480 N → 0.32 GPa), using a force-sensor calibration from earlier work on other samples (Refs. 6, 41; 1 µV ≈ 30 N) with no stated uncertainty, and no stress error bars are shown anywhere. A distribution of local stresses would naturally give some regions without static order and others with nearly unchanged T_so and B_int (Figs. 2d, 4c), and the long low-temperature tails of V_M(T) at 0.16–0.23 GPa up to about 35–40 K look like this. If so, the factor-2.5 reduction of V_M, the LTT/LTLO domain coexistence and the percolation argument (p. 7) would partly reflect high- and low-stress regions rather than an intrinsic mixed state, and the apparent factor of about 2 between the stress scales of the μSR cell and the FC100 cell could have the same origin. The authors should give a finite-element calculation of the stress and strain distribution for this exact sample, holder and epoxy geometry, weighted by the area the muon beam sees through the hematite masks, and quote the fraction of the illuminated volume within, say, 20% of the nominal stress; check for bending; estimate the stress uncertainty; ideally verify with a direct strain measurement (strain gauge or digital image correlation) or a thinner, longer sample; and discuss whether the fractional V_M could reflect a stress distribution rather than intrinsic coexistence.

5. Data from two stress apparatuses and different crystals are combined on one stress axis without cross-calibration, and the sample basis is described inconsistently. The Introduction (pp. 2–3) says μSR, AC susceptibility and resistivity were "performed on the same crystal", but Methods A describe a 7 × 5.1 × 0.3 mm³ crystal in the piezoelectric μSR cell and separately mounted crystals, of unstated dimensions, in a Razorbill FC100 in a PPMS. Fig. 4a overlays T_so and Tc,mid (μSR cell) with Tc,zero (FC100), and Fig. 4b plots V_M and the LTT peak height on a common σ axis and calls them "directly correlated". Nominal stress can differ substantially from the stress the sample carries because of epoxy compliance, different free lengths and aspect ratios, and differential thermal contraction, so the tracking in Fig. 4b and the convergence of Tc,zero with Tc,mid near 0.15 GPa in Fig. 4a may partly reflect calibration rather than physics. The authors should state unambiguously which crystal (and growth batch) was measured in which cell; give the resistivity-sample dimensions, epoxy thickness and free length, and the stress uncertainty for those samples; provide a common reference for the two stress scales, for example Tc,mid or the resistive Tc or hump of one crystal measured in both cells, or a direct strain measurement in each; include an estimate of stress-transmission efficiency, for example from finite-element modelling; and justify plotting the two data sets on one axis.

6. The extraction of V_M in the presence of a large cell background is not documented, although the 0.97 → 0.38 reduction is a primary result. Methods A state that only about 50% of muons stop in the sample; the rest stop in the hematite masks (rapid depolarisation, which looks "magnetic" in weak TF) or in the Ti holder and epoxy (non-magnetic, precessing at the applied field). Yet V_M reaches about 0.97 at 0 GPa (Fig. 2d), so a background must have been subtracted, whereas Eq. (5), V_M = 1 − P_TF(0,T), assumes none. Methods D say "baseline asymmetry shifts were corrected by allowing P_TF(t) to be adjusted for each temperature", which suggests a temperature-dependent free parameter that can move V_M, and any change of background or stopping fraction with stress (sample, mask or epoxy displacement as the cell deforms) would appear as a change in V_M. The authors should give the fit model with its background term, the background fraction and asymmetries at each stress and how they were determined (for example from a run above T_so at each stress or with a dummy holder), state which parameters in Eqs. (3)–(5) were fixed and which free across temperature and stress, show raw (not A0-normalised) weak-TF amplitudes above and below T_so at 0 and 0.23 GPa, check V_M from the ZF fits (Eq. 3) against the weak-TF values, and state whether the 0 GPa μSR point was measured in the cell after mounting.

7. The headline Tc numbers mix 2D and 3D criteria and resistive and magnetic onsets, and the claim of "the highest bulk Tc reported in the 214 family" (p. 7; Fig. 4d) is not supported as stated. The abstract's rise "from 5 K to 37 K under 0.5 GPa" combines the zero-stress susceptibility onset (5 K; the text gives Tc,mid = 3 K) with the in-plane zero-resistance temperature at 0.56 GPa, which the authors themselves describe as the 2D transition (p. 5); the highest 3D Tc,mid actually measured is about 35 K at about 0.23 GPa, because susceptibility was not measured above that stress, and it lies below optimal LSCO. The 46 K "onset" at 0.56 GPa has no stated criterion; in Fig. 3b the downturn begins on the shoulder of the residual hump, and unstressed LBCO-1/8 already shows a resistive drop near 40 K from 2D correlations, so the onset depends on how the normal-state background is defined. Fig. 4d compares this resistive onset with LSCO data from a single 1989 study (Ref. 32) without matched criteria or sample form, and the 214 comparison omits oxygen-intercalated La2CuO4+δ (Tc about 40–45 K) and compressively strained LSCO films (about 45–50 K). The authors should quote stress-matched numbers with stated criteria (3D Tc,mid from χ; Tc,zero and onset from ρ, each with its stress) separately throughout, including the abstract and title; define the onset criterion (for example intersection with an extrapolated normal-state background or a fixed percentage drop) and apply the same one to the LSCO reference data; state in Fig. 4d the criterion and sample form for each data set, which stress is plotted for each doping, and use consistent symbols; measure χ_AC at about 0.5 GPa if possible; restrict "bulk" to the susceptibility (and ideally superfluid-density) results; and discuss the other 214 cuprates or soften the "highest in the 214 family" and "optimal" statements.

10. The zero-stress reference states are uncertain and differ from known ambient behaviour. In Figs. 3d–f the zero-stress Tc,zero is about 21 K (x = 0.115), 23 K (x = 0.125) and 20 K (x = 0.135), and Fig. 4a puts x = 0.125 at about 25 K; so at "0 GPa" the 1/8 sample has the highest Tc,zero of the three, contradicting its description as the lowest-ambient-Tc composition, and well above the in-plane zero-resistance temperatures usually reported for unstressed LBCO-1/8 crystals (about 10–16 K). This points to residual strain from epoxy mounting and differential thermal contraction against the Ti holder or cell, a slightly different composition, or a different resistance criterion; Fig. 3 already shows small tensile values (−0.03, −0.01 GPa), suggesting the nominal zero is not strain-free. Any of these affects the stress axis and the doping comparison. The authors should state the Tc,zero criterion (threshold or noise floor), show ρ(T) of each crystal free-standing or before mounting, explain how zero stress was established after cooldown in each cell, and discuss the resulting uncertainty in the zero of stress.

11. Several interpretive statements go beyond what is measured. (i) "Spin-stripe order remains long range" (abstract, p. 4): μSR coherent oscillations show a well-defined local field distribution, not a correlation length; long-range order needs neutron or X-ray data. (ii) The conversion of static stripes to "dynamically fluctuating stripes" in the non-magnetic regions (p. 7, abstract) is inferred from the absence of a signal "invisible to μSR on its characteristic timescale", which is equally consistent with the stripes simply melting; no dynamical probe is reported. (iii) Stress "enhances pairing", acts "primarily at the level of phase coherence" via restored interlayer Josephson coupling, and shifts the system from a PDW-dominated regime to a uniform d-wave state: no c-axis transport, anisotropy, gap or pairing-symmetry measurement is presented. (iv) The title speaks of the "stripe state" and the Introduction promises to track "the evolution of the charge stripe order", but charge order is not measured. The authors should either add discriminating evidence (the stress dependence of λ_L/λ_T or of the relaxation rate λ_nm of the non-magnetic fraction, or LF-μSR, as signs of slow fluctuations; a diffraction correlation length; c-axis resistivity under stress or the ratio of resistive to susceptibility transitions to test interlayer frustration; loss of the 2D/3D separation as a PDW signature), or recast these points as hypotheses, remove "demonstrate" from the abstract, and tone down the title and abstract.

12. Reproducibility, reversibility and the stress protocol are not documented. Each result rests on one crystal per doping and technique; there is no evidence that Tc, V_M or the resistive hump recover when stress is released, so plastic deformation, cracking (which could also contribute to a resistive drop), epoxy slipping or creep at 0.5–0.6 GPa cannot be ruled out. The paper does not state at which temperature the stress was changed (stress applied above T_LTT selects twin and LTLO domains differently from stress applied at base temperature) or whether the crystals were thermally cycled between stress points. The authors should state the protocol and the number of samples per doping, show at least one unloaded or partially unloaded (return-to-zero) measurement after the highest stress with any hysteresis, and repeat the key x = 0.125 result on a second crystal.
