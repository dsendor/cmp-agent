A. Must address

1. The headline "disorder mean free path exceeding 200 µm" is not determined by the TMF analysis as constructed (abstract; Fig. 3j, which the text calls 3i; Methods Eqs. 3–6; Fig. S7b). Eq. 5 gives ℓ_mfp = −(πd/2)/ln[A(T)/A_0], with d ≈ 5 µm and A_0 defined as the average of the four lowest-temperature peak areas. This normalization divides out any temperature-independent scattering, including the disorder scattering the abstract refers to, so ℓ_mfp → ∞ as T → 0 by construction whatever the actual disorder level. What Eq. 5 returns is the temperature-dependent part of the scattering rate, which makes fitting Matthiessen's rule with a finite ℓ_0 internally inconsistent. Numerically, ℓ = 200 µm corresponds to A/A_0 ≈ exp(−7.85/200) ≈ 0.96, a 4% drop in peak area. Below ~30 K the extracted value is therefore set by percent-level changes that are comparable to the scatter in Fig. S7b and to the effect of analysis choices: the integration window (dashed lines in Fig. 3e–h), the linear baseline under the peak (whose shape visibly changes between 2 K and 198 K), the linear-in-B Hall subtraction, and possible thermal broadening of the lineshape beyond the window. Eq. 5 also assumes the only T-dependent loss is bulk scattering along a semicircular path of length πd/2, neglecting T-dependent boundary specularity (p = 0.5 in the simulations), diffuse loss at the edge and contact transmission. The 1R–6R focusing peaks all have roughly the same total path length (~πd/2), so they demonstrate boundary specularity rather than a mean free path far above 8 µm; the negative bend resistance (Fig. S6c) supports ℓ of order 10 µm. Fig. 3j has no error bars, and the T range and whether ℓ_0 is free or fixed in the Matthiessen fit are not stated, though α = 1.56 ± 0.05 depends on them. The Methods concede the method "is not well suited to determine the mean-free path at lowest temperatures" and that "we can only note that ℓ_0 ≥ 200 µm", but neither caveat reaches the abstract or main text, and the comparison with "best-in-class" mono- and bilayer devices [44–46] compares quantities obtained by different methods and at different densities. The authors should obtain an absolute rather than self-normalized bound, for example by fitting the absolute low-T focusing amplitude and the 1R/2R/… peak ratios to the Boltzmann solver (Fig. 3b) as a function of ℓ_mr with p fixed independently, by mapping the measured low-T negative square resistance (about −6.5 Ω, Fig. S6c) onto the simulated R(ℓ_mr, ℓ_ee) map (Fig. S9a), or by focusing over several contact separations. They should add error bars to ℓ_mfp(T) propagated from A_peak, A_0, the window and the baseline; show robustness to those choices; state the lowest T at which ℓ_mfp is resolved rather than bounded; state the Matthiessen fit range and treatment of ℓ_0; and state quantitatively the agreement with the bend-resistance sign change. Failing an absolute bound, the abstract and main text should say that only the temperature-dependent scattering length exceeds 200 µm below 30 K, and the comparison with Refs. 44–46 should be dropped or qualified by how those values were obtained.

2. All electronic and magnetic evidence of device quality comes from a single device, while the claims are made about the method (abstract, title, conclusion; Figs. 2–4; Fig. S5). The capacitance map (Fig. 2b), nSOT magnetization (Fig. 2c), TMF (Fig. 3), current imaging (Fig. 4a–f), channel and square transport (Fig. 4g,j) and Fig. S6 all appear to come from the one 13-layer, 740 µm² device of Fig. 2a / Fig. S5a–c, and the imaging covers only its central 10 × 10 µm² (100 µm²). The other devices in Fig. S5 (660–1330 µm²), including the >1300 µm² device cited in the abstract, are shown only by optical and IR images taken after transfer, not repeated after nanofabrication, with no transport or capacitance data. Yet the abstract and conclusion say the approach "enable[s] highly uniform devices" with ~90% yield, presents ultrahigh mobility as a property of the method, and claims to move "standard metrics of RMG device quality—area, yield, and uniformity—into a qualitatively new regime". One device shows such a device can exist; it cannot show that quality is a property of the process rather than of one good flake, nor give the device-to-device spread, and device quality rather than domain area is what the community cares about. The authors should report basic quality metrics for several more cryo-shock devices, measured at the same (n_e, D) where possible: a capacitance or R(n_e, D) phase diagram with the known spin/valley-ordered features, TMF or negative bend resistance, and a mobility or quantum-mobility estimate (e.g. the Shubnikov–de Haas onset field), with the spread. Ideally these would include the >1300 µm² device, a thinner (<10-layer) device, and a matched device from a standard-exfoliated flake processed identically, which would show whether the cryogenic shock degrades or improves disorder. A compact table of all Fig. S5 devices (layer number, rhombohedral area, whether stacking survived fabrication and cooldown, one transport metric) would help. If no other devices were measured, the abstract and conclusion should state that the transport and imaging come from one device and narrow the claims accordingly.

3. The exfoliation statistics (Fig. 1g,h; Methods; Fig. S2) do not yet establish that the 12% → 32% increase is a controlled, reproducible effect. The numbers of flakes, exfoliation runs, chips, tape preparations and bulk crystals per method are not given, nor whether both methods used the same crystal or its source (natural, Kish, HOPG), nor the run-to-run spread; the Methods state only that equal total substrate areas were compared. Flakes from 3 to ~20 layers are pooled although rhombohedral content depends on thickness, the layer-number distributions of the two methods are not shown, and the two populations occupy different flake-size ranges (standard flakes mostly near 10³ µm², cryo flakes systematically larger), so differences in thickness or size distributions alone could shift the averages. The exclusion rules (cracks, wrinkles, dirt, aspect ratio, IR-visible micro-domains, areas < 80 µm²) are said to introduce "approximately equal systematic error" for both methods, but a thermal shock plausibly creates more cracks and wrinkles, so that assumption needs checking. The ovals show ±1 standard deviation with no significance test, and it is unclear whether 12%/32% are per-flake arithmetic or area-weighted means and how Bernal-only flakes plotted at zero are handled. The authors should give N (flakes, runs, crystals) for both methods with per-run fractions and spread; show the rhombohedral fraction against layer number for both methods; compare size-matched subsets (e.g. whether cryo flakes of ~1000 µm² have a higher fraction than standard flakes of the same size); report the excluded area fraction for each method; state the averaging; and give a significance test.

4. The stacking identification does not distinguish ABC from other non-Bernal or mixed stacking, yet the text calls the quantity "32% rhombohedral" (Fig. 1g; Fig. 2). The y-axis of Fig. 1g is (1 − A_Bernal)/A_total, so any non-ABA region counts. For 10–20 layers, mixed sequences such as ABCB, or ABC blocks separated by a buried twin or stacking fault, can give similar IR and sMIM contrast, and sMIM was done only on selected regions. A tighter effective-mass determination (concern 12) would also support uninterrupted ABC stacking.

5. The claim that the cryogenic shock converts Bernal to rhombohedral stacking via differential-contraction stress ("suggesting that the cryogenic shock induces a partial conversion", "likely via differential thermal contraction induced stress", p. 3) is not separated from selection effects. Bulk natural graphite already contains 10–15% rhombohedral stacking; a cleaving process that yields larger, less fragmented flakes (as Fig. 1g shows) could preserve more native ABC regions, change where the crystal cleaves to extract ABC-rich regions, or reduce relaxation during peeling, all without conversion. The authors should add a discriminating control: the same protocol with the LN2 step replaced by slow cooling, LN2 immersion without the 110 °C step, heating without the quench, or a varied quench temperature differential or rate; measure the rhombohedral fraction of the source crystal by XRD or Raman; image the same flakes by IR before and after a shock applied to already-exfoliated flakes on chip; or examine the flakes left on the tape. If conversion is real, evidence of shear (partial dislocations, domain-wall density, residual strain by Raman 2D/G mapping) would support the mechanism. Otherwise the text should present conversion as one hypothesis among others.

6. The assignment of the resistance minimum in the 0.5 µm channel (Fig. 4g–i) to the Gurzhi effect lacks the standard discriminating tests. The minimum near 15–30 K is shown at (n_e, D) points in the single-surface, spin/valley-polarized regime, where melting of magnetic order, changing spin/valley fluctuations or a phase boundary crossed with temperature could produce a non-monotonic R(T) with no hydrodynamics; the orange (2.5, 0.85) curve has a sharp kink near 7–10 K that looks more like a phase transition than a smooth viscous crossover. A ballistic-to-diffusive crossover with temperature-dependent boundary specularity is another candidate. The comparison with simulation is only qualitative: the measured dips are 10–25% (a few tens of Ω/sq on 100–400 Ω/sq), whereas the simulated channel resistance drops by about an order of magnitude from ~210 Ω/sq (Fig. 4i), and temperature is mapped to ℓ_ee only as a "proxy". The transport points also differ from the (n, D) of the hydrodynamic image (6.5 × 10¹² cm⁻², 0.41 V/nm). The authors should show the dependence on channel width, the dR/dI-versus-current dependence (Joule heating of the electron fluid should mirror the temperature dependence), the perpendicular-field dependence (negative magnetoresistance from suppressed viscosity with characteristic field set by r_c ~ W), the absence of the minimum where theory predicts none (e.g. n_e = 10¹³ cm⁻², D = 0), a map of the (n, D, T) region where dR/dT < 0 to rule out a phase boundary, and transport at the imaging (n, D) or imaging at the transport points. They should give the implied ℓ_ee(T), check it against the ℓ_ee ≈ 50 nm used elsewhere, and compare magnitudes quantitatively with Fig. 4i using parameters from the same device.

7. The "size-driven crossover from Poiseuille to porous flow" (abstract; Fig. 4g–l) rests on two geometries that also differ in measurement configuration, not on a width series. The channel data (Fig. 4g) are a four-terminal channel resistance; the square data (Fig. 4j) use injection at the top corners and voltage across the bottom (Fig. 4k; Fig. S6 insets), a bend-type configuration that is negative in the ballistic limit for reasons unrelated to viscosity (Fig. 4l, Fig. S9a). A monotonic R_s(T) in the square rising from negative values is expected from a crossover from ballistic bend resistance to ohmic transport driven only by decreasing ℓ_mr (compare Fig. S6c and the ℓ_mr axis of Fig. S9a), and also in ordinary diffusive flow; the paper itself says R in the square is "insensitive to ℓ_ee" (Fig. 4l). The absence of a minimum therefore does not by itself show porous flow, and with only two sizes there is no crossover to track. The simulations include the contact configuration, which helps. The authors should measure channels of several widths (e.g. 0.5, 1, 2, 5 µm) in the same stack and contact configuration and show that the dR/dT < 0 window and amplitude scale with W/ℓ_G, or add a local four-terminal measurement across the square; show, using Fig. S9a along a realistic (ℓ_ee(T), ℓ_mr(T)) trajectory, that the measured square R_s(T) requires small ℓ_ee; confirm that the channel and square lie in the same rhombohedral domain and were measured in the same cooldown; and state that separating porous from ohmic flow relies on the imaging. Otherwise the crossover should be presented as consistent with, rather than established by, the data.

9. Electron heating during current imaging and transport is not discussed (Fig. 4a,c,e use 10 µA at T ≈ 1.6 K). In an ultraclean sample electron–phonon cooling is weak; with sheet resistances of hundreds of Ω/sq in narrow regions plus contact resistance, the electron temperature could rise well above the bath and strengthen e–e scattering, mimicking a hydrodynamic profile, and the effect could differ between the ballistic and flat-band points. The authors should show that the normalized flow profiles are independent of current (e.g. 1–2, 3–5 and 10 µA), estimate T_e from the dissipated power, state the excitation frequency and whether the current is AC, and give the excitation current for the TMF and Fig. 4g,j R(T) measurements.
