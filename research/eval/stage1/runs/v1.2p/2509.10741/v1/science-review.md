A. Must address

1. The measured energies lie outside the kinematic bound of an intra-band continuum. An intra-band continuum of a band with Fermi velocity v_F extends only up to ω ≈ v_F·q (plus a small curvature term) and must collapse to zero energy as q → 0. The paper takes v_F(σ) ≈ 2 eVÅ (p. 2), and the σ sheets are nearly kz-independent cylinders (Fig. 1c), so for them only q_∥ matters. At q_∥ = 0.016 Å⁻¹ (Fig. 2b) this bounds the σ intra-band continuum at ~0.03–0.05 eV, yet the feature peaks at ~0.3 eV, an order of magnitude higher. At q_∥ ≈ 0.05–0.055 Å⁻¹ (Fig. 3e) the bound is ~0.1 eV, but ω_exp ≈ 0.4 eV (ω/q ≈ 7 eVÅ). Over the whole range of Fig. 3e the data lie well above v_F·q, and the dashed linear fit extrapolates to a finite intercept of ~0.25–0.3 eV at q_∥ = 0, which an intra-band continuum cannot have. Even taking the full |q| ≈ 0.15 Å⁻¹, v_F·|q| is only ~0.3 eV, and smaller for the c-axis component, along which the σ bands are nearly flat. The BSE projection (Fig. 2c, †) nonetheless assigns the feature to σ states near Γ. The χ⁰ peak positions in Fig. 3e (triangles) do extrapolate toward zero, but they disagree with the data by up to ~0.25 eV precisely at the smallest q, where the Lindhard picture is most diagnostic; the text dismisses this as spectral weight "split between different features". The velocity numbers are also internally inconsistent: the quoted "speed of ≈1.4 eVÅ, around half of the Fermi velocity of the σ bands" does not match half of "roughly 2 eVÅ" (1 eVÅ), and the slope read from Fig. 3e is closer to ~2–2.2 eVÅ (about 0.43 eV at 0.055 Å⁻¹ to 0.66 eV at 0.155 Å⁻¹). Since the identification as the Lindhard continuum rests on the dispersion, and the dispersion as presented lacks the defining property of an intra-band continuum, the authors should: state the fit form, the slope and the intercept with uncertainties; give the in-plane and c-axis Fermi velocities of each σ and π sheet; overlay the per-sheet upper edges v_F·q, computed with the full 3D q of each spectrum, on Figs. 3b–e; and explain how a feature at 0.3–0.4 eV at q_∥ ≤ 0.055 Å⁻¹, including the clear peak at q_∥ = 0.016 Å⁻¹, can be an intra-band σ excitation, or identify which bands (for example π, with their kz dispersion and larger velocities, at the actual total momentum transfer) could support it.

2. The intra/inter-band decomposition in Fig. 4c, which carries the claim that the analysis "conclusively demonstrates" an intra-band origin, looks unphysical as presented and is ambiguously defined. At q_∥ = 0.088 Å⁻¹ the "ππ, intra" component extends to ~1.5 eV and the "σσ, intra" component to ~0.5–0.6 eV, implying ω/q ≈ 17 eVÅ and ≈ 6–7 eVÅ, far above any Fermi velocity in MgB2 (≲ 5–6 eVÅ for π, ~2–3 eVÅ for σ, and 2 eVÅ by the paper's own value). Possible causes include insufficient k-point sampling relative to q, a mislabelled q (for example a large implicit q_⊥), or an "intra-band" label assigned by band-index ordering that mixes bands across crossings of the 6 σ and 2 π tight-binding bands. SI Sec. F states "an N×N×N grid ... where N=512"; if this means 512 points in total (8×8×8), the grid spacing (~0.3 Å⁻¹ in-plane) exceeds the entire measured q range, and intra-band transitions at q ≤ 0.17 Å⁻¹ cannot be resolved with δ = 0.01 eV. It is also unclear whether transitions between the two σ bands (light and heavy hole, degenerate along Γ–A and split away from it) count as "intra" or "inter"; such σ1–σ2 inter-band transitions are a natural alternative origin of low-energy weight with a finite energy at q → 0, and would explain the finite intercept of Concern 1. Bands may be labelled by energy ordering, orbital character or continuity, and the choice changes the decomposition. Finally, the caption says σ–π inter-band excitations are negligible, but the legend shows only σσ-inter and ππ-inter, with no σπ curve. If the weight above v_F·q comes from σ-light → σ-heavy transitions, the feature is inter-band and the paper's title and main conclusion change. The authors should define the decomposition precisely (band index a → b for each of the eight TB bands), clarify the k-mesh and show convergence of χ⁰ with mesh and δ at the measured q, show each Fermi-surface-crossing band pair separately (including the σπ component), and verify that each "intra" channel vanishes above its own max(v_F·q).

3. The "dispersion" is measured by rotating the momentum transfer at roughly fixed |q|, so in-plane momentum, out-of-plane momentum, incident angle and polarization all change together. Fig. 3a labels run from (q_∥, q_⊥) = (0.05, 0.14) through (0.10, 0.08) to (0.16, 0.02) Å⁻¹, i.e. |q| ≈ 0.149, 0.128 and 0.161 Å⁻¹ (the photon momentum at 190 eV is ~0.096 Å⁻¹), while θ_in, the polarization projection and self-absorption change. The authors note that grazing geometry changes the σ/π orbital sensitivity (pp. 3–4), and Fig. 4a shows that orbital weighting alone moves spectral weight between dispersive features. The peak shift from ~0.4 to ~0.65 eV (Fig. 3e) could therefore reflect a changing σ/π mixture of unresolved contributions, or the changing q_⊥ to which the 3D π sheets are sensitive, rather than an in-plane dispersion; the "nearly linear dispersion" and the ~1.4 eVÅ velocity extracted against q_∥ alone (p. 3) do not have the meaning given to them, since a particle–hole continuum depends on the full 3D q through v_F·q, which in MgB2 is strongly anisotropic. It is also unclear whether the Lindhard and RPA calculations (Figs. 3d, 3e, 4) used the actual (q_∥, q_⊥) of each spectrum or only q_∥ along Γ–M with q_⊥ = 0; the axes suggest the latter. The authors should give a table of θ_in, 2θ, q_∥, q_⊥ and |q| for every spectrum; recompute χ⁰ and χ along the experimental (q_∥, q_⊥) trajectory; and show data that separate momentum from geometry, for example a second scattering angle (or a second incident energy within the resonance) giving different |q| at the same θ_in, spectra at the same q_∥ reached at different θ_in, and the Γ–K data mentioned in SI Sec. A but never shown (near Γ the σ continuum should be nearly isotropic in-plane, so Γ–M versus Γ–K is a useful check).

B. Should address

4. The Raman character of the feature and the role of the intermediate state are not tested. All RIXS data are taken at a single incident energy on the sharp, exciton-like σ-hole pre-edge resonance at ~187 eV (Fig. 2a). In metals, RIXS near threshold commonly contains a fluorescence-like (non-Raman) component whose apparent energy loss changes with detuning and absorption geometry, and an asymmetric feature rising from the elastic line with a long high-energy tail (Fig. 3a) is just what threshold fluorescence or intermediate-state relaxation would produce. Over the relevant range the unoccupied σ states near Γ/A extend only ~0.5–0.8 eV above E_F (Figs. 2c, S1), the same energy scale as the feature, so a process tied to the σ hole-pocket depth could also produce it. The authors should show an incident-energy RIXS map across the pre-edge at fixed geometry (one or two q values) to demonstrate a constant energy loss and resonance behaviour consistent with the BSE; add main-edge (~192–194 eV) and below-edge reference spectra, where the predicted orbital selectivity differs; take spectra with σ incident polarization at the same q, which under Eq. 32 change the p_x/p_y weights at fixed q and so separate matrix-element effects from dispersion; and report whether the BSE RIXS feature survives with the core-hole potential switched off, or in an independent-particle RIXS calculation. This would test the key assumption that the measured spectrum is proportional to an orbital-projected charge susceptibility.

5. The BSE RIXS simulations may not be converged at the relevant momenta and energies, so their agreement with the data is weaker support for the intra-band assignment than presented. SI Sec. C uses a 36 × 36 × 28 k-mesh, whose in-plane spacing (~0.065 Å⁻¹) is comparable to or larger than the measured q_∥ (0.05–0.16 Å⁻¹, and 0.016 Å⁻¹ in Fig. 2b); a Fermi–Dirac smearing of 0.02 Ry ≈ 0.27 eV, comparable to the feature energy itself, which blurs the occupations that define the intra-band window and could alone generate a low-energy peak at a few hundred meV; 0.1 eV broadening; and only 12 bands with the first 4 occupied bands removed. The BSE spectrum in Fig. 2b also has almost no weight between ~0.6 and 2.5 eV, where the experiment shows a large tail, and shows two sharp low-energy peaks (~0.15 and ~0.35 eV) not seen in experiment. SCAN was chosen because it best reproduces the XAS (uncaptioned comparison on SI p. S8); it is not stated whether the low-energy RIXS result depends on the functional. The authors should state how momentum transfers smaller than the k-spacing are represented; repeat the calculation with a denser (or shifted or interpolated) k-mesh and much smaller smearing, at least at one q, and show the low-energy feature and its dispersion are stable; and perform a "switch-off" test, showing that the low-energy BSE feature disappears or changes when σ intra-band (or all intra-band) transitions are excluded, or project the BSE exciton density onto band pairs (same band versus different band). That would be a far more direct test of the intra-band assignment than the single-band projection in Fig. 2c.

7. A predicted acoustic plasmon is not considered as an alternative origin. Ref. [12] (Silkin et al., PRB 80, 054521), which the authors cite, predicts a low-energy acoustic plasmon in MgB2 from out-of-phase σ and π carrier oscillations, dispersing roughly linearly with a velocity between the σ and π Fermi velocities, in the energy and momentum window studied here. The introduction names acoustic plasmons, yet this collective mode is not discussed, and a linearly dispersing peak (rather than a broad continuum) is arguably closer to that prediction. The orbitally resolved RPA (Fig. 4a) should either contain such a mode or show that it is absent or overdamped; the authors should state which and discuss Ref. [12] explicitly.

8. The comparison uses only peak positions from a symmetric fit to an asymmetric feature; the lineshape and intensity scaling expected of a continuum are not tested. The feature is described as "markedly asymmetric" (p. 2, Figs. 3a, S2), yet its position is extracted from a symmetric Voigt alongside an elastic line whose tail overlaps it strongly at the lowest q, with the high-energy tail absorbed into another component (Fig. S2). A Lindhard continuum has no unique peak velocity: for a 2D cylinder Im χ⁰ ∝ ω/√(v_F²q² − ω²) up to v_F·q, and its defining features are the onset at ω → 0 and the upper edge near v_F·q; its integrated weight should grow with q at small q. The error bars in Fig. 3e are not defined, and electron–phonon coupling to the E2g mode in the σ bands, and phonon or multiphonon contributions near the elastic line, are not considered in the fit. The authors should state how the error bars were obtained, which parameters were free or fixed, how the elastic line and the 3 eV feature were constrained, and how ω_BSE and the χ⁰ points were extracted (maximum, first moment, or fit); fit with an asymmetric or model-based lineshape (for example the broadened, weighted χ of Fig. 4a convolved with resolution); report edge- or moment-based quantities applied identically to data and theory; compare full lineshapes at each (q_∥, q_⊥), at least at two or three representative q; and show how the integrated low-energy intensity scales with q after normalizing for the geometry-dependent cross-section and self-absorption.

10. The novelty claims are overstated and need to be placed in context. The title, abstract and introduction say the Lindhard continuum "has remained experimentally elusive" and that "no feature resembling the textbook illustration ... has been reported". However: the intra-band single-particle-excitation continuum dispersing with v_F·q has long been observed by resonant inelastic light scattering from 2D electron gases in semiconductor heterostructures, relying on the same resonance-enabled escape from Coulomb screening; non-resonant inelastic x-ray scattering and momentum-resolved EELS on simple metals (for example Li, Na, Al) have measured S(q, ω) beyond the plasmon cutoff, where the particle–hole continuum dominates and follows the Lindhard form with known corrections; electronic Raman scattering probes intra-band particle–hole excitations in metals at small q; and particle–hole continua have been compared with Lindhard-type calculations in RIXS and momentum-resolved EELS of metallic oxides and other systems, including Ref. [23]. Ref. [21] reports monochromated EELS on MgB2 that "distinguish intra-band and inter-band transitions"; the paper should explain what that work saw and why the present result differs, rather than citing it only for inter-band features. The sum-rule argument (Eq. 1) applies only at small q below the plasmon cutoff and shows that the continuum carries small, not zero, weight in L(q, ω), so the statement that EELS "cannot see" intra-band excitations should be softened. The claim should be narrowed, for example to the first momentum-resolved observation by RIXS, or by an orbital-selective probe, of the small-q, below-cutoff intra-band continuum in a 3D multiband metal, and this literature discussed.

11. The sum-rule argument is not connected quantitatively to MgB2. The statement that "EELS ... cannot see low-energy intra-band excitations" (p. 5) is derived for a single-component system. MgB2 is a multiband metal with σ and π carriers of different velocities; out-of-phase σ–π density fluctuations are not fully screened and can carry continuum or acoustic-plasmon-like weight in the total response, which the cited ab initio loss function (Ref. [22]) and low-energy charge-excitation work (Ref. [12]) address. The authors should show the computed total loss function for MgB2 at the experimental q (from their RPA or GPAW calculation) and quantify the ratio of continuum weight in the total response to that in the orbital-projected response, so that the MgB2-specific claim rests on a number rather than on the toy model of Fig. S4; a comparison with EELS on the same film would strengthen it further.
