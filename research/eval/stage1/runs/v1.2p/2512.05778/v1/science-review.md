A. Must address

1. No Raman control shows that the structural memory is thermal rather than a consequence of laser exposure. All Raman cycling (Figs. 1, 3) is driven by laser power of up to 150 mW at 532 nm on a ~50 µm spot (several kW/cm²), with "almost instantaneous" heating and cooling, T_B ≈ 38 K. The cold-finger ("standard") heating data (Fig. 2) were never run as a cycling protocol, and the heater-versus-laser comparison in the Supplement (Figs. S1–S5) uses a continuous power ramp, not cycles. At least two other explanations fit the data. (i) Photo-induced effects: visible light is known to melt charge-orbital order, photo-dope, induce metastability and switch the insulator-metal state in Pr–Ca–Mn–O based manganites, and to cause local oxygen loss; a spurious ~660 cm⁻¹ feature attributed to oxygen vacancies is already present. Strong thermal gradients and strain from local heating could also contribute. (ii) A shift in the local temperature reached at the 10 mW read-out: if high-power exposure changes absorption, surface roughness or thermal contact, the "T_B" spectrum is taken at a different temperature, and because the JT intensity is a strongly non-monotonic function of temperature (Fig. 2d), a modest offset could mimic the effect. The paper calls the effect a thermal memory, and this cannot be concluded without a matched control. The authors should repeat the T_T series on a fresh spot using the cold-finger heater for the excursion, with Raman read out at T_B at constant low power (10 mW or lower) and, ideally, with the same T_B, ramp rate and dwell as the PPMS runs, and compare directly with Fig. 3. They should also report, for every T_B spectrum in Fig. 3, the local temperature from the Stokes/anti-Stokes intensity ratio of the ~250 cm⁻¹ (or 485 cm⁻¹) mode, using the low-frequency region already recorded (Fig. S2), to show that T_B is the same after each cycle.

2. The headline claims of laser-induced, non-volatile resistive switching and laser-assisted phase engineering are not demonstrated. The Abstract, Discussion and Conclusions state that the work demonstrates "laser-induced thermal cycling to induce non-volatile structure/resistive-switching", "a novel method for controlling phase separation and resistive properties through localized laser heating", and that "for the first time" resistive switching is "associated with the local structure". These rest on two separate experiments. Magnetization and resistance (Fig. 4) were measured on the whole pellet in a PPMS with global cryostat cycling at 5 K/min, T_B = 30 K and a different T_T grid (Raman: 59, 81, 102, … K; bulk: 50, 60, … K). The Raman cycles use local laser heating of a surface layer a few tens of nm deep within a 50 µm spot, with "almost instantaneous" cooling and T_B ≈ 38 K. Kinetic arrest and the retained FMM fraction in phase-separated manganites depend strongly on cooling rate, so rates differing by orders of magnitude are not equivalent histories. No transport or magnetization was measured on laser-cycled material, and no Raman spectrum was taken after cryostat cycling, so the structure–transport "correlation" rests on qualitative similarity between two different experiments. Non-volatility is also untested: retention of the high-resistance state at T_B over time, repeated set/reset cycles (T_C < T_T < T_MI followed by T_T > T_COO, several times) and the energy and time cost of a cycle are not given; erasing requires heating above ~205 K and reading requires staying near 30–40 K, which should be stated plainly if neuromorphic relevance is claimed. The authors should either measure transport or magnetization on a laser-cycled region of the same specimen (for example a narrow bar or bridge, a patterned two-terminal gap or a laser-addressed constriction illuminated by the beam, or a contacted pellet in an optical cryostat), or take Raman spectra after bulk cryostat cycling at the PPMS rates, and add retention, cooling-rate and repeated write/erase measurements. Otherwise the laser-related claims in the Abstract, Discussion and Conclusions should be removed and the result restricted to a qualitative correlation between thermally driven, history-dependent resistivity and the Raman data.

3. The cumulative protocol confounds the T_T dependence with accumulated history, elapsed time, relaxation and illumination dose, and there is no null measurement. Each cycle starts from the state left by the previous one and T_T increases monotonically on the same spot (Fig. 1b; Methods), so the response at a given T_T is entangled with all earlier cycles, total illumination dose and time spent at low temperature; Fig. 4c makes the history explicit (M_i(n) ≈ M_f(n−1), and the M_i values change from cycle to cycle). LPCMO in the DPS regime at 30–40 K is known to relax slowly (Ghivelder2005, Sacanell2004, cited by the authors), and the 5-minute wait before a ~5-minute acquisition (Methods) is short compared with those relaxation times, so the rise and fall in Fig. 3c–e could partly reflect relaxation and drift at T_B. The observation is also what ordinary minor-loop hysteresis and kinetic arrest of the first-order FMM/COO transition would give: on warming above T_C^W ≈ 91 K FMM converts to COO, part of that COO is retained on recooling from inside the hysteresis window, and warming above T_COO erases it. Without controls the claim that the state at T_B encodes T_T, and that this is a "previously unidentified regime", is not supported. The authors should: record repeated spectra (and ρ) at T_B at 10 mW for the full duration of a cycle sequence without any excursion (the null); run non-cumulative cycles, recooling from above T_COO or from 300 K before each T_T, for several T_T values, and compare with the cumulative series; run a decreasing-T_T sequence and at least one non-monotonic sequence (for example a T_T between T_C and T_MI after a reset above T_COO); repeat a given T_T twice in succession to see whether the effect saturates or accumulates; measure the time dependence of the Raman intensity and ρ at T_B for at least tens of minutes after each cycle; and include a spot that went through the same cryostat history but was never illuminated at high power. The same issue applies to the M and ρ cycles.

B. Should address

4. The laser-heating temperature scale is coarse, partly circular, and its uncertainty is underestimated. T_T for every laser cycle comes from Table S1, built by "visual inspection and comparison" of power-dependent with temperature-dependent spectra on a ~25 K matching grid, mainly using the width and relative intensity of the 250, 480 and 614 cm⁻¹ structures (Fig. S1: inflections at 75, 100, 125, 150, 175 K matched to 40, 50, 60, 70, 80 mW). These are the same JT intensities and widths whose T_T dependence is the reported effect, so the coincidence of the Raman thresholds with T_C, T_MI and T_COO (Fig. 3) is partly built in. The matching was done on a continuous power ramp, itself a cumulative thermal history. Further problems: below ~75 K (40 mW) the spectra are admitted to be indistinguishable, so T_B = 38(4) K is pure extrapolation; a linear T(P) with the intercept fixed to zero is imposed (footnote, p. 12; a free fit gives −3(5) K), and a temperature-independent 21.8 K offset is then added, without explaining why the intercept was not fixed at the cold-finger temperature; the thermal conductivity and optical absorption of LPCMO change strongly between the FMM and AFMI-COO phases, so the temperature reached at a given power should depend on the phase state of the spot, which by the paper's own claim changes from cycle to cycle, and linearity in P is not expected; a Gaussian spot at these powers has strong lateral and depth gradients, so T_T is a distribution, smearing the axis exactly where the effect sits; and Fig. S5d–f shows integrated areas under laser heating roughly 40% below those under cryostat heating near the maximum (~4.5 vs ~7.5 in S5d), at odds with the stated "great agreement" and pointing to an offset or gradient. The quoted ±4–8 K errors are fit-propagation errors only. The authors should determine the spot temperature independently from Stokes/anti-Stokes ratios of the 250 cm⁻¹ (or 485 cm⁻¹) mode at each power, both at T_B under 10 mW and at each T_T, before and after cycling (anti-Stokes signal down to ~−250 cm⁻¹ is recorded but absorbed into the background, Figs. S2, S6); re-plot Fig. 3 on that scale; give realistic uncertainties including the matching-grid resolution; and estimate the radial and depth temperature profile (heat-diffusion model with known thermal conductivity and optical penetration depth, or a spot-size series).

6. Possible laser-induced modification of the surface is not ruled out. The same spot is irradiated repeatedly up to 150 mW on ~50 µm (roughly 7–8 kW/cm²) over more than 15 cycles. An unidentified band near 660–670 cm⁻¹ (red dashed line, Fig. 2a) is attributed to "local oxygen vacancies" without evidence; a band near this frequency is also the fingerprint of laser-induced Mn3O4-like surface decomposition in manganites, and in Fig. 2b it grows strongly near 180–210 K. It is not tracked through the cycles. The background at high Raman shift is visibly higher after some cycles (Fig. 1e,f) and much higher at 150 mW (Fig. S6f). The authors should track the ~660–670 cm⁻¹ feature and the background versus cycle number; show quantitatively, beyond the visual comparison in Fig. 1h, that a full sequence followed by a reset reproduces the as-cooled spectrum; take spectra after a final warm-up to room temperature and re-cooling; and inspect the irradiated spot afterwards by optical microscopy or a micro-Raman map.

7. Intensity normalization and background subtraction may produce or distort the reported trends. All JT intensities are divided by the ~250 cm⁻¹ rotational mode (Figs. 2d, 3c), but Fig. S5a,d shows this mode's area itself changes about fourfold with temperature and phase fraction, peaking near 120 K, so the ratio mixes changes in both modes and shifts the apparent maximum (Figs. S1 and S5e,f place the JT maximum near 100–125 K, Fig. 2d near T_MI ≈ 150 K); in Fig. 3c the reference mode at T_B could also depend on history. The fits use a fixed 7th-degree polynomial background plus several broad Lorentzians (FWHM 50–120 cm⁻¹) over the whole range, with fit quality "judged based on the visual inspection"; the background shape changes markedly between conditions (Figs. S6a, b, e) and after cycling (Fig. 1e,f). The authors should show the absolute (unnormalized) fitted JT and rotational intensities at T_B versus T_T, including I_rot alone; show that the trends in Fig. 3c–e survive background-independent metrics (peak heights above a local linear baseline, integrated intensity in fixed windows), a lower-order or physically motivated electronic-continuum background, the background left in, and a different number of Lorentzians; and report how laser focus and collection efficiency were kept constant between cycles, including how the same spot was maintained as the cryostat contracted.

8. JT-mode intensity is used as a measure of AFMI-COO phase fraction without justification. The Discussion reads intensity gains as the "strength of the COO phase" and as FMM-fraction changes, and the claim that "the strength of the COO phase achieved at T_T is retained" rests on comparing intensities measured at temperature T (Fig. 2d) with intensities at T_B after visiting T_T (Fig. 3c). Raman intensity per phase also depends on temperature (Bose factors, polaron dynamics), on optical penetration depth (much shorter in metallic FMM regions), on resonance conditions at 532 nm close to JT-polaron and charge-transfer excitations, and on electronic screening by metallic regions. The two-fold Raman gain versus the halving of M (Fig. 4d) is attributed loosely to "the AFM character of the insulating phase". Structural characterization is limited to 278 K (Fig. S7), although the interpretation rests on Pnma (FMM) versus P2₁/m (AFMI-COO). The authors should discuss these factors and calibrate JT intensity against an independent phase-fraction estimate: low-temperature synchrotron diffraction after selected cycles (P2₁/m vs Pnma fraction or superlattice intensity; their PAINEIRA access would allow this, even at one temperature in the DPS regime), saturated DC magnetization at T_B, or field-induced COO→FMM conversion at T_B. A structural probe would also support the claim that the irreversibility is "structural" rather than purely electronic.

9. The paper is not positioned against the established literature on thermal history, memory and kinetic arrest in phase-separated manganites, and the novelty is overstated. History-dependent FM fraction, glassy relaxation, cooling-rate dependence, minor-loop memory and erasure by heating above the charge-ordering temperature are well documented in LPCMO and related compounds (Ghivelder2005, Sacanell, Ward2011, Kundhikanjana2015, all cited, and the kinetic-arrest/CHUF literature). The M and ρ behaviour in Fig. 4 resembles what those works predict. Calling the effect a "previously unidentified regime" and "a new form of nonequilibrium phase dynamics" requires a direct comparison. The authors should state what is new beyond the Raman read-out (for example a lattice signature absent from transport, a quantitative difference in the T_T dependence, or a spatially local readout), frame the M/ρ results accordingly, and replace "irreversible", a misnomer for a state that resets on heating above T_COO, with "history-dependent" or "metastable".

12. The "magnetization" is low-field AC susceptibility and does not measure FMM fraction. M was measured in ACMS mode with a 10 Oe, 11 Hz AC field and no DC field, and reported as µB/Mn at the ~10⁻² level, two orders of magnitude below saturation. In a polycrystalline ferromagnet this response is dominated by domain-wall dynamics and demagnetization and is not proportional to FM volume fraction. The authors should label and report it as χ′ (and χ″), and add DC magnetization at a moderate field (of order 1 T) or M(H) loops to saturation at T_B after each cycle (or selected T_T), so that the Raman and ρ changes can be related to a real FMM fraction.

13. Resistance was measured with two probes. Silver-paint contacts on a polycrystalline pellet, probes ~0.7 mm apart, 0.1 µA: contact and grain-boundary resistance, both possibly history dependent, are included and cannot be separated from bulk percolation, and the conversion to resistivity (Ω mm) needs a stated geometry. 14. Differences between the local, surface Raman probe and the bulk M/ρ measurements are not discussed. At 532 nm Raman samples only the top tens of nm of a freshly broken polycrystalline surface, where surface strain and grain-boundary effects, known to shift the phase balance in LPCMO, may differ from the bulk; M and ρ are bulk-averaged. A laser-heated spot is also mechanically constrained by the cold surrounding matrix, and phase separation in LPCMO is strongly strain-sensitive (as the introduction notes), so thermal stress from local heating may drive part of the effect, which is absent in uniform PPMS heating. The authors should discuss and, where possible, estimate these effects.
