Concerns

A. Must address

1. The Hall signatures do not distinguish trions from excitons plus free carriers, and the authors' own model predicts them without trions. On p. 6 the paper states that the negative Rxy in the hole (W) layer "unambiguously demonstrates the presence of a trion Hall effect and trion drags." In the Methods, the negative-trion result is Rxy ≈ −(B/e)[(1+f)/n_e + f²(1−σX0/σe0)/n_T], with f = σT0/(σT0+σX0). Setting f = 0 (σT = 0: no trions, only excitons and free electrons) leaves Rxy ≈ −B/(e n_e), an electron-Hall response in the hole layer with no trion at all. This is the familiar perfect-drag mechanism (Refs. 30, 31, ED Fig. 8): with the Mo layer open, any W-layer current carried by excitons must be returned by free electrons in the Mo layer, and their Hall field is transferred to the W layer because excitons pin E_h = E_e. Equivalently, the hole-layer resistivity is ρ_h = ρ_X + ρ_e (exciton and free-electron channels in series), whose Hall part is −B/(e n_e). The same reasoning applies to Rxy(drag) (Fig. 3b, Fig. 4c,d): with current driven in the Mo layer and the W layer open, exciton coupling makes the W-layer transverse field follow the Hall field of the free excess carriers, so the drag Hall signal takes the sign of the majority excess carrier, positive for p > n and negative for n > p. That is exactly the sign change across Vg = 0 presented as the trion signature. On the positive side the authors themselves find Rxy ≈ B/(n_h e), carried only by free holes (p. 5), so the drop of n_H in Fig. 3d only counts holes removed from the free pool; holes bound into excitons or trions give the same result, as the text concedes ("loss of free holes to the formation of trions and excitons"). Fig. 3a and the left side of Fig. 3d are therefore not trion Hall signals either. Because the title and abstract claims rest on this inference, the authors need a discriminating test:
   (a) Derive Rxx, Rxy, Rxx(drag) and Rxy(drag) explicitly from Eqs. (1)–(5), both with σT = 0 and with σT ≠ 0.
   (b) Fit all four quantities together along the Vb ≈ 0.88 V cut (red line in Fig. 3a), and the T dependence in Fig. 4b,d, with n_e, n_h and n_T constrained by the gate electrostatics and the RC-determined valley occupations and as few free scattering times as possible. Show that the σT = 0 model fails quantitatively and the σT ≠ 0 model succeeds.
   (c) In Fig. 3d, overlay the net excess density |p − n| expected for excitons plus free carriers and identify where n_H departs from it in a way only trions explain (each positive trion removes two holes, so the two pictures predict different n_H(Vg)). Fig. 3d shows |n_H| ranging from well below to well above the gate-determined electron density, which such a fit can exploit; show whether the magnitude of the negative Rxy or of the Hall drag exceeds what free electrons plus exciton drag can give at the measured n_e.
   (d) Use a null case where trions are absent: the X region inside the EI triangle, or the electron-doped side outside the trion triangle at matched exciton density.
   (e) Identify an observable unique to charged composites, for example the drag Hall angle versus trion density at fixed excess density, the ratio Rxy(drag)/Rxy, or the B dependence of Rxy(drag).
   If no such discriminator is found, the claim should be restated as a Hall response of a mixed exciton/trion/free-carrier fluid that is consistent with, rather than demonstrating, a trion Hall effect, and "unambiguously" removed. The paper should also say which result carries which claim: the optical evidence for trions (Fig. 2c) is stronger than the transport evidence for a trion-specific Hall effect.

2. The Hall signals are measured where the longitudinal resistance exceeds them by one to three orders of magnitude, and the paper does not show how the Hall components were isolated. In the trion regions Rxx is 10^4–10^6 Ω (Fig. 1e, ED Figs. 6, 7) and Rxx(drag) reaches 10^6 Ω (Fig. 2a, ED Fig. 3a), while the reported Rxy and Rxy(drag) are about 2–15 kΩ (Figs. 3, 4). A contact misalignment of a fraction of a percent, or an inhomogeneous current path, would mix Rxx into Rxy with arbitrary sign and could create both the sign change across Vg ≈ 0 and the negative values on the n side. The negative-Rxy region sits directly against the grey "completely insulating" areas (Fig. 3a, Fig. 4a, ED Fig. 6b,c) where Rxx diverges; the authors note that "Rxy cannot be reliably measured near Vg = 0V due to the diverging Rxx" (p. 5) and that Rxx is much higher on the negative-trion side (p. 4). The appearance of the Hall features below about 8 K (Fig. 4) closely tracks the rise of Rxx in ED Fig. 7b, which an admixture would also produce. In Fig. 4d both Rxy(drag) curves settle at about −0.3 kΩ at 10 K regardless of trion sign, suggesting a field-even offset, while the text calls Rxy(drag) negligible above 8 K. The Methods do not say whether any Hall quantity was antisymmetrized, and Fig. 3c covers only B > 0. Further routes to a kΩ-scale spurious transverse voltage include capacitive and out-of-phase pickup at 7.33 Hz, preamplifier loading (100 MΩ input impedance against MΩ-scale source impedances), and interlayer AC coupling in the drag configuration, which is only minimized by a potentiometer. The circuit in ED Fig. 2a also appears to take the Hall voltage between W-3 and W-4 with W-3 on the current path; if so, contact resistance adds directly to "Rxy". Since the headline result is a sign of Rxy, the authors should: (a) show Rxy and Rxy(drag) at ±B with the antisymmetrized [R(+B) − R(−B)]/2 and symmetric parts for the maps and line cuts of Figs. 3a–c and 4, demonstrating that the symmetric part does not drive the sign change; (b) show Rxy(B) through B = 0 at fixed (Vg, Vb) in both trion regions; (c) report the out-of-phase lock-in components, the contact resistances in these regions and the criterion used for the grey masks; (d) give the Hall-probe geometry and the expected admixture fraction, and clarify the ED Fig. 2a wiring; (e) check Onsager reciprocity of the Hall drag, Rxy(drag),Mo→W(B) = Rxy(drag),W→Mo(−B); (f) explain or quantify the −0.3 kΩ residual at 10 K in Fig. 4d.

B. Should address

3. The conductivity-tensor model in the Methods appears internally inconsistent, is incompletely specified, and is not compared with data. For a negative trion (two electrons in the Mo layer, one hole in the W layer) moving with velocity v ∝ (−2eE_e + eE_h), the contribution is J_e ∝ (4E_e − 2E_h) and J_h ∝ (−2E_e + E_h), a symmetric block as Onsager reciprocity requires at B = 0. Eq. (1) instead has the asymmetric trion block [[2, −2], [−1, 1]]σT, and Eq. (2) its transpose for positive trions. It is not stated how the 2×2 field-dependent σT of Eq. (3) enters the 4×4 tensor Σ, what sign ωc,T takes for each trion type, or which limits are used (ωcτ ≪ 1 or not; Landau quantization neglected at 4 T). σe0 in the Rxy expression is not defined, and the term f²(1−σX0/σe0)/n_T can take either sign depending on unknown ratios, so the model does not by itself fix the sign of Rxy. The expressions for Rxx, Rxx(drag) and Rxy(drag), which underlie the Hall-drag claim (Fig. 3b, Fig. 4c,d), are never given, and the drag conductivity σD is neglected "at low temperatures" without saying whether this holds at 8–10 K where the comparison is made. The closed-form Rxy and f depend on these choices. The authors should correct or justify Eqs. (1)–(2), define all quantities, give explicit expressions for all four measured resistances for both trion types, and state the approximations.

4. Interlayer leakage and excitation level are not characterized against a sub-meV energy scale. Vb ≈ 0.86–0.90 V is applied across a 1.5–2 nm hBN barrier, and the Mo layer is tied to the Vb source (AC-grounded) through the potentiometer; any tunnelling current would provide a parallel path that contaminates both the "open-circuit" Rxy and the drag signals, and could add an electron contribution to the measured hole-layer Hall voltage, an artifactual route to the negative Rxy. The 1 mV RMS excitation is comparable to or larger than the inferred trion binding energy (0.7–1.2 meV) and the cyclotron energy, and in the insulating regime a substantial fraction may drop across the channel rather than the contacts. The authors should report the interlayer leakage current versus Vb and Vg over the Fig. 3a range, and show that Rxy and Rxy(drag) in the trion regions are in linear response, with no excitation-amplitude dependence or heating. Combining amplitude and temperature dependence (does the signal disappear at an excitation corresponding to the binding energy?) would also test the energy scale independently.

5. The ~8 K scale attributed to trion ionization may be the exciton or excitonic-insulator scale, and its conversion to a binding energy is not justified. The ~8 K onset of Rxy and Rxy(drag) (Fig. 4) is equated with a trion binding energy of 0.7 meV (p. 6). ED Fig. 7b shows the insulating behaviour at Vg = 0 (the EI itself) also emerging below about 8 K at 4 T, and drag from excitons alone switches on at the exciton-formation scale; the Rxy(drag) onset in Fig. 4c is not separated from it. The statement that the binding energy "decreases gradually from ≈ 8K near charge neutrality to zero near |Vg| ≈ 0.06V" has no supporting figure. A thermal ionization crossover in a dense or degenerate 2D fluid occurs well below E_b/k_B because of the Saha entropy factor, so equating 8 K with 0.7 meV underestimates E_b. The authors should plot the extracted crossover temperature against Vg together with the onset of Rxx(drag) or the drag ratio inside the EI region under the same conditions, show whether the trion scale differs from the EI/exciton scale, and explain how a crossover temperature maps onto a binding energy.

6. The optical binding-energy estimate rests on unexamined choices, and the two estimates disagree. The 1.2 meV from the 25 mV threshold shift at 6 T (Methods) and the 0.7 meV from transport at 4 T differ by almost a factor of two yet are called "in good agreement". In the conversion: C_Q uses a constant zero-field density of states with g_s g_v = 2, whereas only one, initially empty spin-valley species is injected (g_s g_v = 1 would roughly halve C_Q); at 6 T the hole cyclotron energy (~1.5 meV for m_h = 0.45 m0) is comparable to or larger than E_T,b, so the density of states is Landau-quantized; the electron-layer (bilayer MoSe2) quantum capacitance and the negative compressibility/exciton compressibility expected near the EI are neglected; C_2L uses ε_r ≈ 3.5 and d = 2 nm although the Methods give 1.5–2 nm, and electrons may sit partly in the MoSe2 layer far from the WSe2. The "expected injection threshold without interlayer Coulomb interactions" (red solid line in Fig. 2c, ED Figs. 3–5), the baseline for the shift, is not described (how computed, which g-factors and band offsets). And 6 T is where the trion regions are said to vanish in device 2 (Fig. 3c, ED Fig. 6), so the choice of field needs comment. The authors should describe the baseline, include both layers' quantum capacitances at finite field, propagate the uncertainties into an error bar on E_T,b, compare optical and transport estimates at matched field and temperature, and present the agreement with the valley Zeeman scale with these caveats.

7. The Hall results come from a single device, and phase boundaries are transferred between devices. Rxy and Rxy(drag), including the negative Rxy, the B dependence (Fig. 3c) and the T dependence (Fig. 4), come only from device 2 (Figs. 3, 4, ED Figs. 6, 8). The Rxx phase diagrams (Fig. 1d,e, ED Fig. 7) come from device 1, and the RC and Rxx(drag) maps (Fig. 2, ED Figs. 3–5) from device 3, which is not shown in ED Fig. 1 or described. The trion regions sit at very different Vb: about 0.74–0.78 V, 0.86–0.89 V and 0.84–0.90 V in devices 1, 2 and 3. Nevertheless the dashed boundaries "corresponding to those in Fig. 1f" are overlaid on all of them, so the trion assignment of the Hall features relies on boundaries determined optically in another device and cryostat. The authors should show Rxy and Rxy(drag), including the negative Rxy and the Hall-drag sign change (Figs. 3d, 4b), in at least one more device; state how the kite-shaped boundaries were determined in each device (from which data, by hand, by an electrostatic model, or copied and rescaled); overlay the RC-derived boundaries on Rxx(drag) and Rxy measured in the same device; add device 3 to ED Fig. 1 with a summary table of devices, Vb offsets and which figure comes from which device; and state whether the results survived thermal cycling.

8. A Hall effect, a response linear in B at fixed particle densities, is not demonstrated, because the field sweep changes the phase as well as the Lorentz force. Fig. 3c scans B at fixed Vb = 0.88 V, while the whole phase diagram shifts upward in Vb with B (ED Fig. 6a–d, where the EI apex moves from about 0.875 V to above 0.90 V). The trion region appears near 2 T and disappears near 6 T, so the appearance and disappearance of negative Rxy may partly reflect the fixed cut entering and leaving the triangles geometrically rather than trion stabilization. The authors should show Rxy and Rxy(drag) against B (antisymmetrized) at fixed (n, p) points that stay inside the trion regions over a finite field range, for example near the triangle apex, tracking the region in (Vg, Vb) if needed, or give Rxy maps at several fields with the boundaries marked. Exploiting the narrow B = 0 trion line along p = 2n (Fig. 1d) at low field (|B| ≤ 1–3 T), before Landau quantization and Zeeman polarization set in, would give the cleanest test of a Lorentz force on trions.

9. Why the trion regions appear and expand with B is not explained, and Landau quantization may shape them. At B = 0 the trion region is a narrow strip along p = 2n (Fig. 1d); at 2–6 T it becomes two broad triangles (Fig. 1e, Fig. 3c, ED Fig. 6). The text explains the disappearance at high field (valley Zeeman energy exceeding binding) but not the onset near 2 T or the growth. The authors' own estimate puts the cyclotron energy (≈0.8 meV at 4 T) at the binding-energy scale, and quantum oscillations appear inside the trion regions (ED Fig. 8); Fig. 3c shows vertical stripes. Landau quantization of the free carriers, rather than trion formation alone, could therefore shape the high-Rxx triangles and the Rxy structure. The authors should explain the field-induced stabilization (for example, valley Zeeman splitting relative to the Fermi energy of the minority species) and discuss the role of Landau quantization.
   Raised by: r1, r3

10. A semiclassical Drude framework is applied deep in an insulating regime. In the trion regions Rxx is far above h/e² (Fig. 1e, ED Fig. 7c) and dRxx/dT < 0. Eqs. (3) use Drude tensors with well-defined ωcτ for every species, and both n_H = B/(eRxy) as a carrier density and the picture of mobile trions under Lorentz deflection rest on that regime; in hopping or strongly localized transport the Hall coefficient is not simply related to carrier density or sign. The authors should give the estimated ωcτ and mobilities of each species at 4 T, discuss whether the large Rxx is intrinsic insulating behaviour or the open-circuit drag constraint (the Mo layer unable to carry counterflow), and justify the semiclassical tensor.

11. The gate calibration behind Fig. 3d is not documented. The dashed electron and hole densities come from "calibrated gate capacitances", but the hBN thicknesses, capacitances, how Vb enters n and p, the charge-neutrality reference, the calibration procedure (Hall density away from the EI, Landau-level fan) and the uncertainty are not given; the assumption that Δ is proportional to the field because the hBN layers are "nearly the same" thickness is also unquantified. The comparison of n_H with these lines is the key quantitative evidence on the positive-trion side and at 10 K, and concern 1 requires it. The 10 K curve also shows a peak near Vg ≈ 0.01 V reaching about 2.9 × 10^12 cm^-2, roughly twice the expected hole density; calling it a "weak anomaly" from "remnant electron-hole interactions" understates it. The authors should give the electrostatic model and parameters, show how it was checked, add error bands, and explain the 10 K anomaly.

13. The electronic structure of the bilayer MoSe2 electron layer is assumed to be monolayer-like. Figs. 1b and 2d and the Mo-layer polaron analysis (ED Fig. 3) treat the electron layer as a single spin-valley-locked monolayer. In 2H bilayer MoSe2 the conduction band carries an additional layer degree of freedom, and the spin-valley ordering differs between the two layers. This bears on the spin-singlet negative-trion argument, on identifying σ± polaron features with K/K′ electron occupation, and on the effective interlayer separation in C_2L. The authors should state the layer polarization of the electrons under the applied Δ and justify the monolayer-like valley picture, for example with optical signatures from both MoSe2 layers.

14. The optical and transport phase diagrams are compared under different conditions without checking for illumination effects. RC is measured in a different cryostat (1.6 K base, 9 T) with white-light illumination of a type-II heterobilayer, where photodoping or photoinduced interlayer charge transfer can shift thresholds; the intensity limit (below 50 nW/μm²) is stated without evidence that the ~1 meV threshold features in Fig. 2c and ED Figs. 4–5 are unaffected. The authors should show that the injection thresholds do not depend on light intensity (for example a power series) and estimate optical heating.
