A. Must address

1. The measured R-3 phase fraction does not track the magnetic suppression, and the ambient state contradicts the proposed mechanism. The central claim is that a growing fraction of A-A (P-3m1) stacking drives the suppression of ferromagnetism. However, Fig. 5b shows the R-3 volume fraction essentially flat at about 0.5–0.55 from ambient pressure (0.7 GPa) to about 5.7 GPa, falling only between about 6 and 8.4 GPa. Over that same range Tc falls from 33 K to about 17–19 K (Fig. 3), and the 10 K moment at 2 T falls from 2.8 to about 0.85–0.9 μB/f.u. at 5.1 GPa (Fig. 2). Most of the magnetic suppression therefore happens with no measured change in phase fraction. In addition, the starting sample already contains about 45–50% P-3m1 by Fig. 5b, yet at ambient pressure the crystal is a nearly saturated ferromagnet (Tc = 33 K, 2.8 μB/f.u., no antiferromagnetic contribution, p. 2). The authors' own simulation (Fig. 13b) gives Tc ≈ 28 K for 45% AA, about 11 K below the pure-AB value, and Fig. 13a gives a reduced ordering tendency. The Methods (p. 4) state that the best sample had ~80% R-3 at the start of the high-pressure experiment, which is inconsistent with the ~0.55 in Fig. 5b. Fig. 3 combines several runs, presumably on different crystals whose P-3m1 fractions were never measured, yet they fall on one curve, which suggests Tc is insensitive to the initial stacking-fault content. The authors should reconcile Fig. 5b with the Methods statement and with full ferromagnetism at ambient pressure; determine the initial phase fraction of each crystal used for magnetization; plot Tc and Ms against the measured AA fraction rather than against pressure; and either show quantitatively that the A-A concentration rises across 0–6 GPa or revise the mechanism (or restate it as a hypothesis).

2. The P-3m1 refinement does not establish A-A stacking, and the paper describes the high-pressure structure in two incompatible ways. Tables 1 and 3 give a CdI2-type subcell (a = 3.64 Å, c = 6.12 Å, Z = 1) with Cr disordered over a single 1a site at 2/3 occupancy, in which the honeycomb order is averaged away. This average structure is what one expects for random stacking among the three equivalent vacancy registries (random or turbostratic layer shifts), or for in-plane Cr/vacancy disorder. Ordered A-A stacking of complete honeycomb layers would instead keep the √3×√3 in-plane cell (a ≈ 6.3 Å, a P-31m-type cell with c ≈ 6.1 Å) and keep the honeycomb reflections at h − k ≠ 3n as sharp Bragg peaks; random stacking would turn them into diffuse rods along c*. Page 11 itself says the model arises when "the placement of the adjacent layer is randomized along the c-axis", yet p. 12, p. 17 and the Conclusions state that "the only viable option is the A-A type stacking fault". Neither scattering scenario is discussed, and the reciprocal-space images in Fig. 4 are too small to judge. The distinction is central because the DFT value JL = −0.35 meV is computed for one specific ordered A-A stacking, while random stacking would mix several interlayer registries with different signs of coupling (Ref. 36), so the magnetic interpretation depends on it. The authors should analyse the intensity along the honeycomb superstructure rods (h − k ≠ 3n, plotted along L), including diffuse scattering, at high pressure, in the unloaded crystal and in the high-resolution maps of Fig. 7, and state whether these reflections are absent, diffuse or sharp; fit a stacking-fault model (for example DIFFaX-type calculations with A-A, ABC and random-shift probabilities as parameters) to decide between ordered A-A, random stacking and in-plane disorder; and state which ordered supercell was used in DFT to represent "the AA phase" and how a structure with fractional Cr occupancy was mapped onto it.

3. There is no direct evidence for the proposed antiferromagnetic high-pressure state. The abstract and Conclusions propose "an antiferromagnetic phase emerging" beyond pc with intact Cr moments, but the only data above pc are a featureless M(T) at 0.01 T at 9.2 GPa (Fig. 1) and a nearly linear M(H) to 2 T at 10 K reaching about 0.5 μB/f.u. (Fig. 2). A Cr3+ (S = 3/2) Curie paramagnet at 10 K and 2 T gives about 0.6–0.7 μB, so these data are equally consistent with a paramagnet, a spin glass, a state with reduced moments, or a phase with strongly reduced intralayer exchange. The proposed state, with intralayer ferromagnetism intact and only the interlayer coupling reversed, predicts clear signatures: (i) a Néel transition with a susceptibility cusp at a temperature set mainly by the intralayer exchange (tens of K, comparable to the former Tc), so weak interlayer AFM coupling should not remove all ordering down to 2 K as the 9.2 GPa curve appears to show; (ii) a spin-flip or metamagnetic step in M(H) at a field set by |JL| = 0.35 meV per Cr, estimated at a few tesla, restoring the full ~3 μB/Cr; and (iii) a Curie-Weiss θ whose sign and size reflect dominant intralayer FM exchange. None is tested: the isotherms stop at 2 T at a single temperature, and the claim that "the individual Cr ion moments remain intact" is never checked. The authors should measure M(T) at several fields (for example 0.1, 1 and 5 T) above 6.5 GPa; measure M(H) to the maximum available field (7 T) at 2 K and at temperatures above any candidate ordering temperature, at several pressures; fit the high-temperature susceptibility to Curie-Weiss; state whether a Néel anomaly exists and estimate the expected spin-flip field; test whether the moment recovers toward 3 μB at high field; and compute the field-dependent magnetization of their spin model for comparison. Element-specific probes (XMCD under pressure) or neutron diffraction would test the moments and magnetic structure directly. Without such data the statement of an emerging antiferromagnetic phase should be withdrawn or clearly marked as speculation.

B. Should address

4. All structural data are at room temperature, while the magnetism is measured at 2–60 K, and the three critical pressures are compared as if they were the same quantity. Diffraction (Figs. 4, 5, Tables 1 and S1; 289/296 K) and Raman (Fig. 10) are at room temperature. The paper itself notes that the R-3/monoclinic stacking of CrX3 depends on temperature (first-order transition at 420 K), and stacking transitions in CrI3 shift and show hysteresis on cooling; both the stacking energetics and the R-3/P-3m1 boundary may change on cooling. The causal claim therefore assumes that room-temperature phase fractions hold at 10–30 K. The loading and cooling sequence of each experiment is not stated. The magnetic pc (6.5 GPa, low T), the XRD transition (6.4–8.4 GPa, RT) and the Raman pc (~10 GPa, RT, on the same type of sample) are quoted together without reconciliation. The authors should provide low-temperature structural data under pressure (low-T single-crystal or powder synchrotron XRD in a DAC at a few pressures across 3–9 GPa, neutron diffraction, or at minimum low-T Raman), or argue quantitatively why the room-temperature boundary applies at 10 K; state the thermal and pressure history of each magnetization run; and discuss whether the differences between the three critical pressures come from temperature, sample or calibration.

5. The atomistic simulations do not reproduce the effect they are said to "corroborate". The Monte Carlo (Fig. 13) stops at 45% AA, where Tc = 28 K; it never shows Tc → 0, an AFM ground state, or the experimental pc and the Tc = 6 K point at 6.4 GPa. Pure AB stacking gives 39 K against 33 K measured. Fig. 13a shows M/M0 → 1 at low T for every AA fraction, so the model does not account for the large drop of the saturated moment in Fig. 2. The intralayer J1–J3 are fixed at ambient-pressure literature values (Ref. 73, not computed consistently), although the Introduction (p. 2) cites DFT predicting that the exchange increases with pressure, which would raise Tc; JL is taken at zero pressure. AA and AB "atoms" are assigned randomly per site (p. 16), whereas stacking is a property of whole interfaces between layers, so a site-random sign of the interlayer coupling is not the physical disorder. Given that intralayer exchange dominates, the authors should explain physically how antiferromagnetic coupling on some interfaces can drive Tc toward zero rather than produce a ferrimagnetic or A-type state with Tc near the intralayer scale. They should model disorder per interface; use pressure-dependent J1–J3 and JL from DFT at the experimental lattice parameters; extend the AA fraction to 100% and report the resulting ordered state and its ordering temperature; and compare simulated Tc(p), M(T) and M(H) at 2 T directly with Figs. 1–3, using the measured phase fractions as input.

6. The magnetization of a decompressed P-3m1 crystal, the most direct test of the mechanism, is not reported. Table S1 shows the crystal remains P-3m1 after unloading (a = 3.6498 Å, no 1/3 satellites). If P-3m1/A-A stacking carries antiferromagnetic interlayer coupling, such a crystal should show no or a strongly reduced spontaneous moment at ambient pressure, separating the effect of stacking from that of compression without a DAC. The authors should measure M(T) and M(H) of a pressure-cycled crystal (and, where possible, of deliberately cut or deformed crystals with known P-3m1 fractions) at ambient pressure, and state whether the magnetization in the DAC recovers on releasing pressure.

8. The absolute normalization and background subtraction of the DAC magnetization are not described, and the moment-versus-pressure trend depends on them. Fig. 2 compares absolute moments (μB/f.u.) across pressures for a microgram-scale sample, with background from a separate empty-cell run. At 1.1 GPa the 2 T moment drops by about 25–30% (2.8 to about 2.0–2.1 μB/f.u.) while Tc changes by only 1–2 K (31 K, Fig. 3); at T/Tc ≈ 0.3 thermal reduction cannot explain this, and it is hard to reconcile with any homogeneous mechanism, so it could reflect mass, background or sample-integrity errors (partial loss or fracture). The ambient curve in Fig. 1 (smooth, green) may come from a different crystal measured by PPMS VSM (Methods mentions both). The 0.01 T M(T) values (~0.08 μB/f.u.) are governed by domains and demagnetization, so their pressure dependence in Fig. 1 does not measure Ms. The authors should state how the sample mass or volume was determined and its uncertainty; whether the ambient reference was measured on the same crystal inside the loaded cell; show raw sample and empty-cell signals, and the background size relative to the sample signal at 2 T, especially at the highest pressures; address demagnetization for the out-of-plane plate geometry; give error bars on Ms or normalized single-sample curves; and if possible re-measure at ambient pressure after release.

9. Pressure calibration and hydrostaticity at low temperature are not established, which matters for a material the authors describe as damaged by "any kind of physical incursions". The paper does not say where and at what temperature the ruby was read in the Be-Cu turnbuckle cell, and pressure in such cells typically changes on cooling. The methanol-ethanol-water 16:3:1 medium is a glass below ~200 K, and Methods (p. 4) quote a hydrostatic limit of ~14 GPa whereas Refs. 45–46 place it near 10 GPa, coinciding with the Raman pc. Since cutting creates stacking faults (p. 3, Fig. S1a), non-hydrostatic stress developing on cooling could create stacking faults independently of pressure and differ between runs, in which case the result would describe stress-induced damage rather than a pressure-driven phase. The authors should state the temperature and location of each pressure determination with its uncertainty (or the estimated low-T correction); report ruby linewidths or R1–R2 splitting versus pressure, or a pressure gradient across the culet; correct the hydrostatic limit; discuss whether runs with different loadings and cooling protocols give the same Tc(p) (Fig. 3 suggests they might, which would be a useful argument); and ideally repeat key points with another medium (for example Daphne 7575 or a gas medium).

10. The fit that determines pc has the wrong sign and no uncertainty, and the "loss of ferromagnetism" rests on an extrapolation. Eq. (1) on p. 6 and the label in Fig. 3 write Tc = Tc(0)(1 − p/pc)^(−1/3), which diverges at pc; the exponent must be +1/3. The exponent is not motivated, the paper does not say whether it was fixed or free, no fit uncertainty is given, and Tc is not defined from the 0.01 T M(T) curves (onset, inflection, dM/dT minimum). With Tc(0) fixed, pc = 6.5 GPa is set almost entirely by the 6.4 GPa point (Tc ≈ 6 K, near the 2 K base temperature); only two points (5.9 and 6.4 GPa) lie above 5.1 GPa and there are no data between 6.4 and 9.2 GPa. The authors should correct the equation, justify or free the exponent, give pc with an error bar, compare with a linear or other fit, define Tc, add a pressure between 6.5 and 9 GPa if feasible, and otherwise state the result as "Tc below 2 K above ~6.5 GPa"; the comparison with the 8.4 GPa linear extrapolation of Ref. 20 should be framed accordingly.

15. The Raman assignment is not supported by a factor-group analysis. In R-3 (C3i) the Raman-active modes are 4Ag + 4Eg, so the "A2g" label for the 181.8 cm⁻¹ mode (p. 14) is inconsistent. A P-3m1 cell with Cr at 1a and Br at 2d has only A1g + Eg Raman-active modes, yet four or five modes (Ph1, Ph3, Ph6, Ph7, Ph8; red lines in Fig. 10) are assigned to P-3m1 on the basis of pressure slopes alone. Several modes evolve continuously through pc with only a slope change (Ph5→Ph8, Ph4→Ph7), which fits a single phase stiffening better than a superposition of two phases. The spectra are described as polarized but no polarization analysis is shown. The authors should give the mode count for each candidate structure (including P-31m for true A-A stacking, or an ordered AA supercell), use parallel and crossed polarization to assign symmetries, fit peak positions and widths with uncertainties instead of relying on guide lines on a colour map, and compare as-grown and cut crystals at ambient pressure to test whether the "hard" modes scale with the XRD-determined P-3m1 fraction.
