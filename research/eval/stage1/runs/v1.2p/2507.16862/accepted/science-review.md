A. Must address

1. **A uniform phase histogram at a fixed lab time does not show that the phase is chosen at random when the condensate forms; shot-to-shot jitter in the condensate frequency or onset time would produce the same histogram.** The claim of spontaneous phase formation rests on the Fig. 2a insets at t = 1.25 µs and the "Phase evolution of the BEC" section (p. 1433: "the uniform phase distribution must be an intrinsic property of the BEC"). But the phase is read against a 3.2-GHz LO at a fixed time, about 0.25 µs after pump-off and roughly 0.3–0.5 µs after the coherent signal first appears. Over that interval the condensate frequency chirps by roughly 100 MHz, from about 3.42–3.45 GHz to about 3.32 GHz (Fig. 3a; Extended Data Fig. 2a,c), through the density-dependent nonlinear shift, so it depends on magnon number. The accumulated phase is φ(t) = φ0 + 2π∫f(t′)dt′. A shot-to-shot spread of only δf ≈ 2–4 MHz in the frequency trajectory (from pump-power noise, magnon-number fluctuations, sample temperature) or about 10 ns in onset time gives a phase spread 2π·δf·Δt larger than 2π, so the histogram would be uniform even if φ0 were fixed by the pump-pair dynamics. Such variation is plausible near threshold, given the large ensemble spread in amplitude in Fig. 4b. The narrow grey pump-phase sector does not rule this out, because the pump has a fixed frequency and no chirp. The 0.10-µs and 0.90-µs histograms add nothing here, because at those times the band-bottom signal is noise-dominated (Fig. 2a left panels; Fig. 4b). As it stands, the headline test does not separate "random phase set at formation" (spontaneous U(1) symmetry breaking) from "deterministic phase scrambled by jitter" (not symmetry breaking). The authors should: (a) report the shot-to-shot distribution of instantaneous frequency and amplitude at onset and at 1.25 µs, and estimate the phase spread that frequency jitter alone would produce; (b) show the phase distribution as close to onset as possible (for example where the coherence value first clearly exceeds baseline, around 0.6–0.8 µs in Fig. 3b) and plot the circular spread (mean resultant length R) against time from onset through the plateau: under deterministic-plus-jitter R starts near 1 and decays, under spontaneous formation it is near 1/√N from the first coherent cycles; (c) for each shot, extract f(t) and onset time, subtract the accumulated phase 2π∫f dt from a common reference point (for example the moment the amplitude first crosses a fixed threshold), and show that the residual phase is still uniform; (d) plot phase against each shot's onset time or integrated amplitude, since jitter-set phases would correlate with these; (e) report these statistics at each pump power across threshold (Fig. 4 powers), so that phase randomness is seen to appear together with condensation rather than from onset jitter near threshold; and (f) ideally, run a positive control: inject a weak coherent seed at the condensate frequency, phase-locked to the reference, before or during pumping, show that the same chain and analysis give a narrow histogram, and show that it broadens as the seed power is reduced. That would establish both that the method can detect a locked phase and where spontaneous selection takes over.

2. **The phase stability of the reference and detection chain is asserted but not shown.** The text (p. 1433) says that "all externally induced factors are stable within a few degrees of phase shift", but Extended Data Fig. 1 states that the oscilloscope was not locked to the shared 10-MHz reference "due to technical difficulties" and that phase stability "was confirmed in preliminary measurements", which are not shown. The grey "pump phase" sectors in Fig. 2a are the reference against which the BEC phase is called random, yet the paper does not say how the 7.8-GHz pump phase was measured through a chain that removes it (diplexer, 5-GHz and <4-GHz low-pass filters F1, F2) and that lies outside the 3.2-GHz IQ band, nor why the bars appear only in the first two insets. The grey sectors also look several tens of degrees wide, not "a few degrees". Other open points: whether the pulse generator's gating of SG2 is synchronous with the 7.8-GHz carrier, and the oscilloscope trigger jitter relative to the roughly 120-MHz IF period (about 8 ns). If the reference jitters by tens of degrees or the unlocked scope's trigger jitter is a sizeable fraction of the IF period, the uniformity claim loses its footing. The authors should describe how the pump phase was measured and give its spread numerically, and show a reference measurement: a CW or pulsed tone near 3.3 GHz derived from the locked synthesizers, sent through the identical path (antenna, diplexer, filters, amplifier, IQ mixer, unsynchronised oscilloscope) with the same pulse timing and repetition rate, giving a narrow phase histogram over 1,000 triggers at the same delay as the BEC measurement, with its width in degrees.

3. **The data do not establish that the coherent k ≈ 0 state is a thermalised Bose–Einstein condensate rather than a noise-seeded nonequilibrium instability, and a random phase alone does not single out a BEC.** A random phase seeded by noise is generic to any coherent mode that grows from fluctuations: a laser above threshold, parametric self-oscillation, auto-oscillation, or the kinetic instability that the authors themselves describe on p. 1432 as directly populating the spectral minimum, together with cascade four-magnon scattering (which might also carry phase correlations with the pump pairs). The signatures offered for BEC (a narrow line at the band bottom above a pump threshold) are shared with these processes. The paper shows no magnon distribution over energy or wavevector, no evidence of a Bose–Einstein-like distribution with μ reaching the band bottom, and no thermalisation timescale compared with theory. This matters especially in the perpendicular geometry: the authors cite a published comment (ref. 32) that questions BEC and spin superfluidity in perpendicularly magnetised YIG, but do not discuss its arguments on the sign of the effective interaction and the stability of the uniform mode; the upward frequency shift at high density implied by Fig. 3a is relevant. The authors should: provide spectrally (ideally k-) resolved occupation data, for example time-resolved BLS on the same sample or the antenna response across the band, showing a thermalised distribution feeding the k ≈ 0 peak with a delay consistent with four-magnon scattering rates; show that condensation follows gas density rather than the instantaneous pump (dependence on pump duration and power); compare the roughly 0.6-µs delay before any signal (Fig. 3a) with expected thermalisation and kinetic-instability timescales; if earlier work (refs 27, 28) establishes thermalisation under the same conditions, state the matching film, field, pump frequency and power; address ref. 32 explicitly; and, failing such evidence, state what would distinguish the scenarios in their data and narrow the claim to the spontaneous phase of a coherent band-bottom state. The abstract and Discussion statements that the result "confirm[s] a central prediction of Bose–Einstein condensation theory" and that random phase is "required ... to prove the formation of a true BEC" should be tempered: random phase is necessary but not sufficient.

4. **The coherence value does not distinguish a coherent condensate from a narrowband incoherent (thermal-like) population.** As defined in the Methods, the coherence value is the integral of the normalised |autocorrelation| of the single-shot signal over a 100-ns window, relative to a pure tone. It measures spectral purity within one shot: any signal with linewidth well below 1/(100 ns) = 10 MHz, including a single-mode thermal (chaotic) field or a thermalised gas piled up in a band-bottom mode, approaches 1. The rise in Figs. 3b and 4a may therefore track spectral narrowing (already known from BLS) and the emergence of the signal above noise, rather than the formation of a coherent state, which is the headline. The authors should use the single-shot amplitudes they already have to test field statistics: over the 1,000 shots at fixed t, give the |z|² (amplitude) histogram and the intensity correlation g^(2)(0) = ⟨|z|^4⟩/⟨|z|^2⟩^2 after subtracting the noise contribution, in the pre-condensate, onset and plateau windows. A coherent condensate gives a Rician/narrow amplitude distribution with g^(2) → 1; a thermal mode gives a Rayleigh/exponential intensity distribution with g^(2) = 2. They should also compare the measured condensate linewidth with the 10-MHz window resolution so readers can see what a high coherence value actually excludes.

5. **The coherence value is uncalibrated: it depends on signal-to-noise ratio, chirp, amplitude modulation and spurious tones, so it is not a clean measure of phase coherence.** Pure noise gives a baseline of about 0.18 (explained only in Methods), not 0 as the Fig. 3b caption implies. A perfectly phase-coherent signal that chirps by tens of MHz within 100 ns, or whose amplitude changes within the window, gives a value below 1; the authors themselves attribute the dip at pump-off (about 1.0–1.1 µs, Figs. 3b, 4a) to the nonlinear frequency shift, which is chirp, not decoherence. The SNR dependence is visible in the paper: the low-amplitude 22-dBm curve saturates at about 0.45 rather than about 0.75, and the Fig. 4 caption states both that coherence "does not depend on the absolute amplitude" and that the 21-dBm increase "is not very pronounced" because of low SNR, while the text attributes the late decay to SNR loss. These statements need reconciling. Stationary spurious tones (item 7) can raise the value. The authors should compute the calibration curve C_w(SNR) for an ideal tone in the measured noise, using the 100-ns window and actual sampling rate, and compute the metric for synthetic signals: (i) pure tone, (ii) narrowband Gaussian (thermal) field of the measured linewidth, (iii) tone with the measured chirp, (iv) tone with the measured amplitude envelope, each added to the recorded noise at the observed SNR, and for pump-off or no-pump traces processed identically. Then plot measured coherence against measured SNR across pump powers, rescale or subtract the noise baseline, show which model reproduces the data and whether the plateau value of about 0.75 rather than 1 comes from noise, chirp or finite linewidth. A complementary standard measure, such as the within-shot phase-noise spectrum or linewidth after chirp removal, or g¹(τ) of the demodulated, chirp-corrected signal, would help.

6. **The claims that the condensate "does not undergo dephasing" and that coherence persists after the signal falls below noise are not established.** On p. 1434 the paper states that "the BEC is phase-stable until the signal vanishes" and that at t = 3.5 µs "the amplitude of the signal has vanished below the noise threshold, but a clear remanence in coherence can be observed". The coherence value only probes 100-ns windows, and Fig. 4a shows it falling steadily from about 1.8 µs, following the amplitude, which is what both SNR loss and true dephasing would produce; without the pure-tone C_w(SNR) curve (item 5) the two cannot be separated. At 3.5 µs the 25-dBm curve is within a few hundredths of the 0.18 baseline. The authors should quantify long-time phase coherence within a shot directly, for example g¹(Δt) for delays of 0.1–1 µs after removing each shot's deterministic chirp, or the residual phase deviation from a smooth phase model over the whole plateau, and compare the resulting coherence time with the magnon lifetime and with the spectrum-analyser linewidth (Fig. 3a); overlay on Fig. 4a the expected coherence for an ideal tone with the measured amplitude decay; and test the 3.5-µs "remanence" for significance against pump-off traces processed the same way over the same ensemble, and against the artifact level (item 7). Otherwise these claims should be removed.

7. **IQ-mixer artifacts overlap the condensate band.** Extended Data Fig. 2c shows "a constant band at 3.77 GHz and a shadow between 3.2 GHz and 3.3 GHz", next to the condensate at about 3.32 GHz; with the LO at 3.2 GHz the IQ image lies near 3.08 GHz. A stationary spurious tone is itself coherent and would raise the coherence value and could bias phases toward a fixed value; a phase-unstable spur could add phase noise; IQ gain and phase imbalance can distort phase histograms. The authors should identify the origin of these features, quantify their amplitude relative to the condensate signal, calibrate and correct the IQ imbalance, state whether the artifacts set the 0.18 baseline, and report the coherence value and phase histogram after notching them out and for pump-off (or sub-threshold, 20 dBm) traces computed in the same windows, showing that the baseline and plateau value do not change.

8. **Shot independence and heating are not documented.** The repetition period of the 1,000 shots, the magnon lifetime relative to it, and heating of the film by 1-µs pulses at 23–25 dBm (about 0.3 W, after +50 dB amplification) are not given. Residual magnons, residual coherent precession or thermal memory from a previous shot could seed or correlate the next one, which bears directly on the phase statistics; heating could also shift M_s and hence the condensate frequency. The authors should state the repetition rate and waiting time, show that the system relaxes fully between shots, show that consecutive-shot phases are uncorrelated (for example an autocorrelation of φ_n against shot index), and check that the result survives a change in duty cycle.

9. **The argument that the parametric magnons carry no phase reference rests on a non-observation the detection chain may be unable to make.** On p. 1432 the paper states that no coherent signal at f_p/2 = 3.9 GHz is observed and takes this as evidence of phase randomisation among parametric pairs. But 3.9 GHz lies at the 4-GHz diplexer crossover and at the edge of the F2 "< 4 GHz" low-pass filter (Extended Data Fig. 1); the antenna suppresses 18-µm waves by about a factor of 18 (as stated); and 3.9 GHz mixed with the 3.2-GHz LO gives a 700-MHz IF that should be checked against the IQ-mixer IF port and oscilloscope bandwidth. The detection window is only "several hundred megahertz" above the Kittel frequency. Since pairs are phase-locked to the pump through φ_k + φ_−k = φ_p, this also bears on item 1. The authors should give the measured transfer function of the full receive chain (filter roll-off, amplifier gain) at 3.3 and 3.9 GHz and the expected f_p/2 signal level if the pairs were coherent, turning the non-observation into a quantitative bound, or qualify the statement so readers can tell absence from suppression. A phase-sensitive measurement at f_p/2 synchronous with the pump would address this directly.

10. **The uniformity of the phase histograms is not tested statistically.** The polar histograms in Fig. 2a are shown without a quantitative test, bin count or normalisation. 12. **The paper does not observe the incoherent stage it says it tracks.** The abstract says the measurement reveals "how an initially incoherent magnon gas thermalizes and undergoes a spontaneous transition". 14. **The novelty statements are stronger than the literature supports.** The introduction states that the BEC phase "has only been revealed through phenomena dependent on spatial phase differences" and that observing spontaneous coherence "has been impossible"; the Discussion states that "no direct proof of randomness in the established condensate phase has yet been provided". This overlooks interference of independently prepared atomic condensates showing a random relative phase (ref. 14), temporal phase coherence and phase jumps in photon BECs coupled to a reservoir, shot-to-shot spontaneous polarisation build-up in polariton condensates, ref. 36 (Demidov et al., "Observation of spontaneous coherence in Bose–Einstein condensate of magnons"), earlier inductive detection of microwave radiation from magnon condensates, and ref. 11 (Snoke and Girvin) on phase-coherence onset. The authors should position the work against these and state precisely what is new (single-shot, pump-referenced phase of a magnon condensate), tempering "first" and "proof" language.

15. **The explanation of the coherence oscillations is speculative and untested.** The plateau oscillations (period about 0.2–0.3 µs, 1.2–2 µs in Figs. 3b and 4a) are attributed on p. 1434 to incoherent magnons reflected from the sample edges, which the authors call "still a subject of investigation". Because these oscillations sit in the plateau used to argue for full coherence, the hypothesis should be tested or clearly labelled as conjecture. Alternatives to consider include beating between the condensate and a nearby mode or residual chirp within the window, and relaxation oscillations between the pump-fed reservoir and the condensate.
