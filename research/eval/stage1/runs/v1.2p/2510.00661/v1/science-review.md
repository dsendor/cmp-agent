A. Must address

1. No quantitative estimate shows that Harper broadening can be appreciable at 300–500 T in Cu2O (pp. 8–9, Fig. 4b). The only argument given is that at 300 T, ℓ_B ≈ 14.8 Å ≈ 3.5 a0 and α = φ/φ0 ≈ 0.013 (q ≈ 77, i.e. about 77 unit cells per flux quantum). In the Harper/Hofstadter problem at such small α, the width the periodic potential gives to the lowest Landau-level-derived states near a band edge is controlled by the overlap of neighbouring cyclotron orbits and is expected to be exponentially small in 1/α. Moreover ħω_c at 300 T (about 80 meV for μ* = 0.415) is far below the eV-scale widths of the Cu 4s conduction band and Cu 3d valence bands, so the carriers should still see a well-averaged potential; the leading lattice correction at ℓ_B/a0 = 3.5 should be a smooth band non-parabolicity of order (a0/ℓ_B)², not a threshold. Since ℓ_B falls smoothly as B^(-1/2), nothing in the proposed mechanism explains a "sudden" onset at 300 T. The paper also describes the mechanism as "the significant increase of the degeneracy of the energy levels" (p. 9), which is backwards: Harper physics lifts the Landau-level degeneracy and splits each level into subbands. Finally, the exciton is neutral, so the authors need to explain how electron and hole Harper broadening would carry over to a bound 2p0 state whose extent along B is set by the Coulomb interaction. The paper itself only calls the agreement "at least qualitative" (p. 9), yet the title and abstract rest on it, and the observed excess width at 450–500 T is several meV (roughly 4–20 meV depending on how it is read from Fig. 4a). The authors should compute the expected broadening, for example with a tight-binding model of the Cu2O conduction and valence bands using realistic hopping parameters, the real cubic lattice and Peierls phases for B ∥ [001], treating both carriers (or with a lattice-corrected magneto-exciton calculation), and overlay the predicted extra FWHM versus B (0–500 T) on Fig. 4a. If the prediction is orders of magnitude too small, the title and abstract must change; if no estimate can be produced, Harper broadening should be presented as one speculative candidate among several, and the mechanism should be stated precisely.

2. Intrinsic, effective-mass-level explanations for both the broadening and the energy shortfall above 300 T are not considered (pp. 6–9, Fig. 2, Fig. 4a, Fig. S3). Each of the following is expected in this field range in Cu2O and would produce excess width and a slower-than-hydrogenic shift without lattice-scale physics. (i) Level crossing and mixing: the text calls 2p0(Sz=+1) "well separated from other excitonic transitions", but the authors' own Fig. S3(a) shows several calculated levels, notably 2p−1(Sz=0) (grey dashed), 2p−1(Sz=+1), 2p0(Sz=0) and branches descending from 3p, approaching or crossing the 2p0(Sz=+1) curve between 200 and 500 T; the measured points lie between the purple and grey dashed curves. An unresolved neighbouring line, or an anticrossing with such a level or with a field-brightened s/d or dark state, would give both apparent broadening and a downward energy deviation. (ii) Valence-band non-parabolicity and yellow (Γ7+)–green (Γ8+) mixing: the spin-orbit splitting is about 130 meV, while the reduced-mass cyclotron energy reaches about 80 meV near 300 T and about 140 meV at 500 T, and the hole cyclotron energy is several tens of meV. As the wavefunction is squeezed to larger k the effective hole mass grows — the same physics behind the known quantum defect of the P series — which naturally yields the "increase of reduced effective mass" reported on p. 3. (iii) Magneto-polaron resonances and screening: the internal energy spacings approach and exceed the Cu2O LO-phonon energies (about 19 and 82 meV); the hole cyclotron energy (0.116 meV/T × B / m_h with m_h ≈ 0.58–0.69) crosses the ~82 meV Γ4− LO mode between roughly 400 and 500 T, and resonant Fröhlich coupling and the crossover from static to high-frequency screening would shift and broaden the level. The authors should, in Fig. 4a and Fig. S3, mark which calculated levels lie within one FWHM of 2p0(Sz=+1) at each field; fit the 350–500 T spectra with and without an additional component; show the 2p0(Sz=+1) energy residuals (data minus model) versus B; and estimate each mechanism quantitatively with literature band parameters and phonon energies (at minimum a multiband k·p estimate of the yellow–green correction, compared with the observed energy shortfall of about 8–10 meV at 500 T), explaining why the data favour a lattice-periodicity effect.

3. The >300 T anomaly rests on a few points from a single technique, with no stated reproducibility or controls on experimental artefacts (Fig. 4a, Fig. 2, Methods C–D). Both the extra width and the energy shortfall come only from the EMFC points at 350, 400, 450 and 500 T; neither NDPM nor STC data reach above about 150–200 T to cross-check them, and since the same crystal was used throughout, the EMFC result appears to be a single shot on a single sample. The manuscript does not state: how many EMFC (and STC) shots were taken and whether the upturn appears in more than one shot or in the up- and down-sweep of one shot; how the field at the sample was measured (pickup coil, Faraday rotation) and calibrated, and its uncertainty at 300–500 T; the field homogeneity across the 1.8 × 1.8 mm sample and the 800 µm probed spot inside the imploding liner; the streak-camera time window and resolution, dB/dt at each field, and hence the field interval averaged per spectrum, and how the B(t) trace was synchronised with the spectra; the spectral resolution of the STC/EMFC polychromator compared with the NDPM spectrometer; whether the EMFC spectra were polarisation-resolved (unpolarised detection would superpose the rapidly rising σ− lines); and the sample temperature during the shot and why 45 K was used. Field inhomogeneity and field averaging each broaden the line by (dE/dB)·ΔB, which grows as dE/dB for 2p0(Sz=+1) rises above 200 T. Sample heating during the pulse (eddy currents, shock) would produce exactly the observed pair of signatures — red shift from gap shrinkage plus phonon broadening — starting from 45 K. A few-percent field error would move points by 10–25 T, comparable to the claimed energy deviation given the curve's slope. The authors should report the number of shots and show the upturn in at least two independent EMFC runs (or up- and down-sweep data); give the field uncertainty and inhomogeneity in tesla at each field and the field change within each exposure, convert these into linewidth contributions using dE/dB from Fig. 2, and subtract or bound them in Fig. 4a; add horizontal (field) error bars to Figs. 2 and 4; state the polarisation configuration and resolution for EMFC; and give an estimate or proxy of the sample temperature near peak field (for example from the 3p or background absorption edge, or from a zero-field temperature series of the 2p linewidth and energy at 45–150 K showing what heating would be needed to mimic the data).

4. The high-field linewidths depend on fits of a weak feature that are not shown, and their robustness is not established (Fig. 1b, Fig. 3, Fig. 4a). In Fig. 1b the 2p0(Sz=+1) feature at 350–500 T is a small dip, barely above the noise, on the low-energy tail of a steep, rising absorption step that moves through 2.17–2.20 eV, so the extracted width depends strongly on the background model and on the line shape — exactly where the upturn appears. Only one fit, at 120 T where the feature is strong, is shown (Fig. 3); the background there (orange) is a hand-chosen smooth curve whose form above 300 T is not shown. The line shape is called "multiple Gaussian functions" in the Fig. 3 caption but "asymmetrical Gaussian functions" in the text, with no functional form and no definition of FWHM for an asymmetric profile. The origin of the FWHM error bars (fit standard error? shot-to-shot spread?) is not explained, and the 500 T point has the smallest error bar although it is the weakest feature. The authors should show the fits at 300, 350, 400, 450 and 500 T with residuals; state which parameters were free and fixed, including asymmetry and background form; give the line-shape function and FWHM definition; report how the FWHM changes under reasonable alternative backgrounds (polynomial order, fit window) and symmetric versus asymmetric line shapes; explain the error bars; and plot the integrated intensity of the line versus field, to distinguish a genuine broadening at constant oscillator strength from a fading line whose width is poorly constrained.

5. The argument that excludes the 2p±1 assignment uses the hydrogen-atom form of the orbital Zeeman term (p. 6 point (ii); SI Sec. I, Fig. S2). The paper states that "the exactly ħω_c splitting of the 2p±1 states is imposed by time reversal symmetry", with ω_c = eB/μ, and concludes that the 2p±1 assignment would require μ ≈ 2 m_e, "five times" too large. That holds only when one particle is infinitely heavy. For an exciton at zero centre-of-mass momentum with comparable masses, the linear orbital term in the relative-motion Hamiltonian is (eħB/2)(1/m_e − 1/m_h)·m, not (eħB/2μ)·m. With literature values m_e ≈ 0.99–1.0 and m_h ≈ 0.58–0.7 m_e, the expected 2p+1–2p−1 slope is about 0.05–0.08 meV/T, the same order as the observed 0.11–0.116 meV/T in Fig. S2; the two hypotheses predict nearly the same linear slope (they overlap in Fig. S2), so the slope alone cannot discriminate, and the m ≠ 0 branches in Fig. S3 (Makado–McGill plus spin Zeeman only) omit this term. Further gaps: the yellow series is said to "originate from an electric quadrupole transition" (p. 5; SI p. 3), but in Elliott's classification the np excitons are dipole-allowed (Γ4−); it is 1s that is quadrupole-allowed, so the selection-rule reasoning built on this must be redone; the Γ7+ hole is a spin-orbit-coupled state, so the pure "Sz" labels and the "hole g-factor close to zero" need justification and a literature value with uncertainty for B ∥ [001]; and the statement that "σ± couples strongly only with spin-flip transitions regardless of m" (SI Sec. II, p. 5) is asserted from fits, not derived, and appears to contradict the selection rules stated a paragraph earlier. Because the assignment is a headline claim of the abstract and both μ* and the "deviation from the hydrogen model" depend on it, the authors should redo the comparison in Fig. S2 with the correct (1/m_e − 1/m_h) coefficient and literature masses, state which Hamiltonian (with or without the mass-asymmetry term) was used in the fits, derive the Faraday-geometry σ± selection rules for the Γ5−/Γ4− p-exciton states with the Γ7+ hole for B ∥ k ∥ [001] (O_h → C4h), including the hole g-factor, ideally as a table of which of the nine 2p sublevels appear in σ+ and σ− and with what relative strength, and discuss explicitly how and why their assignment differs from Artyukhin et al. (ref. 18), Zielińska-Raczyńska et al. (ref. 11) and Kobayashi et al. (ref. 27).

B. Should address

6. How μ* = 0.415 ± 0.01 m_e was extracted is not described well enough to support its uncertainty (Fig. 2, Table I, p. 6, SI Sec. II). Ry* = 103 meV is "imposed" by the zero-field 2p–3p splitting under a pure 1/n² law, but the Cu2O P series has known non-zero quantum defects (ref. 24), and Ry* is 5% above the accepted 98 meV; this biases μ*, which the diamagnetic curvature fixes only jointly with Ry* (equivalently ε). E_g = 2.1756 eV is given without an uncertainty (fixed or fitted?) and differs from commonly cited values, and it is not stated how g = 2.0 ± 0.1 was obtained (Fig. S2 or global fit). The 3p and 2p0(Sz=−1) branches are followed only to about 130 T, so above that the mass is effectively set by the single 2p0(Sz=+1) branch between about 130 and 300 T, and "fits very well to the four levels up to 300 T" overstates the constraint; since the fit range ends exactly where the deviation is claimed, the deviation is partly defined by that choice. The authors should state which parameters were free, which points and field range entered the fit and with what weighting, give the covariances among μ*, Ry* and g, and show how μ* changes if Ry* is set to 98 meV, a quantum defect is included, or the fit stops at 150, 200 or 250 T. They should also discuss why μ* lies at or above the top of the literature range (0.35–0.41 m_e), e.g. polaron corrections, mass anisotropy or central-cell effects.

7. The case for a "sudden" change rather than smooth superlinear growth is not made statistically (Fig. 4a, Fig. 4b). The blue line is a guide fitted up to 300 T; relative to it, the 350 and 400 T points sit about 0.7 above with error bars of about ±0.5, so only the 450 and 500 T points clearly deviate, and a quadratic or power law over the full range might describe the data equally well. Fig. 4b, plotted on a log ℓ_B axis with a vertical line at 300 T, gives a visual impression of a threshold that the theory does not motivate. The authors should compare linear, linear-plus-threshold, quadratic and power-law models with a goodness-of-fit or information criterion, so that "sudden" or "abrupt" is supported rather than read off the plot.

8. Data taken at different temperatures and with different instruments are combined in one normalised plot (Figs. 2 and 4, Methods). NDPM data are at 1.3 K, STC at 10 K and EMFC at 45 K, yet all are divided by a single zero-field FWHM (≃ 2.9 meV) whose temperature and setup are not stated; phonon broadening of the 2p line between 1.3 and 45 K is not negligible, and the streak-camera/polychromator resolution likely differs from the NDPM CCD spectrometer. The linear trend up to 300 T that defines the baseline for the upturn may therefore partly reflect normalisation and resolution differences. The authors should give the zero-field FWHM and instrumental resolution for each technique and temperature, normalise each data set by its own zero-field width (or plot absolute FWHM with the instrumental contribution stated), and ideally measure the zero-field 2p FWHM versus temperature (1.3–45 K) with the relevant optics to show that 45 K neither sets nor changes the field dependence.

10. The physical picture of the exciton reaching lattice scale is not put on numbers (pp. 8–9, Fig. 4b). The argument compares the free-carrier ℓ_B with a0, but the relevant object is the bound 2p0 exciton: its transverse size is set by the relative-motion magnetic length and its extent along B is larger. At 300 T ℓ_B ≈ 15 Å is similar to the 1s Bohr radius (around 7–10 Å for ε ≈ 7.4, μ* = 0.415). The authors should give the in-plane and longitudinal sizes of 2p0 at 300 and 500 T from the hydrogen model, explain why lattice effects should switch on at ℓ_B/a0 = 3.5 rather than another ratio, and justify using the cubic-cell flux α (p. 9) for a six-atom cell whose Cu sublattice has a different spacing.

13. The linear-in-B broadening below 300 T, which serves as the baseline for the upturn, has no physical explanation (Fig. 4a, p. 8). 15. Possible absorption saturation or stray light in the 48 µm sample is not addressed (Methods A, Fig. 1b). Near 2.17–2.2 eV the yellow-series continuum and phonon-assisted background make the crystal strongly absorbing, and the high-field EMFC spectra approach the noise floor there; saturated optical density or stray light flattens and distorts line shapes and can bias widths. The authors should show raw transmitted intensities and the reference spectrum used to compute absorption, state the maximum optical density and stray-light level, and confirm that the 2p0(Sz=+1) line at high field lies in the linear range.
