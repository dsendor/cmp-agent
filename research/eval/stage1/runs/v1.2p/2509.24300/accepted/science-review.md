A. Must address

1. No measurement tests whether the carriers in the 100-220 K regime are Cooper pairs. The headline claims (abstract; Results p. 2; Conclusion) say that "signatures of Cooper pairs persist up to high temperatures of ~220 K" and that localized Cooper pairs and moving vortices exist at 220 K in sample 4 (183 K in sample 5). Every Bose glass assignment (Figs. 3, 5, 6) comes from the shape of zero-field R(T) and from the sign of beta' computed from the same curves. Neither is unique to bosons. Electron-electron interaction corrections, Kondo-like scattering and the parallel-channel model of concern 3 can all produce similar shapes. Bosonic weak localization has signatures that fermionic weak localization lacks: a positive magnetoresistance in the Bose glass window, with a field scale set by the superconducting flux quantum h/2e through the localization area, turning over at a pair-breaking field; negative magnetoresistance of Hikami-Larkin-Nagaoka form in the Fermi glass window; and a field-tuned transition with a crossing point and finite-size scaling. Weak localization at 220 K also needs a phase-coherence length larger than the relevant length scales, and this is not estimated. The authors should measure R(T, B) on samples 4 and 5 (and 6/7), with samples 1-3 as fermionic references, in perpendicular and parallel field from about 250 K down through the superconducting onset. They should state the bosonic and fermionic predictions for the sign and field scale of the magnetoresistance, show that it changes qualitatively at the claimed Fermi glass to Bose glass temperature (about 220 K for sample 4, 183 K for sample 5) but not in samples 1-3 at the same temperatures, report how the crossover temperature moves with field, and extract the phase-coherence length. Hall data for the carrier density would help. Where possible, a probe of the 2e charge (Little-Parks or h/2e oscillations in a patterned ring, shot noise), a Nernst signal or a tunnelling gap at 150-200 K would be direct evidence of pairing. Without at least the magnetoresistance, the claim should be reduced to a logarithmic-in-1/T resistance regime whose origin is one possibility among several.

2. The beta-function "vertical flow", presented in the Fig. 5 caption as "an independent criterion ... beyond simple logarithmic fitting", carries the same information as the ln T fits and can arise from the resistance maximum alone. Eqs. (2)-(4) show that the sign of beta' is the curvature of ln g against ln T, so the sign test in Fig. 6a restates what Fig. 3 already tests. More importantly, beta = -(2/p) d ln g / d ln T passes through zero at any extremum of R(T): near the extremum g barely changes while beta sweeps through a large range, which draws a near-vertical line in beta versus ln g whatever the transport mechanism. Samples 4 and 5 have such a maximum near 80-100 K (Figs. 1c, 3a, 3b), and the vertical segments in Fig. 5b, at ln g of about 0 to 1, extend to positive beta. Positive beta means dR/dT > 0, which is the superconducting-fluctuation side of the maximum, not the Bose glass window. Any R(T) that rises on cooling and then rolls over, for example because of an Aslamazov-Larkin or Maki-Thompson fluctuation conductance, a parallel metallic or superconducting minority channel shunting an insulating matrix, or temperature-dependent current redistribution in an inhomogeneous flake, must bend this way just above the maximum. The authors should state which temperature interval each vertical segment in Fig. 5b covers and mark the resistance maximum on it; show that the vertical flow begins near 220 K and persists when only data above the maximum are used; and compute beta for a model fermionic curve (for example G = G0 + G1 ln T plus an Aslamazov-Larkin term with T_c0 near 30-90 K, or a two-channel parallel resistor) and overlay it, to show whether the vertical flow requires bosons.

3. The measured flakes are not characterized, so parallel conduction through a second phase, and other mundane origins of the low resistivity and the R(T) shapes, are not excluded. The starting crystals contain about 5% CaRuO3 and about 6% S-Pbca Ca2RuO4 (Methods), and flakes are told apart from CaRuO3 only by crystal shape in SEM, which does not exclude intergrowths. CaRuO3 is a metal with resistivity of order 1e-4 to 1e-3 Ohm cm, which overlaps the resistivity of samples 4 and 5 (about 2e-3 Ohm cm, Fig. 1b); these flakes are 3-4 orders of magnitude less resistive than bulk Ca2RuO4. A CaRuO3 inclusion or surface layer, an interfacial layer on SiO2, oxygen excess (Ca2RuO4+delta is metallic), Ru-rich regions, or surface damage from sonication in dichloroethane and electron-beam lithography would lower rho and |Delta rho|. A percolating metallic minority in an insulating matrix, or a granular metal or granular superconductor (Efetov-type sigma ~ ln T, Josephson-coupled grains), gives log-like R(T), a broad resistance maximum, the curvature of concern 2, and broad or incomplete superconducting transitions such as those of samples 6/7. The authors should characterize the measured flakes, or flakes from the same batch, at device level: Raman spectroscopy (which distinguishes L and S Ca2RuO4 and CaRuO3 and is sensitive to octahedral distortion), TEM or electron diffraction including cross-sections to show layering and any second phase, and EDX or EELS for stoichiometry. They should also fit R(T) with a parallel-conduction model and discuss explicitly why a percolating mixed-phase picture cannot account for Figs. 1-3.

B. Should address

4. The data shown do not quantitatively separate R ~ ln(1/T) from G ~ ln T. In Fig. 3a (sample 4) the Bose glass window is ln T = 4.6-5.4 (101-220 K), a factor of about 2 in T, over which R changes by about 35% (R per layer roughly 60 to 120 kOhm across the plotted range). In Fig. 3b (sample 5) the window is 105-183 K, and R changes by only about 25% (about 32 to 40 kOhm). Over such ranges the two forms differ only at second order in the fractional change, so a straight line in R vs ln T and in G vs ln T are nearly indistinguishable. The text says the data "align well" with R ~ ln(1/T) "rather than" G ~ ln T, but no residuals, chi-squared or window sensitivity are given. The same applies to the Fermi glass windows (sample 4, 220-290 K; Fig. 2b, 181-299 K). The window edges marked by arrows and dashed lines in Figs. 2 and 3 appear to be chosen by eye, and they define the crossover temperatures then plotted in Fig. 4a. The authors should fit the 100-290 K data of samples 4 and 5 with both forms and with alternatives (a power law, an Altshuler-Aronov form, a Fermi glass term plus an Aslamazov-Larkin fluctuation term, a two-channel parallel resistor) over identical windows; report residuals and reduced chi-squared or information criteria; define the window edges objectively (for example where residuals exceed a stated threshold); show the preferred form is robust when the edges move; and quote uncertainties on the crossover temperatures in Fig. 4a.

5. The two "quantum critical resistances" are not shown to be critical points, and their values depend on unstated assumptions. A quantum critical sheet resistance is normally the T-independent crossing point of R(T) across samples or tuning-parameter values as T -> 0. Here R^{BG-FG} = 65.7 kOhm is tied to beta_c = -1.6 at about 150-220 K, which is a horizontal line separating two groups of samples in Fig. 6a rather than a point where beta' changes sign in one sample. It appears to come from reading the Vollhardt-Wölfle curve at beta_c (Fig. 5a gives ln g of about -0.9, g of about 0.39, about 66 kOhm) rather than from the measured resistance; the steps are not given. The measured per-layer resistances at the marked crossovers are about 80 kOhm for sample 4 (Fig. 3a, ln T about 5.4) and about 32 kOhm for sample 5 (Fig. 3b, ln T about 5.2), which differ by about 2.5 and bracket 65.7 kOhm. Sample 4 superconducts although its R per layer (about 125 kOhm at its maximum) lies far above both "critical" values, and sample 5 (28-40 kOhm) lies above 16.5 kOhm, contrary to the usual meaning of a critical resistance. Since beta carries a factor 2/p, taking p = 2 or 3 rescales beta_c by 2-3 and changes the inferred resistance. In Fig. 6b, the 65.7 and 16.5 kOhm lines mark where the highest-current curve levels off and roughly where curves meet at low T; no temperature-independent separatrix and no scaling collapse (with exponent zv) in current, thickness or Delta rho is shown. The values also scale directly with the per-layer normalization (concern 6). Finally, 65.7 kOhm differs from (5/2)h/e^2 = 64.5 kOhm, and the paper offers two unrelated explanations for the factor 2.5 (8/pi from ref. 42 and the 5/2 Pfaffian state). The authors should state exactly which sample, temperature and procedure give each value; propagate uncertainties in thickness, layer count, geometry and p; show whether different samples give the same value; and either show a crossing or collapse that defines a critical point or call these characteristic crossover resistances. The 5/2, 8/pi and Pfaffian comparisons should be removed or labelled as numerical coincidences unless the error bar is well below the differences between those numbers.

In the Bose glass window R rises on cooling, so Eq. (1) gives beta < 0. From Fig. 3a (sample 4), G per layer goes from about 0.8e-5 to 1.6e-5 S between ln T = 4.6 and 5.65, giving beta of about -1.3 to -1.4. 8. The central structural claim, that thinning relaxes RuO6 tilt, rotation and flattening and that this "rather than ... impurity-induced disorder" sets the ground state (abstract, Fig. 1a schematic, pp. 2 and 4-5, Conclusion), is inferred, not measured. The only lattice parameters for the exfoliated material (a = 5.350, b = 5.343, c = 12.278 Å) are powder data on the parent bulk crystals, matching bulk L phase; the MBE parameters are for strained films, and the text concedes that "X-ray diffraction measurements do not directly determine the octahedral geometry." 9. The bias-current data in Fig. 6b may be affected by Joule heating, and the panel is inconsistent with Fig. 3a. Ca2RuO4 is known for current-driven metallization and heating artifacts (refs. 28, 34, 35). Currents up to 5 microamperes through a 10 nm flake on SiO2 at kOhm to 100 kOhm resistances, in a 3He system, can raise the electron temperature substantially, and Fig. 6b shows strong current dependence from 1 nA to 5 microamperes up to at least 100 K, overlapping the claimed Bose glass window. The separatrix of a family of R(T) curves at different currents is not a quantum critical point. The low-current curves of sample 4 peak at about 4 kOhm on the right axis (2000-6000 Ohm) and about 40 kOhm per layer on the left, whereas Fig. 3a gives about 125 kOhm per layer for the same sample and Fig. 1b (rho about 7e-3 Ohm cm, 10 nm) agrees with Fig. 3a; it is unclear which quantity the 65.7 and 16.5 kOhm dashed lines refer to. The excitation current used for Figs. 1-3 is not stated. The authors should state the bias current for all R(T) data; show I-V curves at several temperatures in the 100-220 K window to confirm ohmic response; estimate the dissipated power and temperature rise at the largest currents and say whether the current-induced transition survives correction for heating; show that the 65.7 kOhm feature is not a heating-limited plateau; say whether Fig. 6b comes from a different cooldown, contact configuration or conversion and show reproducibility under thermal cycling; and use a single normalized axis harmonized with Fig. 3a.

10. The assumptions behind Eq. (1) and the 2D weak-localization forms are not justified for 100-300 K. Setting p = 1 (electron-electron dephasing in 2D) is assumed where electron-phonon dephasing (p = 2-3) usually dominates in oxides; since beta scales as 1/p, the absolute beta values in Figs. 5 and 6, beta_c and the inferred resistances all depend on this choice. The ln T and ln(1/T) forms assume 2D transport, but L_phi at 200 K is likely only a few nm, possibly smaller than the 10-35 nm thickness, and the per-layer treatment assumes decoupled RuO2 layers. The authors should estimate L_phi or the localization length in this range (for example from the magnetoresistance of concern 1), compare it with the thickness, and show how the BG/FG classification, Figs. 5 and 6 and beta_c change for p = 2 and 3.
