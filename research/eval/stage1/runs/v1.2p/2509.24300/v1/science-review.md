A. Must address

1. No measurement in the paper tests whether Cooper pairs exist between T_c^onset and about 220 K. The abstract, p. 5 and the Conclusion claim localized Cooper pairs and moving vortices up to about 220 K (sample 4) and 183 K (sample 5). The only support is the functional form of R(T), ρ ~ ln(1/T) rather than σ ~ ln T (Fig. 3a,b), and the sign of β′. Other mechanisms also give ρ ~ −ln T over a limited window. These include Kondo-like or magnetic scattering, which is plausible given the 180 K ferromagnetic transition reported in Ref. [20], as well as electron-electron interaction (Altshuler-Aronov) corrections and parallel conduction through secondary phases. The diamagnetism cited on p. 5 is not shown for these samples. It is taken from another sample set (Ref. [20]) with an onset near 140 K, which does not match the proposed 220 K and 183 K onsets. It is also argued alongside Ref. [27], which has been retracted, and Ref. [28], which attributes the 220 K bulk diamagnetism to Joule heating. The authors should measure at least one property specific to pairing and vortices on the same samples. The cheapest is perpendicular and parallel magnetoresistance across 100-300 K, compared between the 100-220 K and 220-300 K windows, on samples 4 and 5 and on an insulating flake such as sample 2. Fermionic weak localization gives negative magnetoresistance of Hikami-Larkin-Nagaoka form with a field scale set by the dephasing length. Localized pairs and phase fluctuations give positive magnetoresistance with a field scale tied to 2e and orbital pair breaking. Kondo or magnetic scattering gives isotropic negative magnetoresistance. Other useful tests are a Nernst signal, diamagnetic susceptibility of the same flakes with its temperature dependence, the field dependence of the "vertical flow" temperature, and I-V linearity in the putative Bose glass window. Without such evidence, the 220 K Cooper-pair statement should be withdrawn or reworded as a crossover in the temperature dependence of resistance that is consistent with, not evidence of, bosonic localization. The diamagnetism paragraph should either present nanoflake magnetization data with sample details or be softened.

2. The fits do not quantitatively distinguish ρ ~ ln(1/T) from σ ~ ln T over the windows used. The boson/fermion assignment for samples 4 and 5 rests on Fig. 3(a,b). The sample 4 window is 101-220 K (ln T 4.6-5.4, Δln T ≈ 0.8, a factor of about 2.2 in T), over which R changes by about 50%. The sample 5 window is 105-183 K (Δln T ≈ 0.55, a factor of about 1.7), over which R changes by about 25% and the data scatter visibly. The "Fermi glass" windows are narrower still: about 220-300 K (Δln T ≈ 0.3) for samples 4 and 5, and about 210-300 K (a factor of about 1.35-1.4) for samples 1-3 (Fig. 2b). For such small fractional changes, R linear in ln(1/T) and G linear in ln T differ only by weak, second-order curvature, and almost any smooth monotonic function looks linear in either variable. The lower panel of Fig. 3(a) shows that G versus ln T for sample 4 is also close to linear over much of the window. The paper gives no goodness of fit, residuals, fit uncertainties or objective criterion for the window boundaries, which appear to be chosen by eye. The sample 4 window ends at 101 K, next to the resistance maximum at about 80-100 K, so the preference for ρ ~ ln(1/T) may simply reflect the start of the downturn. The authors should report residuals or reduced χ² for R ~ ln(1/T), G ~ ln T, activated, VRH and power-law forms over the same windows, with and without a phonon or background term. They should show how the windows were chosen and how sensitive the conclusion is to moving their ends, and show that the preferred form holds over a wider range or is robust to the window choice.

3. The "vertical flow" and the sign of β′ follow from the resistance maximum and do not independently identify bosons. By Eqs. (1)-(2), β_EXP = −(2/p) d ln g/d ln T is computed from the same R(T) curves, and β′ < 0 means only that |β| shrinks as T falls, i.e. the rise in resistance slows on a ln T scale. For samples 4 and 5, R(T) has a maximum near 80-110 K (Fig. 1c), where d ln g/d ln T passes through zero and changes sign. Approaching it, β moves vertically at nearly fixed ln g and ln|β| diverges, which produces the upturn in Figs. 5(b) and 6(a) whatever the underlying physics. A fermionic description, with a weak-localization/electron-electron-interaction background plus Aslamazov-Larkin and Maki-Thompson fluctuation conductivity above a transition near 80-100 K, gives the same curvature and the same sign of β′, and so does a crossover between two conduction channels. Eqs. (3)-(4) give opposite signs of β′ only if the data follow one of the two pure logarithmic forms exactly, which assumes the conclusion. The MBE samples 6 and 7 never superconduct yet are also assigned β′ < 0. In Fig. 6(a) their β hardly varies with T, their arrow in Fig. 5(b) points left rather than up, and a slowing resistance rise in them could equally come from saturation or percolation. The authors should fit R(T) of samples 4 and 5 with a fermionic WL/EEI background plus 2D fluctuation conductivity, stating the fitted T_c and background, and show quantitatively that it fails where the bosonic form succeeds, or that β′ < 0 survives after subtracting it. They should show that the vertical flow begins well above the resistance maximum, and that it is absent in a control sample whose resistance maximum has a non-superconducting origin (for example a magnetic or CaRuO3-type anomaly). They should also explain separately why samples 6 and 7 are assigned β′ < 0.

4. The phase and structure of the measured flakes are not established, so the conclusion that thinning relieves RuO6 distortion rather than adding disorder or secondary phases is unsupported. Per the Methods, Ca2RuO4 and CaRuO3 were told apart only "by observing the crystal shape using a scanning electron microscope". The c-axis value of 12.278 Å attributed to "nanoscale single crystals" appears to come from powder XRD (Rigaku Miniflex600) on the parent material, not from the measured flakes. CaRuO3 is a metal with resistivity of order 10⁻⁴-10⁻³ Ω cm, the same range as the flake resistivities in Fig. 1(a,b) and 3-4 orders below bulk Ca2RuO4. Other Ruddlesden-Popper intergrowths (e.g. Ca3Ru2O7), the metallic L phase, Ru metal or RuO2 inclusions, Ca-deficient surface layers from sonication and oxygen non-stoichiometry (Ca2RuO4+δ) could each lower the resistivity by orders of magnitude without any change in correlation or distortion. Ru inclusions could also give superconducting-like, filamentary drops. The statements that "the distortion in RuO6 octahedra is eliminated" (p. 6), that impurity-induced disorder "cannot explain this phenomenon" (p. 6) and that "thinning does not introduce disorder" (Conclusion, abstract) are therefore assertions, not results. The authors should characterize the measured devices or sister flakes from the same batch: Raman (which separates the S and L phases of Ca2RuO4 from CaRuO3), TEM or electron diffraction giving in-plane and c-axis lattice parameters and the octahedral tilt and rotation, and EDX/EDS or EELS stoichiometry. They should compare the flake resistivities with CaRuO3 reference values, and ideally measure the structure as a function of temperature.

6. The two "quantum critical" resistances are not derived from a critical-point analysis, and the step from β_c to 65.7 kΩ is not given. In Fig. 6(a), β_c = −1.6 is a horizontal line drawn between groups of different samples at 100-250 K. It is not a crossing point or a temperature-independent separatrix, and samples 6 and 7 lie near −1 to −1.5 at low T, overlapping it. The text says the resistance is "estimated from g_c = −1.6", conflating β_c and g_c, but g is a positive dimensionless conductance and no conversion from β_c to a resistance is stated. With g = (ħ/e²)σ, 65.7 kΩ corresponds to g ≈ 0.063 and ln g ≈ −2.8. In Fig. 6(b), 65.7 kΩ is the low-temperature value of the 5000 nA curve and 16.5 kΩ sits near the 150 nA curves. Bias current is not a thermodynamic tuning parameter, it is not shown to produce a separatrix, and the curves at the quoted values are not flat in T. A quantum critical point needs a continuous tuning parameter, a temperature-independent R at R_c and a collapse of R(T, x) onto F(|x − x_c|/T^{1/zν}). Without these, the "two quantum critical points" of the abstract and p. 8 are too strong a claim. The authors should state the explicit relation from β_c to R_c. They should show that some sample or tuning parameter actually sits at β_c with dβ/dT ≈ 0, or derive β_c from a crossing or scaling analysis. They should show a separatrix and scaling collapse in R(T) against a continuous tuning parameter (thickness series, gate or magnetic field at fixed thickness) with the exponent product zν, and quote uncertainties on R_c. Otherwise the critical-point language should be dropped.

10. The weak-localization framework is applied far outside its regime of validity, and its applicability at 100-300 K is not justified. Samples 4 and 5 have R□/layer of 30-125 kΩ, i.e. g = (ħ/e²)/R of about 0.03-0.15 per layer. This is well outside the weak-localization regime (g ≫ 1, k_F ℓ ≫ 1) assumed by Das-Doniach and by the −1/g branch of β_VW. Quantum interference corrections between 100 and 300 K in an oxide need a phase-coherence length longer than the mean free path. At these temperatures the dephasing length is expected to be comparable to microscopic lengths, and phonon scattering dominates. The authors should estimate k_F ℓ, ℓ and the dephasing length ℓ_φ for each sample and explain why weak-localization physics should apply at these temperatures in this material.

11. The universal collapse in Fig. 5(a) and the choice p = 1 need a stated method, and part of the agreement is built in. β_EXP scales as 2/p, and p is fixed at 1 (p. 7) without justification, tying the cutoff length to temperature through L² = DT^{-p}. For any VRH or activated law ln g ≈ c − (T0/T)^x, β ≈ (2x/p)(ln g − c), a straight line whose slope is set by the hopping exponent (2/3 for 2D VRH) and whose position depends on c. Collapse onto β_VW in the strongly localized region is therefore nearly automatic. g is defined as (ħ/e²)σ, which is not dimensionless for σ in S/cm. Bulk 3D crystal data (Nakatsuji 1 and 2, in Ω cm) and 25-300 nm films are placed on a curve whose weak-localization branch is two-dimensional. The ln g values plotted in Fig. 5(b) for sample 4 (about 0) and sample 5 (about −1 and 0) do not match ln g ≈ −3.2 and −2.2 implied by Fig. 3. The authors should define g precisely for each data set (sheet conductance per layer or per film, and the normalization), state the parameters of β_VW and any fitted constants, show how the collapse, the vertical flow and β_c change with p (e.g. p = 1, 2, 3), explain why bulk 3D data belong on the same curve, and state what agreement or disagreement would have meant.

18. The Bose glass terminology departs from standard usage, and finite-temperature crossovers are presented as quantum phases. In the Fisher framework the Bose glass is an insulating, compressible T = 0 phase with zero conductivity. The abstract's "absence of global phase coherence yet finite conductivity" and "bosonic order" follow the Das-Doniach usage of weakly localized bosons, and a finite-conductivity bosonic state is usually called a Bose or anomalous metal. "Mott insulator" in boson SIT language also differs from the fermionic Mott insulator of Ca2RuO4. A phase entered at 220 K and left at about 100 K on cooling is a finite-temperature crossover, not a ground state, yet the boundaries are called quantum critical points. The authors should state which definitions they use, what experimental property distinguishes the phases, and separate finite-temperature crossovers from ground-state claims in Fig. 4(a).
