1. Significance and novelty

The authors use element-resolved, time-resolved STXM (Fe and Co L3 edges, about 1 ns and 20 nm resolution) to image a dense array of skyrmions in a compensated Pt/FeCoB/Ir/Pt/CoB/Ir synthetic antiferromagnet driven by bipolar SOT pulses. They report two regimes. At low current (2.8×10^11 A/m²) some skyrmions stay pinned while others move, and the mobile ones recoil after the pulse with 3–20 ns time constants. At high current (up to 1.2×10^12 A/m²) the whole array translates rigidly, with a skyrmion Hall angle of (1.4 ± 1.5)° and no inertia detectable within the time resolution. The most novel claim is a quantitative skyrmion–skyrmion interaction potential: an exponential with a range of about 30–38 nm, obtained by inverting the Thiele equation on the post-pulse recoil trajectories and said to agree with micromagnetics. Time-resolved imaging of a dense SyAFM skyrmion ensemble is new and of broad interest. The interaction-potential result, however, rests on modelling assumptions the paper does not yet test.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- Technically demanding experiment. Pump–probe STXM of collective AFM skyrmion dynamics has not been reported before, and imaging both sublattices adds value.
- The high-current data (Figs. 4, 5) give a direct, time-resolved confirmation that the SkHE is suppressed (θ_SkHA consistent with zero) over a range of current densities and both polarities, and show that the array moves in step with the drive within 1 ns.
- The idea of reading an interaction law from reproducible, deterministic post-pulse relaxation is new and could be useful. It complements Boltzmann-inversion approaches based on thermal equilibrium (Ref. 28).
- The low-current data show a clear, reproducible, class-dependent recoil (Fig. 2c,d; Table 1), which is interesting in itself.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The inverse Thiele analysis ignores pinning forces, although the paper's own data show that pinning restoring forces are about as large as the recoil being fitted (Eq. 1, Fig. 3, Methods, pp. 9–11, 18).
   - What the paper shows. The "immobile" class I skyrmions are displaced by about 20 nm during the pulse and recoil with the largest amplitudes in the paper (17.5 and 20.1 nm, Table 1; Fig. 2c caption: "I-skyrmions showing the strongest recoil"). The text itself says the restoring force arises "from skyrmion–skyrmion repulsion and the surrounding pinning landscape" (p. 9).
   - The problem. The model fitted to the L and R trajectories contains only dissipation and a pairwise exponential repulsion from measured neighbour positions (Methods, p. 18). Any elastic restoring force from a local pinning well acting on L/R skyrmions themselves is therefore absorbed into a*, b*. The result is then a lumped effective potential, not the intrinsic skyrmion–skyrmion interaction claimed in the title and abstract.
   - A second problem. The paper argues (p. 9) that R-type skyrmions such as R1 undergo "a breakdown of the rigid-particle approximation" and deform strongly. Yet R1 is the showcase trajectory fitted with a rigid-particle Thiele model (Fig. 3a). The tracked core position of a deforming skyrmion also relaxes through shape (breathing/elongation) relaxation, which a centre-to-centre force cannot represent.
   - What to do:
     - Include a harmonic (or otherwise parametrised) pinning term for each fitted skyrmion, with its stiffness constrained by the class I recoil, and show whether a*, b* survive.
     - Show that the fitted parameters are the same when the fit is restricted to weakly deformed skyrmions, compared with deformed ones.
     - Report the number of skyrmions and trajectories used, the minimum loss, and the residuals.
     - Show the a–b correlation in the loss landscape.
     - Show that the exponential form is preferred over alternatives such as a K1-type decay or a power law.
   - Note also that the predicted curves in Fig. 3a are offset from the data by roughly 10–20 nm in both x and y, even at t = 0, although the Methods state that starting positions are taken from the data. This should be explained, because it bears directly on the quality of the fit.

2. Pump–probe averaging could produce smooth exponential recoil from stochastic motion, and the paper does not show that the trajectories are deterministic (pp. 4, 6, 11, Table 1).
   - The Thiele inversion assumes each frame shows a deterministic trajectory. In pump–probe STXM over about 10^9 cycles, a skyrmion that hops stochastically between two pinning sites, or depins at a random delay after the pulse, would give a time-averaged contrast whose centroid relaxes smoothly and roughly exponentially. That would yield a τ_relax and an "interaction potential" with no direct mechanical meaning.
   - The paper asserts that "the dynamics are highly reproducible across all cycles, confirming their non-stochastic origin" (p. 6), but gives no evidence that would distinguish the two cases. This matters especially in the low-pinning SyAFM regime, where the authors' own earlier work (Ref. 20) reports enhanced thermal diffusion, and with 1 µs between pulses.
   - What to do. Show the XMCD contrast amplitude and the apparent skyrmion size or edge width of L, R and M skyrmions against delay time, compared with the static image. Stochastic motion would appear as reduced peak contrast, smeared edges or double ("ghost") positions during the recoil window. Showing that the post-pulse static state matches the pre-pulse state at full contrast, and that I skyrmions are not blurred, would support the deterministic picture. Without this, the quantitative potential and the "deterministic control over billions of cycles" claim are not established.

B. Should address

3. A thermal explanation for the 3–20 ns relaxation has not been excluded (pp. 6, 9, Methods p. 17).
   - A 5 ns pulse at 2.8×10^11 A/m² through a roughly 115 nm metallic stack on a 100 nm SiN membrane can raise the temperature significantly. Membrane cooling times are typically nanoseconds to tens of nanoseconds, overlapping the reported τ_relax. A transient change in anisotropy, DMI or interlayer exchange during cooling would change skyrmion size and equilibrium spacing and cause a post-pulse relaxation of centroids, independent of collisions.
   - What to do:
     - Estimate the temperature rise, for example from the time-resolved device resistance during the pulse or from a thermal simulation.
     - State the temperature during the 1.2×10^12 A/m² runs.
     - Show whether skyrmion diameters change during and after the pulse.
     - Explain why τ_relax for the same class differs by up to a factor of 2.5 between polarities (L: 9.6 vs 3.9 ns; R: 7.4 vs 18.9 ns, Table 1). A purely repulsive, conservative recoil does not obviously predict this.

4. The comparison with micromagnetics is less independent than presented, and the experimental and simulated values do not quite match the wording (pp. 10–11, 18, abstract).
   - The experimental best fit is a* = (38 ± 5) nm, b* = (236 ± 9) nm (Methods). The simulation gives a* = (28 ± 4) nm. The abstract quotes "a range of 30 nm, in full agreement with micromagnetic simulations". The two a* values differ by about 1.6 combined σ. That is acceptable, but "full agreement" and the quoted 30 nm should be stated more carefully, and the abstract should give the experimental value.
   - The simulation parameters (A_ex, D_DMI, K_u, interlayer exchange, α, layer count, grain or pinning model) are not given in the main text. It is unclear whether they were measured independently or tuned. It is also unclear whether the simulated a*, b* came from the same inverse method applied to simulated trajectories, which would test only self-consistency.
   - What to do:
     - Give the parameters and their provenance.
     - Add a direct micromagnetic calculation of the static two-skyrmion energy against separation, as an independent check that the inverse method recovers the true potential.
     - Explain how the dissipation constant D used for the meV axis in Fig. 3b was obtained (α, the skyrmion profile, and its uncertainty), since the absolute energy scale depends entirely on it.

5. Sublattice selectivity at the Co edge, and the claim of no delay between sublattices (pp. 4, 6–7, 13).
   - Sublattice A contains FeCoB/CoFeB (Fig. 1a), so the Co L3 signal includes an opposite-sign contribution from sublattice A in addition to the CoB sublattice B. The Co-edge images are therefore not purely sublattice B.
   - The two sublattices were also imaged in separate sequential runs (p. 6). A claim of "no delay or phase shift between the sublattices" (p. 13) needs a common time reference across runs and a figure.
   - What to do:
     - Quantify the fraction of the Co-edge XMCD that comes from sublattice A.
     - Show the high-current sublattice B data (Fig. 4 shows sublattice A only), overlaid on the A trajectories with timing uncertainty.
     - State the upper bound on any interlayer lag this places, compared with the predictions of Ref. 43.

6. "Absence of inertia" and "GHz-class operation" are stronger than the data support (pp. 13, 15, abstract).
   - With 1 ns time resolution and 2.5 ns pulses, Fig. 4d can only exclude inertial lags well above a few hundred picoseconds.
   - The experiment runs at about 1 MHz repetition, with 1 µs gaps between pulses.
   - What to do. Turn the result into a quantitative upper bound on the effective mass or lag time, for example by fitting v(t) to the drive convolved with the instrument response with a free delay, and moderate the GHz claim accordingly.

7. Number of devices and regions, and robustness of the classification (pp. 4–7).
   - The paper does not say how many strips or fields of view were measured, or whether the low-current recoil and the extracted potential are reproduced in a second region, device or cooldown. Because the method relies on the specific local pinning configuration, showing that a*, b* are the same for a different pinning arrangement is the most direct evidence that they describe intrinsic interactions.
   - The I/M/L/R classification from a single displacement map (Fig. 2a) should be given with its thresholds.
   - Error bars or spreads should be shown on Fig. 2c,d and Table 1.

8. Comparison with the literature on velocities (Figs. 4, 5).
   - The velocities measured here are at most about 50 m/s at 1.2×10^12 A/m², more than an order of magnitude below the SyAFM velocities cited in the introduction (Ref. 21, approaching 1 km/s).
   - What to do:
     - Discuss the origin of the difference (stack, dense array compared with isolated skyrmions, residual pinning, current-shunting estimate).
     - State how J is computed, including the current-carrying thickness and shunting through Ta, Ir and Pt.
     - Define the "dynamic velocity" in Fig. 5 (peak or pulse-averaged).
   - The "viscous flow" interpretation also needs the v(J) intercept and threshold. The 2.8×10^11 A/m² point is missing from Fig. 5.

9. External field and degree of compensation during dynamics (Fig. 1b,c; p. 4).
   - The static images were taken at B_z = −250 mT. The field applied during the time-resolved measurements is not stated.
   - A finite field in a SyAFM induces a net moment and breaks compensation, which bears on the SkHE argument and on the "fully compensated" assumption behind removing the gyrotropic term from the Thiele analysis.
   - What to do. State the field used and, if it is non-zero, estimate the residual net topological or angular-momentum imbalance.

10. "Lattice" characterisation (Figs. 1b,c, 2b).
    - The images show a dense but visibly disordered array with elongated objects, and the Delaunay triangulation is irregular. Claiming the "first AFM skyrmion lattice" requires a quantitative measure of order, such as the coordination-number distribution, the bond-orientational order ψ6, or an FFT of the static image.
    - Otherwise, "dense skyrmion ensemble" would be more accurate.

C. Minor

11. Labelling errors in Fig. 1 and in which class serves as boundary condition.
    - The Fig. 1 caption refers to panel (f), but the device and pulse schematic is panel (e).
    - On p. 11 and in the Methods (p. 18), the pinned boundary skyrmions are called "pinned M skyrmions", whereas M denotes the mobile class and I the immobile class. Please correct this and make clear which skyrmions supply the boundary conditions.

12. On p. 14 the SkHE angle is said to be "obtained from the slope of the linear fit to v_y versus v_x", but Fig. 5 shows separate fits against J_x. Please clarify. Also correct the typographical error in F_ext = −(ħ/4pie) jθ_SH B, and define B consistently with Ref. 39 and J with j.

13. Fig. 3b: give the units and the meaning of the left axis (nm·m·s⁻¹) more transparently, and explain why the histogram extends to 400 nm, where the potential is essentially zero and does not constrain the fit. The statement that the potential is probed "from 25% compression to 25% expansion" should give the reference spacing.

14. Table 1 should include fit uncertainties, and the exponential fits should be shown on Fig. 2c,d.

15. Typographical and language issues: "ensembles" ("enembles", p. 2), "scattering" ("scattring", p. 3), "dynamics and" ("dynamicsand", p. 3), "four clusters", "an incoherent", "an radially", "inverse analysis" ("inverse analyis" in the abstract).

16. Code and data are available only "on reasonable request". Given that the central result is a numerical inversion, depositing the tracked trajectories and the inversion code would greatly help reproducibility.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Add a pinning term to the Thiele inversion. Benchmark the method on micromagnetic simulations that include a known pinning landscape and a known pair potential, to show that the inversion separates the two.
- Use contrast-amplitude and edge-width analysis of the time-resolved frames to show that the recoil is deterministic rather than an average over stochastic events.
- Repeat the low-current measurement in a second field of view or device. Report a*, b* for each, and for each skyrmion separately, to show that the potential is intrinsic.
- Measure or simulate the transient temperature, and track skyrmion size against time, to separate thermal relaxation from interaction-driven recoil.

5. Verdict: major revision
