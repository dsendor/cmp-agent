Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper proposes "ultrafast X-ray sonography": a laser-launched bipolar strain pulse acts as a depth marker for two coexisting structural phases, read out through their separate Bragg peaks in time-resolved hard-X-ray diffraction. Applied to the laser-driven AFM-to-FM transition in a 44 nm FeRh(001) film at EuXFEL, the authors conclude that the FM phase nucleates in the near-surface region as columnar domains about 30 nm across, which then merge into a continuous layer (scenario III). Getting depth- and in-plane-resolved information on a picosecond phase transition from a spatially averaging probe would interest a broad audience. The novelty, though, is mainly in how established picosecond ultrasonics and UXRD modelling are combined and interpreted, so the main claims depend on how firmly the modelling can tell the competing scenarios apart.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- High-quality XFEL data: the AFM and FM (002) Bragg peaks are resolved together, with clear phase-specific strain oscillations (Fig. 2b,c; Fig. 3a; Extended Data Fig. E2).
- The idea that each phase's strain response encodes where that phase sits is simple and general, and Fig. 1 explains it clearly.
- The strain model uses thermophysical parameters calibrated earlier on the same sample below the transition threshold (Refs. 18, 26), which limits the number of free parameters.
- The measurement covers four fluences, and the fluence trend (scenario IV best at 3.9 mJ cm^-2, scenario III at 5.2 mJ cm^-2 and above) is physically plausible given the depth-dependent absorption.
- The qualitative exclusion of pure in-plane heterogeneity (scenario II) and of back-side nucleation (scenario V) is convincing from Fig. 3 alone.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. Whether scenario III is statistically and systematically distinguishable from scenarios I and IV is not shown. The headline conclusion (surface nucleation as columns that merge into a continuous layer, rather than a continuous layer growing from the top or columns that never merge) rests on the global residual chi^2 in Fig. 4d and Extended Data Fig. E3. Some specific problems:
   (a) The chi^2 is an unweighted sum of squared intensity differences, with no measurement uncertainties and no normalisation by degrees of freedom. A difference such as about 0.058 against 0.065 for scenarios III and IV at 7.7 mJ cm^-2 (Fig. E3c), or the near-degeneracy of I, III and IV at 3.9 mJ cm^-2 (Fig. E3a), therefore cannot be judged against noise.
   (b) Systematic model deficits look at least as large as the differences between scenarios. The FM peak broadening in Fig. E1c is not reproduced by any scenario, the FM peak position in Fig. E1b deviates by up to about 0.01 Å^-1 at 3-8 ps, and the authors note that the bottom 5.5 nm of FeRh had to be removed ad hoc.
   (c) Several inputs are held fixed or tuned and could shift the ranking. These are the optical penetration depth (optimised), the nucleation time tau = 8 ps, the 0.6 % transformation expansion, the 5.5 nm dead layer, and the neglect of latent heat and of phase-dependent heat capacity and electron-phonon coupling (Methods, p. 10).

   The authors should give noise-weighted residuals (for example, using the shot-to-shot or pulse-train scatter of the normalised intensities) and an estimate of the significance of the differences between scenarios. They should also show that scenario III stays preferred when each of the parameters in (c) is varied within a plausible range. Without this, the claim that sonography "identifies" the nucleation scenario is not established.

2. The scenario space is discrete and restrictive, and the promised quantitative extraction of D_FM(t) and A_FM(t) is not delivered. On p. 5 the paper says the method can "quantify the transient relative thickness D_FM(t) and in-plane coverage A_FM(t)". In practice the Methods fix the time dependence of each scenario a priori. In III and V, D_FM is constant at V*_FM from t = 0, so the columns appear at their final depth immediately and never grow into the depth. Scenario IV uses one arbitrary choice, D_FM = 1.3 V*_FM. Fig. 4b varies D_FM only at the single delay t = 9 ps.

   Several physically plausible alternatives are not tested: a continuous surface layer whose thickness grows while the in-plane coverage is still incomplete; columns that lengthen with time; and a depth-graded coverage that follows the exponential absorption profile. Any of these could fit as well as III. The authors should either fit D_FM(t) and A_FM(t) delay by delay with Eq. (2) as the constraint (giving trajectories with uncertainties), or at least include a continuous family between I, III and IV and show that the "columns then merge" picture is selected by the data and not by the form of the parametrisation. The minimum of the 9 ps scan in Fig. 4c should also be stated with its width, in D_FM.

3. The ~30 nm lateral domain size (abstract, p. 8, Methods) is under-supported, and it coincides suspiciously with the crystallite size. The estimate comes from attributing the excess FM-peak broadening (0.06 Å^-1, Fig. E1c) entirely to Poisson-coupled in-plane expansion in a five-site toy model (Fig. E4). The quoted ±10 nm is an estimate rather than a derived uncertainty, and the authors acknowledge both the uncertain origin of the broadening and the limits of the model.

   Other sources of the excess width are not ruled out: inhomogeneous strain between mosaic crystallites (average diameter about 25 nm, Ref. 32) that transform at different times; a depth gradient of strain within the thin FM region; and a spread of transition temperatures (Refs. 7, 42), which the authors themselves invoke at low fluence. The near-equality of the 30 nm domain size and the 25 nm crystallite size suggests that the measurement may simply return the crystallite size.

   The authors should do three things. First, show how the predicted broadening scales with l_FM (for example 10, 20, 30, 50 and 100 nm) on the same plot as the data. Second, show that the broadening decays on the nucleation timescale, as claimed, and not on a thermal timescale, using all four fluences. Third, either test the alternatives quantitatively or tone the claim down to an order-of-magnitude, model-dependent estimate in the abstract.

B. Should address

4. Fig. 4d and Extended Data Fig. E3c (both 7.7 mJ cm^-2) do not agree. In Fig. 4d the minima of the global residual lie at about 0.09-0.13, and the experimental V*_FM is marked at 0.46, as in the text (p. 5). In Fig. E3c the minima lie at about 0.058-0.08, and the dashed line sits near V*_FM ≈ 0.43. The authors should explain whether the two figures use different data subsets, normalisations or delay ranges, and make the quoted V*_FM consistent.

5. The sample may have changed during the experiment, and heat may accumulate within the pulse trains. The Methods attribute the missing contribution of the bottom 5.5 nm to "interface diffusion of FeRh and W ... due to too strong laser excitation at high-repetition rate", which implies the sample changed during the beamtime. Separately, the pump runs at 56.25 kHz within 10 Hz trains, and the starting temperature (300 K) is only about 70 K below the transition.

   The authors should state when the degradation happened relative to the four fluence datasets, and whether static (002) rocking curves or reflectivity before and after show it. They should also show the unpumped reference peaks along the train to confirm there is no cumulative heating or residual FM fraction. A residual FM fraction would bias V*_FM and the scenario fits, and the low-fluence conclusion (scenario IV, a partial coverage of 0.66) is especially sensitive to the initial state.

6. The low-fluence conclusion and the thermal interpretation are weakly supported. At 3.9 mJ cm^-2 (Fig. E3a) the curves for I, III and IV are nearly degenerate. Scenario I has its minimum near V*_FM ≈ 0.16, slightly above the measured value, but at a residual close to the others. The statement that D_FM "can be approximated by the depth until which the optical excitation overcomes the equilibrium transition threshold" (p. 7) is not shown quantitatively. The authors should plot the fitted D_FM against the depth at which the modelled deposited energy density crosses the threshold, for all four fluences.

7. The paper has no independent test of the sonography inversion on a structure where the answer is known. The parameters were calibrated below threshold, but no test shows that the full analysis chain (strain model, then dynamical diffraction, then chi^2 ranking) recovers a known phase distribution. Possible tests are synthetic data generated from one scenario with realistic noise and refitted with all five scenarios, or a bilayer of two lattice-mismatched materials with known thicknesses. Such a test would show how far the method can be trusted and what its depth resolution is. This matters because the paper presents sonography as a general, "sample-agnostic" technique.

8. The FM strain reference and the extraction of the peak parameters need more detail. The FM strain in Fig. 2c is computed relative to "the expected equilibrium value", which is not given; nor is the "certain threshold" on peak integral used to show it. Eq. (1) assumes one sound velocity for both phases, but the FM and AFM elastic constants differ, so the authors should state the value used and estimate the effect on d_phase. The data in Figs. 2c and E1 carry no error bars, so the reader cannot judge the 0.25 % strain oscillations or the 3-8 ps feature around which the scenarios differ most.

9. The claim to reconcile the literature (abstract and Introduction) is stated rather than shown. The paper should state explicitly how surface columnar nucleation reconciles the fast structural rise (Ref. 17), the slower rise of the magnetisation (Ref. 25), and the depth or nanostructure dependences in Refs. 18 and 26. It should also compare the 30 nm size with the domain sizes known from static imaging of FeRh films (for example Refs. 6, 42). At present this is a single sentence on p. 7.

C. Minor

10. Fig. 3: the scenario sketches between panels are small and hard to match to the text. In scenario IV, the caption's "1.3 V*_FM" choice should be justified (why 1.3?).

11. Fig. 4b: label which horizontal line corresponds to which scenario, and give the D_FM values of these lines in the caption.

12. Fig. E2 caption: "Within increasing fluence" should read "With increasing fluence". The caption also says all four fluences are above threshold, so the reader needs the threshold fluence.

13. Fig. E3 caption: "ferromagentic" should read "ferromagnetic". The statement that scenario V's deviation is "even larger" than scenario II's should be backed by its values, at least in the text.

14. Methods: give the X-ray footprint against the pump spot (136 × 142 µm pump; 50 µm X-ray at 20.9° incidence gives an elongated footprint), so that the homogeneity of the probed excitation can be checked. Also give the fluence uncertainty.

15. Data and code are "available upon request". Depositing the reduced sonograms and the udkm1Dsim input files would let others check the scenario ranking, given that the conclusions rest on the modelling.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Replace the discrete five-scenario comparison with a constrained fit of D_FM(t) and A_FM(t) at each delay, with noise-weighted residuals and confidence regions. This would turn the qualitative scenario choice into the quantitative "sonogram" the title promises.
2. Add a robustness table: the ranking of scenarios I, III and IV under variation of the penetration depth, tau, the transformation strain, the dead-layer thickness and the latent heat.
3. Validate the inversion on synthetic data and, ideally, on a reference bilayer. Report the depth resolution the method achieves in this geometry.
4. Use the proposed double-pulse scheme, or a dedicated transducer layer, at least in a test measurement. Separating the strain marker from the driver of the transition would remove the coupling between heating, phase nucleation and the strain probe that complicates the present analysis.

5. Verdict

Verdict: major revision
