Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports two-terminal, field-free, bipolar current-induced switching in nanoscale PtMn3 (7 nm)/MgO (2 nm)/PtMn3 (10 nm) all-antiferromagnetic tunnel junctions (AFMTJs) on oxidized Si. It attributes the switching to an "octupole-driven spin-transfer torque" (OTT), with a resistance change of up to 363% at room temperature and J of about 10 MA/cm². A DFT conduction-channel analysis and a three-sublattice LLG model support the attribution. If the switching is magnetic and driven by a spin torque from one noncollinear antiferromagnet onto another, this would be the antiferromagnetic counterpart of STT in MTJs, a significant result for antiferromagnetic spintronics. The resistance change would also be far larger than earlier AFMTJ reports. As submitted, however, the evidence that the resistance switching is magnetic, and that it is driven by a spin torque, is indirect.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The question is important and timely: electrical writing of an AFMTJ by current through the junction, without a heavy-metal SOT layer or a field, is a sought-after milestone.
- The stacks are grown on 150 mm wafers in an industrial PVD tool and patterned to 50 to 200 nm, which makes the result relevant to devices.
- Switching is reported in many devices (36 in Fig. 2b) and at four diameters (Extended Data Fig. 1).
- The switching and the resistance contrast disappear above about 360 K (Fig. 2c). This matches the known noncollinear-to-collinear transition of Mn3Pt and is a useful first hint of a magnetic origin.
- The sublattice-resolved picture (intra- versus inter-sublattice spin currents, β1 > β2) is intuitive and gives a simple symmetry argument for why a spin-neutral current can carry a staggered torque. The equivalence with the octupole-polarization picture (Extended Data Fig. 6) is a nice conceptual link.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. It is not shown that the two resistance states are magnetic (octupole parallel and antiparallel) rather than bipolar resistive switching of the barrier. Every switching loop in the paper (Fig. 2a, 2c, Extended Data Fig. 1) is current-written and current-read. No resistance-versus-field loop, and no state written any other way, shows that the high and low states correspond to the octupole configurations that give TMR.

Several features of the data are also typical of bipolar resistive (memristive) switching in ultrathin oxide barriers:
- The bias is very large. In the 100 nm device of Fig. 2a, R_AP ≈ 2.4 kΩ and the AP→P switch occurs at about −0.85 mA, so about 2 V falls across a 2 nm MgO layer (about 1 V/nm) for 1 ms, near or above typical MgO breakdown fields.
- The MgO is described as rough and not well crystallized (STEM, p. 5–6), which favours vacancy migration and filament formation.
- Several loops in Extended Data Fig. 1 (for example 75 nm right, 100 nm first and third panels, 200 nm left) show gradual, ramp-like resistance changes before or after the jump.
- The switching polarity is not consistent between devices (see concern 2).
- The 363% contrast is several times larger than earlier AFMTJ reports with epitaxial barriers, yet these junctions have an amorphous barrier and polycrystalline electrodes.

The disappearance at 360 K is helpful but not decisive, because it could also reflect a change in the electrodes or barrier conduction. The authors should, at minimum:
(i) show that the same high and low resistance levels can be set by a non-current method (a large in-plane or out-of-plane field sweep, field cooling through the transition, or SOT writing through the Pt underlayer as in their ref. 13), and that current-written states respond to field as expected;
(ii) measure control junctions under identical pulse conditions: Pt/MgO/Pt, and/or a junction with a collinear or non-ordered Mn-Pt electrode, at the same RA and diameter, to show that no bipolar switching appears;
(iii) report I–V characteristics and the read bias, and show that the read resistances of both states are independent of read current and stable over time (retention);
(iv) show that switching survives thermal cycling through 360 K (switching recovered after cooling, with the same resistance levels).

2. The switching polarity and the sign convention are inconsistent across the figures. A deterministic spin torque requires a fixed current-polarity relationship, and the paper does not establish one. In Fig. 2a, positive current drives low→high (P→AP). In Fig. 2c, positive current drives high→low. In Fig. 2d, AP→P is plotted at positive J and P→AP at negative J. Extended Data Fig. 1 contains devices of both polarities (for example 50 nm left versus 75 nm left).

The two PtMn3 layers also have no pinning, and they differ only in thickness (7 versus 10 nm), so the paper never states which layer is the "free" layer or why the reference layer does not also switch. For an STT mechanism, the polarity should be fixed by the current direction relative to the reference layer, and should be the same in all devices of the stack. Random polarity is more naturally explained by forming-dependent resistive switching or by thermal processes. The authors should:
- state the current-sign convention (top to bottom or the reverse);
- report for all 36 devices which polarity was observed;
- explain how the free and reference layers are defined (for example, from thickness-dependent anisotropy or grain size);
- if polarity really varies, give a physical explanation consistent with OTT.

3. The OTT mechanism is not separated from heating-driven or thermally assisted switching. All switching uses 1 ms pulses, and the pulse-width study (Fig. 2d) uses 100 µs to 50 ms. These are long compared with any thermal time constant of a nanopillar. The dissipated power is about 1 to 2 mW in a 100 nm pillar, which can raise the device temperature by tens of kelvin or more. Only about 60 K separates room temperature from the 360 K transition at which the order (and the contrast) vanishes.

A current that heats the free layer close to or through its ordering transition and lets it re-order would produce switching with no spin torque. A thermoelectric or Oersted contribution (the Oersted field at the pillar edge at about 1 mA and 50 nm radius is a few mT) could then pick the final state.

The paper does not estimate the junction temperature during pulses, does not show switching at ns pulse widths, and does not show switching at reduced base temperature. The authors should:
- estimate the pulse-induced temperature rise, for example from the temperature dependence of R calibrated against R during the pulse;
- show switching at ns to µs pulse widths where heating is limited;
- show the base-temperature dependence of J_c below 300 K, which should behave differently for spin torque and for heating-to-transition.

4. The claim that the mechanism is "confirmed by our density functional theory calculations" (p. 3) is stronger than the calculations support. The DFT (Fig. 3, Extended Data Figs. 4 and 6, Methods Eq. 3) computes bulk, k‖-resolved conduction channels of PtMn3 along ⟨111⟩. It contains no junction calculation: no PtMn3/MgO/PtMn3 transmission, no non-equilibrium spin density, and no torque computed on the free layer.

The key quantity, β1 − β2, is inferred qualitatively from the colour maps and never evaluated. The micromagnetic simulation then sets β_eff = β1 − β2 = 1 (Extended Data Table 1), the maximal value, with η = 0.53. The agreement with the experimental J ≈ 10 MA/cm² therefore follows from these inputs and is not a prediction.

Beyond that, the argument that electrons "preferentially retain their sublattice identity and spin as they tunnel" relies on k‖ conservation (p. 7), which the authors' own STEM contradicts: the MgO is not well crystallized and the PtMn3 layers are polycrystalline with grains of various in-plane orientations. With in-plane grain rotation across the barrier, the Γ–K axis of sublattice 1 in one electrode is not aligned with that in the other.

The authors should either:
- provide a quantitative estimate of β1 − β2 (or of the torque per unit current) from a junction calculation, and discuss how it survives diffuse or incoherent tunnelling and grain misorientation; or
- reword the theory as a plausible symmetry-allowed mechanism, and drop "confirmed" and "consistent with the experimental observations".

They should also discuss how their mechanism relates to the previously predicted STT in noncollinear AFM junctions (ref. 46, Ghosh, Manchon and Železný, PRL 2022). The abstract's statement that the effect "has been assumed non-existent" does not reflect that earlier prediction.

B. Should address

5. The PtMn3 is not shown to be chemically ordered L1₂ with noncollinear order, although the whole interpretation assumes it. The films are deposited at room temperature with no post-annealing (Methods). The L1₂ Mn3Pt phase normally needs elevated-temperature growth or annealing to order, while a chemically disordered fcc Mn-Pt alloy has different (or no long-range noncollinear) magnetic order. The FFTs in Fig. 1d show the fundamental (111) spots only and do not show L1₂ superlattice reflections. No composition is given, and no XRD, magnetometry, or anomalous Hall/Néel-temperature characterization of the device stack is shown.
   - Please report stoichiometry (for example by RBS or XPS) and XRD of the device stack, including superlattice peaks if present.
   - Give independent evidence of the noncollinear phase and its transition temperature in these films, for example AHE in a reference Hall bar from the same wafer.

6. The NV magnetometry comes from a different material and should not be used to explain the device behaviour. Extended Data Fig. 2 is from an epitaxial (100) PtMn3 film grown on a single-crystal MgO substrate annealed at 850 °C, while the devices are polycrystalline (111) films grown at room temperature on amorphous SiO₂. The 100 to 170 nm "domain size" is used to explain why some devices switch abruptly and others gradually (p. 6). The stray field from the (100) surface may also come from grain structure, uncompensated moments or roughness rather than octupole domains. Please either image the actual device stack (or a sister film on SiO₂/Pt), or soften the conclusion. Please also explain how the domain-size argument fits with the fact that 200 nm devices (Extended Data Fig. 1d) show abrupt switching.

7. The energy-barrier analysis is partly circular and its parameters are inconsistent.
   - The main text (p. 7) says the 39 kT from Fig. 2d is "consistent with our theoretical estimation". But Methods (p. 23) obtains K_eff = 1 kJ/m³ by matching the model to Δ = 39 kT, so the agreement is built in.
   - K_eff = 1 kJ/m³ differs from K_u = 10 kJ/m³ in Extended Data Table 1.
   - M_s = 1110 emu/cm³ (Methods p. 23) differs from 153 kA/m (153 emu/cm³) in Extended Data Table 1.
   - The barrier model uses a 7 nm layer while the simulated free layer is 10 nm.
   - In Fig. 2d the two branches have very different slopes. A rough reading gives Δ of about 25 to 30 kT for AP→P and about 50 kT for P→AP. Please state how 39 kT was obtained (which branch, which fit function, and which diameter).
   - The result depends on the assumed t₀ = 1 ps. Please show the sensitivity.
   - The ms-long pulses put this analysis in the thermally activated regime, where heating (concern 3) also changes the effective temperature, so the extracted Δ should be treated as an effective value.
   - Please also justify the statement that 1 kJ/m³ is "consistent with the literature" (refs. 11, 60, 61): first-principles anisotropies for Mn3X are orders of magnitude larger, and a monodomain model may not apply to multidomain polycrystalline pillars.

8. The switching current densities quoted are inconsistent. The abstract and p. 5 give J ≈ 10 to 11 MA/cm². Fig. 2d shows J from about 10 to 85 MA/cm², with the P→AP branch at 60 to 85 MA/cm² even at the longest pulses. Please give the device diameter for Fig. 2c and 2d. Please also report the distribution of J_c over all switching devices alongside the TMR histogram, so that "of the order of 10 MA/cm²" reflects the typical device rather than the best one.

9. The 363% headline is an outlier. Fig. 2b shows that most devices have contrasts of 0 to 100%; only one device is above 350%, and the device in Fig. 2c shows about 50%. Please give the median and mean, the RA spread, and the yield (how many devices were measured, and how many switched at all). Please also discuss why the contrast is so much larger than in epitaxial AFMTJs (refs. 11–13) despite an amorphous barrier. A TMR calculation for the junction would help here, as would the bias dependence of the contrast, which in genuine TMR normally decreases with bias.

10. Some figures are described in the text in ways they do not show.
   - The text says that devices "showed clear saturation in both directions" (p. 6). Several Extended Data Fig. 1 loops show no saturation plateau before the reverse sweep (for example 75 nm right, 100 nm fourth panel).
   - Fig. 3b is labelled as a "polarization", yet its colour scale runs from 0 to 15 with no sign. Please clarify whether it shows N^(i↓) − N^(i↑), and why it is non-negative everywhere.
   - Fig. 3c and 3d define β1 and β2 schematically. Please give their numerical values from the DFT maps (integrated over the relevant k‖ regions).

11. The DFT settings need justification. Please justify U = 2 eV on Mn (and test sensitivity) and state the lattice constant used. The functional is given as PBE, but ref. 64 is the PBEsol paper; please clarify which was used. Please also confirm that the SOC calculation keeps the T1g (all-in/all-out in the (111) plane) configuration consistent with the stated spin directions σ̂_i.

C. Minor

12. Methods (p. 27) refer to "Extended Data Fig. 1a with the green arrows". The green arrows are in Extended Data Fig. 5a.

13. Reference 34 is missing from the reference list (it jumps from 33 to 35), although refs. 34–38 are cited on p. 2.

14. Please state the read-current magnitude, whether the reading current is applied in both polarities, and whether the quoted resistances include lead and contact resistance in the two-terminal geometry.

15. Please state the number of repetitions per point and how the error bars in Fig. 2d were obtained.

16. Please state the diameter of the device in Fig. 2c.

17. "Record-high TMR" should be qualified, and compared explicitly with the values in refs. 11–13 and 42.

18. The outlook statements about terahertz operation and "fully eliminating dipole interaction" are not supported by data here. Please mark them as prospects.

19. Extended Data Fig. 5 shows switching in about 3 ps at 10 MA/cm² with α = 0.01. Please state whether thermal fluctuations were included, and whether switching is deterministic for both current polarities from an exactly collinear (φ = 0) initial state, where the Slonczewski-type torque vanishes.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Add one decisive figure that links current-written states to magnetic states. For example: R versus H (or field-cooled states) for a device, alongside its current-switching loop, together with a Pt/MgO/Pt control junction pulsed with the same protocol.
- Add ns-pulse switching data and a junction-temperature estimate. This would both strengthen the spin-torque interpretation and address the device relevance claimed in the outlook.
- Use a junction-level transport calculation (even a tight-binding or Wannier-based PtMn3/vacuum/PtMn3 model) to give β1 − β2 and the torque efficiency, and use that number, not β_eff = 1, in the LLG simulations.
- Characterize the chemical order and magnetic phase of the device stack directly (XRD superlattice peaks, composition, and AHE or transition temperature from a co-deposited Hall bar), and image domains in a film grown like the devices.

5. Verdict

Verdict: major revision
