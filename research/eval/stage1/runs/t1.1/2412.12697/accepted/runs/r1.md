Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper reports room-temperature spectroscopic ellipsometry on millimetre-sized CuAlO2 single crystals. It finds a sharp, intense transition near 3.78 eV that appears only for in-plane (ab) polarization. The reported consequences are a peak birefringence |Δn|max = 3.67 at 333 nm, |Δn| > 0.5 across the visible, and a Type-II hyperbolic window between epsilon-near-zero (ENZ) points near 3.8 and 6.2 eV. GGA band-structure and orbital-projection calculations attribute the anisotropy to a dipole-allowed Cu dz2 to Cu px/y transition enabled by the linear O-Cu-O dumbbell. A three-dimensionally bonded, non-van der Waals oxide with this combination of birefringence and UV hyperbolicity would be a useful addition to anisotropic photonics. The key experimental novelty is measuring the c-axis response of the bulk crystal directly. The headline Δn and the hyperbolic window both depend on how reliably that c-axis response was extracted.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- Bulk single crystals with well-defined facets allow direct access to both principal axes, which is rarely possible for layered anisotropic materials.
- The in-plane response is cross-checked independently. Near-normal-incidence reflectance on the (001) face is compared with reflectance calculated from the ellipsometric ε_ab (Fig. 3c), and the energy-loss function (Fig. 3d) is consistent with the sign change of ε_ab1.
- The microscopic picture is physically transparent: a Cu dz2 (A1g-like) to Cu px/y (Eu-like) transition at a centrosymmetric D3d Cu site, dipole-allowed for E ⊥ c and forbidden for E ∥ c. It is supported by orbital-projected bands and real-space Bloch functions (Fig. 4), and it agrees with earlier BSE work reporting a strong in-plane exciton.
- The benchmarking figures (Fig. 2e, Fig. 3e,f) are useful, and source data are deposited.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The extraction of the c-axis dielectric function, on which Δn and the hyperbolic window rest, is not validated (Methods, Eq. 1; main text p. 2; Fig. 2a,b; Fig. 3a,b).
   - What the paper does: it applies the isotropic two-phase inversion (Eq. 1) separately to the (001) top surface and to a side facet. It assumes that the "measured ellipsometric response is dominated by the dielectric-function component parallel to the s-polarized electric field", so that the side facet with s ∥ c gives ε_c.
   - Why this is not secure: ρ = r_p/r_s involves both principal components on any facet. At θ = 70°, the p-polarized wave on the side facet samples only the in-plane component ε_ab, through both its tangential and its normal field components.
   - The standard approximate result runs the other way. For anisotropic samples, the pseudodielectric function mainly tracks the principal component along the line where the plane of incidence meets the surface (the p-direction), not the s-direction (Aspnes, JOSA 70, 1275 (1980)). If that holds here, the side-facet ⟨ε⟩ would be strongly weighted toward ε_ab, not ε_c. The contrast is extreme: ε_ab ranges from about −20 to +47 (ε2) near 3.8 eV, while ε_c ≈ 3–4. In this regime the cross-terms cannot be assumed negligible.
   - Signs of possible trouble: n_c is unusually low for a Cu oxide (about 1.6–1.8 below 3 eV in Fig. 2a), and n_c shows a weak feature near 3.8 eV. Either could indicate residual mixing or that the isotropic inversion is not appropriate.
   - The Mueller-matrix validation does not close the gap. It compares the (001) surface with "a non-axis-aligned facet whose exact Miller index was not determined", not with the side facet actually used for ε_c.
   - Neither the side-facet orientation (indexing of the (100) facet, the misalignment of c within the surface plane, the azimuth of c relative to the s-direction) nor its uncertainty is reported.
   - Why it matters: every headline number (|Δn|max = 3.67, |Δk|max = 5.22, |Δn| = 1.66 at 370 nm, the Type-II condition ε_ab1·ε_c1 < 0, Δε1 in Fig. 3e) uses ε_c.
   - What the authors should do:
     - Fit a uniaxial model jointly to both facets, using multiple angles of incidence (for example 55°, 65°, 75°) and at least two azimuths on the side facet (c ⊥ and c ∥ to the plane of incidence). Generalized or Mueller-matrix ellipsometry on the side facet itself would also serve. Report the fit quality and the correlation between ε_ab and ε_c.
     - Show how much ε_c changes between the isotropic inversion and the full anisotropic model.
     - Give an independent check of ε_c: polarized near-normal-incidence reflectance on the (100) facet with E ∥ c and E ⊥ c, compared with reflectance calculated from the extracted ε_c and ε_ab. This is the c-axis counterpart of Fig. 3c and is straightforward with the existing microspectrometer and a polarizer.
     - Index the side facet by X-ray diffraction and propagate the orientation uncertainty into Δn.

B. Should address

2. The reflectance check does not cover most of the claimed hyperbolic window (Fig. 3c; p. 4–5). The microspectrometer range is 300–1050 nm, i.e. about 1.18–4.13 eV. The measured data therefore reach only the low-energy ENZ edge and the first ~0.3 eV of the 3.8–6.2 eV negative-ε_ab1 window. The text says the measured reflectance "exhibits a high-reflectance band in the same spectral region where ε_ab1 becomes negative" and that this agreement "confirms" the optically metallic band. The data support the onset of the band, not its extent or its upper ENZ near 6.2 eV. That upper ENZ also lies within ~0.25 eV of the ellipsometer limit (6.46 eV), where errors in the oscillator tails and Kramers–Kronig consistency are largest. The authors should state the range actually verified, give an uncertainty on the upper ENZ energy, and if possible add vacuum-UV reflectance or extend the ellipsometric range.

3. The "low-loss" description of the visible range needs quantitative support (abstract; p. 3; Fig. 2b,e). The crystals are black, attributed to ~1.4% Cu2+ defects. Both k_ab and k_c are about 0.07 even at 1 eV, which corresponds to α ≈ 7 × 10^3 cm^-1 and a 1/e intensity length of ~1.4 µm. The birefringence comparison in Fig. 2e is plotted without the matching loss, and several comparison materials (LiNbO3, calcite, KNbO3) are transparent at the 10^-4 level of k.
   - Ellipsometry has limited sensitivity to small k. The sub-gap k may also contain contributions from surface roughness or overlayers, which are not modelled (Eq. 1 is a bare two-phase model).
   - The authors should:
     - plot k_ab, k_c and a birefringence figure of merit (for example |Δn|/max(k_ab, k_c), or the retardance per 1/e absorption length) over the visible range;
     - include a roughness or overlayer layer in the model, or bound its effect, for example from AFM of the measured facets;
     - make clear in the abstract and Fig. 2e that the low-loss claim is relative and limited by sub-gap absorption in these crystals.

4. The band-gap assignment is not robust (Fig. 2d; p. 3). The Tauc fit is made on the low-energy flank of a sharp peak (3.78 eV) that the authors and earlier BSE work identify as excitonic. A direct-gap Tauc extrapolation is not well defined for an excitonic line shape, and the result (3.71 eV) depends on the window chosen. The SI reports an indirect gap of ~3.45 eV, and the literature generally places the fundamental gap of CuAlO2 lower still. Calling the material "wide direct bandgap" in the abstract, and stating that the value "exceeds previously reported experimental band gaps", therefore needs qualification. The authors should:
   - report the fitting window and the sensitivity of the gap to it;
   - state which polarization α refers to (it appears to be ab only), and give the corresponding c-axis absorption edge;
   - refer to the 3.71 eV value as the onset of the strong in-plane absorption rather than the fundamental gap.

5. The number of samples and reproducibility are not stated (Methods). It is unclear whether the top-surface and side-facet data come from the same crystal, and how many crystals or facets were measured. The authors should state this. They should also show the spread of n_ab, n_c and |Δn|max across at least two crystals and repeated spots, since Cu2+ concentration and surface quality may vary between crystals.

6. The theory is qualitative where a quantitative comparison is feasible (Fig. 4; p. 5). The argument for the anisotropy rests on GGA orbital characters, and the computed dielectric tensor is relegated to the SI. Since the peak is excitonic and the c-axis response is the quantity in question, the authors should show the calculated ε_ab and ε_c alongside the experiment in the main text. Even a scissor-corrected independent-particle tensor would do, and a BSE tensor taken from or recomputed after Refs. 17–19 would be better. This would show whether the measured ε_c magnitude (and hence Δn) is reproduced.
   - The selection-rule argument is made in terms of the D3d site symmetry of Cu, but the transition is placed at L along the L–F line, where the little group is lower. A short statement that the site-symmetry argument carries over to the relevant k-points would make the argument complete. Alternatively, show the momentum matrix elements p_x, p_z between the two bands along the path.

7. The hyperbolic figure of merit and the c-axis sign condition need explicit treatment (Fig. 3a,f). ε_c1 dips toward zero near 5.5–5.8 eV (Fig. 3a), close to the ε_c2 peak. If it crosses zero, the window there becomes elliptic or doubly negative rather than Type-II. The authors should:
   - state the minimum of ε_c1 with its uncertainty;
   - confirm that ε_c1 > 0 throughout 3.8–6.2 eV;
   - define the figure of merit consistently with the comparison literature, noting that FOM = |ε1|/ε2 for one component ignores loss in the other;
   - note that FOM ≈ 1.9 implies strongly damped hyperbolic modes.

C. Minor

8. Fig. 3c: state whether the sample/Al ratio was multiplied by the known Al reflectance to obtain absolute R. The agreement in absolute R in Fig. 3c suggests it was, but the Methods do not say so.
9. Raman spectra are described in the Methods but not discussed in the main text. Either refer to the SI result (for example, a phase-purity or orientation check) or remove the description.
10. Fig. 2e: the survey omits some strongly birefringent bulk crystals relevant to the "largest among UV–visible lower-loss materials" claim. Examples are quasi-1D chalcogenides and other recent bulk oxides. Either broaden the comparison or state the selection criteria more explicitly. Also indicate on the plot which CuAlO2 points lie in the strongly absorbing region.
11. Fig. 1c: the cartoon labels the c-axis polarization as "transmitted". The crystals are opaque in bulk (black, k ≈ 0.07). Clarify that this refers to the selection rule, not to measured transmission.
12. p. 2: the statement that |Δn| "remains above 1 over most of the range from 2.86 eV to 6.2 eV" should be checked against Fig. 2c. Above 4.5 eV the curve oscillates around values of ~0.5–1.5. Giving the fraction of the range would be clearer.
13. Report the ellipsometer spot size relative to the side-facet dimensions (~100 µm scale bars in Fig. 1b). Also say whether focusing probes were used, since overfilling a narrow facet would add edge or epoxy contributions to the side-facet data.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Add a dedicated SI section on the anisotropic ellipsometric analysis: the model, angles, azimuths, fit residuals, the correlation between ε_ab and ε_c, and a comparison with the isotropic inversion. Present the polarized (100)-facet reflectance as the c-axis counterpart of Fig. 3c.
- Measure the temperature dependence of the 3.78 eV peak (for example from 10 K to 300 K). This would test its excitonic character and show whether Δn can be increased by narrowing the line.
- Show a device-relevant metric, such as the thickness needed for a quarter-wave plate at 400–700 nm and its transmission given the measured k. This would make the photonics case concrete.
- If feasible, compare with a less defective (more transparent) crystal or an annealed sample to separate intrinsic from Cu2+-related sub-gap loss.

5. Verdict: major revision
