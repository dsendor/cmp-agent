Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors use an on-chip picosecond photoconductive-switch transport platform to send ~2 ps current pulses through thin-film NbN and YBa2Cu3O7 strips. The aim is to outrun vortex entry and self-heating and so reach the intrinsic depairing current density Jc*, which d.c. measurements cannot reach. In NbN they report a sharp collapse of the transmitted supercurrent at about 2.2 times the d.c. critical current density. They compare its temperature dependence with a depairing estimate and with Usadel-based dynamics. In YBCO they report a gradual suppression, which they attribute to the d-wave gap. A direct transport probe of the depairing limit on picosecond timescales would be new and broadly interesting. The NbN result is the strongest part of the paper. The YBCO interpretation and the quantitative comparison with depairing theory are less well supported.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- A clean, well-motivated experimental idea. The transmitted pulse amplitude serves as an in situ, time-resolved measure of how much current the condensate carries. The 20 K normal-state reference, measured in the same device and geometry, is a useful internal control (Fig. 3b, Fig. 4a,b).
- The NbN data are qualitatively striking: the normalized transmitted field is flat up to ~2.2 Jc and then drops abruptly (Fig. 4a). The transmitted-versus-incident current shows two linear regimes and a local maximum that defines Jc* (Fig. 4b). Positive and negative pulse polarities give the same result, so rectification or switch asymmetry is unlikely.
- Jc* is tracked as a function of temperature together with d.c. Jc (Fig. 5a), and microscopic (Usadel) dynamics capture the sharp drop (Fig. 5b).
- The s-wave versus d-wave contrast, if it holds up, points to a new transport-based route to gap anisotropy, with possible applications in superconducting electronics.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

(None. The central NbN observation is supported by the data shown, the normal-state reference and polarity symmetry. The concerns below make the interpretation and the quantitative claims weaker or narrower, but they do not overturn the main finding.)

B. Should address

1. The d-wave attribution for YBCO rests on one temperature, one device and no quantitative model (Fig. 4c,d; Discussion). The gradual suppression is measured only at 50 K, which is about 0.59 Tc for a film with Tc ≈ 85 K. Several other explanations are not ruled out:
   - Thermally excited quasiparticles at this relatively high reduced temperature.
   - Inhomogeneity in a 50 nm commercial film on CeO2-buffered sapphire: twin boundaries, oxygen-deficient regions, and a Tc below the 90 K of optimally doped YBCO.
   - The fact that the largest transmitted currents (~200 GA m^-2, Fig. 4d) appear to lie well below standard estimates of the YBCO depairing current density (several hundred MA cm^-2, i.e. several TA m^-2). The suppression may therefore come from a weaker extrinsic channel rather than from nodal depairing.

   The text also says that for d-wave superconductors "Jc* is not a well-defined quantity". This overstates the theory. Calculations of depairing in d-wave superconductors still give a maximum in J(v_s). The nodes make the superfluid density fall gradually, roughly linearly in current (the nonlinear Meissner effect), but they do not remove the critical current.

   To fix this: (i) measure YBCO at a substantially lower temperature, for example 10–20 K, where a nodal-depairing explanation still predicts a gradual onset but thermal smearing does not; (ii) show NbN at a reduced temperature comparable to 0.6 Tc (the raw data of SI section 7 could be brought into the main text), so the s-wave and d-wave comparison is made at matched T/Tc; (iii) compare the YBCO curve quantitatively, at least to the expected linear-in-current reduction of n_s and to an independent estimate of the YBCO depairing scale; (iv) rephrase the "not well-defined" statement. The abstract's claim that the YBCO response "reflects its d-wave symmetry" should be softened unless (i)–(iii) support it.

2. The depairing estimate used for Fig. 5a does not match the regime of the sample or the paper's own simulation. The analytic Jc*(T) comes from the clean-limit Landau criterion ħk_F·v_s = Δ, giving J* = 2ħe n_s/(π m ξ). But NbN is treated as a dirty-limit superconductor in Fig. 5b (Usadel equation). In the dirty limit the depairing current is set differently: by Kupriyanov–Lukichev, or the GL form J_GL = Φ0/(3√3 π μ0 λ² ξ) near Tc. Gapless superconductivity also sets in before the Landau criterion is reached. "Excellent agreement" is claimed without error bars on the measured Jc* or on the calculated curve. It is also unclear from the main text whether n_s(T) and ξ(T) (SI section 9) are independently measured (for example from λ(T) and Hc2(T)) or adjusted. Because O(1) prefactors differ between these formulas, agreement at the level shown in Fig. 5a is not a sharp test. The authors should:
   - compute Jc*(T) in the same dirty-limit framework as Fig. 5b, or compare with Kupriyanov–Lukichev;
   - state which inputs are measured independently;
   - put uncertainties on both the data and the calculation.

3. The absolute current-density calibration, and so the ratio 2.2 × Jc, needs uncertainties. The current density is inferred from the sampled transmitted field through E(t)·w = S·J(t)·Z0, with Z0 ≈ 50 Ω. Details are deferred to SI section 5. The headline factor of 2.2 combines this picosecond calibration with a d.c. Jc measured in a different way (Fig. 2), so any systematic error in the switch-sampling scale, in waveguide dispersion or loss between the sample and the sampling switch, or in the effective cross-section S (20 nm thickness, ~10 µm width) feeds straight into the ratio and into the Fig. 5a comparison. Please:
   - give the uncertainty of the absolute J scale and how it propagates to Jc* and Jc*/Jc;
   - give error bars for every point in Fig. 5a;
   - describe the d.c. Jc criterion and protocol in Methods (continuous or pulsed d.c., voltage criterion, sweep rate), since Fig. 2 shows abrupt jumps typical of thermal runaway.
   - Note that d.c. Jc depends on pinning and geometry, so 2.2 is a device-specific ratio, not a material constant. The abstract should say so.

4. Heating within the pulse and the role of thermal runaway are not quantified. Once the strip becomes resistive, the dissipation is large. At J ≈ 220 GA m^-2 the current through a 10 µm × 20 nm strip is ~40 mA, and with a normal resistance of ~140 Ω (Fig. 2a) this deposits of order 10^-13–10^-12 J within ~2 ps into a ~10^-17 m^3 volume. Using typical NbN heat capacities, that would raise the electron temperature by many kelvin. This probably explains the slow tail toward the normal state that the BCS simulation misses (Fig. 5b). It also raises a question about the onset: a small amount of dissipation, for example from edge vortex nucleation, current crowding at the contacts, or quasiparticle generation by stray 515 nm light from the nearby switches, could trigger a fast hot-spot collapse that looks as sharp as intrinsic depairing. The authors should:
   - give an electron-temperature estimate during and after the pulse;
   - show that the onset current does not depend on the optical power at the sampling switches or on repetition rate;
   - if possible, compare the onset with a two-temperature model that includes the Usadel depairing.

5. The dependence on pulse duration is not reported. The depairing current is an equilibrium, steady-state concept. Under a ~2 ps drive the order parameter responds on the scale of τ_GL, and the quasiparticles relax on the electron–phonon time (~10 ps in NbN). Both are comparable to or longer than the pulse, and τ_GL grows as T → Tc. The observed threshold could therefore lie above or below the equilibrium Jc*, and by different amounts at different temperatures (Fig. 5a extends to ~0.83 Tc). Measuring Jc* versus pulse width, even over a modest range achievable with different switch gaps or filtering, would show whether the observed onset is the intrinsic limit or a kinetic threshold. This directly tests the paper's main premise: the pulse must be short enough to freeze vortices, but long enough for the condensate to reach the quasi-equilibrium depairing state.

6. Number of devices and reproducibility are not stated. The main text does not say how many NbN and YBCO devices were measured, or whether the sharp drop at ~2.2 Jc and the gradual YBCO response appear in more than one device or after thermal cycling. Please state the number of devices and show the device-to-device spread of Jc* and Jc*/Jc, for example in the SI.

7. Part of the agreement in Fig. 5b comes from fitting. The Usadel simulation takes its parameters from the sample characterization "apart from Dynes broadening, which is set to guarantee numerical convergence and to reproduce the experimental results". Since the Dynes parameter changes the low-energy density of states, it is likely to move the threshold and change how sharp the drop is. The authors should show how sensitive the simulated threshold and sharpness are to the Dynes parameter, state its value, and indicate which features of Fig. 5b are predictions and which are fitted. The tDGL comparison is acknowledged to be outside its range of validity at 7 K (0.47 Tc). It therefore does not establish that the sharpness is specific to s-wave pairing and should be presented as a qualitative reference only.

8. The vortex-exclusion argument should be quantitative for this geometry. The Discussion argues that vortices move at most a few tens of nanometres during the pulse. But the resistive signal does not require full vortex crossing. Edge nucleation (with current crowding near the waveguide contacts and corners) and partial motion of many vortices also produce a voltage. The self-field of a ~40 mA current at the edge of a 20 nm-thick strip can exceed the edge-penetration field, even after zero-field cooling. The argument would be stronger with: an estimate of the edge field and entry barrier at Jc*; an estimate of the voltage from the vortex density and velocity achievable in 2 ps; and, ideally, a narrower or wider strip, or a different edge geometry, showing that Jc* does not scale with the width the way an edge-entry threshold would.

C. Minor

9. Fig. 5a has five temperatures for Jc and Jc*, but Fig. 2a shows d.c. R(J) only at 7, 8.5 and 10 K. Please give the raw d.c. curves for all temperatures in Fig. 5a, and check that the Jc values plotted in Fig. 5a match the transition currents in Fig. 2a at the same temperatures.

10. In Fig. 4a and 4c the x-axis is the transmitted peak current, so the curve becomes multivalued where it tilts leftward. A companion panel against the incident current (as in Fig. 4b,d), on the same normalized scale, would make the sharpness easier to judge. The Fig. 4a caption says filled symbols mark positive and negative pulses, but the plotted symbols look open. Please check.

11. Fig. 4c is described as an "immediate and continuous drop" from zero current. At the lowest currents the data look nearly flat. Please quantify the initial slope (with uncertainty) so that "immediate" can be judged, since this is the key qualitative difference from NbN.

12. The sample dimensions (width ≈ 10 µm, length ≈ 30 µm) appear only in the Discussion. Please add them to Methods together with the YBCO device dimensions and the switch-to-sample distances.

13. Please quote current densities also in MA cm^-2, the unit most of the depairing literature uses, so readers can compare directly with values for NbN and YBCO.

14. Typo on p. 1812: "normal sate".

15. The kinetic-inductance nonlinearity below Jc* deserves a comment. Even in BCS theory, n_s decreases somewhat as J approaches Jc*, which should slightly reduce the transmitted amplitude before the drop. A sentence on why the NbN transmission stays flat up to 2.2 Jc (Fig. 4a), and whether the Usadel calculation reproduces this, would help.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Add YBCO data at low temperature and NbN data at matched T/Tc. This is the most direct way to turn the s-wave/d-wave contrast from suggestive into convincing.
- Measure the onset as a function of pulse width (and, if possible, strip width). This would separate intrinsic depairing from kinetic or edge-entry thresholds and support the paper's central premise.
- Use one dirty-limit framework (Usadel or Kupriyanov–Lukichev) for both Fig. 5a and Fig. 5b, with independently measured inputs and uncertainty bands.
- Include a heating budget, such as a two-temperature estimate, for the post-onset regime to explain the slow tail and strengthen the claim that the onset itself is non-thermal.
