Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors measure in-plane resistivity and high-field (35 T) magnetoresistance on combinatorial La2-xCexCuO4 films spanning 0.116 ≤ x ≤ 0.188 in fine doping steps. They model the data with a Shockley-Chambers Boltzmann treatment that has hotspots at the AFM zone boundary and an anisotropic T-linear scattering channel. A single doping-dependent factor g(x) on that channel falls by about an order of magnitude as Tc goes to zero. From this the authors argue that g(x) is an effective spin-fluctuation coupling constant and that spin fluctuations mediate pairing in electron-doped cuprates. The dataset is unusual: dense doping coverage, matched growth conditions and high fields. The attempt to tie the strength of the anisotropic T-linear scattering, rather than only the A1 coefficient, to Tc would interest a broad audience. The novelty over the known A1–Tc correlation (Jin et al. 2011; Yuan et al. 2022) rests on the MR modelling that isolates an anisotropic inelastic channel and on the Hall analysis that places it at (π,π) hotspots. So the robustness of the modelling decides how much the paper adds.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- A dense, internally consistent MR dataset (13 channels on S1, 7 on S2) taken on films grown under identical conditions. It shows a non-Kohler MR that peaks near 50–75 K and then falls (Fig. 2a–g, Fig. 15). This is a clear, model-independent observation.
- The H/T derivative plots (Fig. 2h–l) show that the quadrature scaling seen in hole-doped cuprates fails in LCCO. This is a useful empirical contrast between the two sides of the phase diagram.
- Fig. 9 tests alternative scattering forms (no hotspots, hotspots only, antinodal hotspots, antinodal broad anisotropy). This is a welcome attempt to show which ingredients the data demand.
- Fig. 8 shows that the longitudinal MR is negligible at 75 K, which supports an orbital origin of the transverse MR at that temperature.
- The phenomenology connects to earlier work on Bechgaard salts and to Hall-effect current vertex corrections in PCCO, and it suggests concrete follow-up experiments.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. Whether g(x) can be determined uniquely, given which parameters are held fixed. The central quantitative result, Fig. 5c and Table 3, comes from fitting only ρ(0, 40 K) and Δρ/ρ(0) at 20 T, 40 K for each S1 channel with a single free parameter g(x). Everything else is held fixed at its x = 0.159 value (Table 2): τ_imp^-1 = 27 ps^-1, α2, the hotspot height and width, ν = 12, and apparently the Fermi surface. Three problems follow.
   (i) ρ(0, 40 K) falls 3-fold across S1 (Fig. 5a). With τ_imp fixed, any x-dependence of the residual resistivity, from Ce disorder, oxygen non-stoichiometry or a composition gradient, is absorbed into g(x). The residual resistivity of each S1 channel is not shown, because S1 was measured only at discrete temperatures (Fig. 6b, c).
   (ii) In the Methods (p. 16) the authors themselves state that doubling the hotspot width or reducing its height 5-fold changes ρ by about 1 μΩcm, and that hotspot sharpening "may explain the larger increase of g(x) below x = 0.14". So part of the steep rise of g(x) at low x may be a hotspot effect, not a change in the T-linear coupling.
   (iii) Table 1 gives tight-binding parameters that depend on x, but the text does not say whether the Fermi surface was updated for each channel. A changing carrier density alone changes ρ.
   Two numbers per channel cannot separate these possibilities. The authors should:
   - fit the full ρ(H, T) at several T (20–50 K) for at least three or four S1 dopings spanning the range, with τ_imp tied to the measured ρ0 for each channel and the hotspot parameters free or scanned;
   - report g(x) with uncertainties that propagate this freedom, using Fig. 5c with error bars;
   - state explicitly which band structure is used at each x.
   If the order-of-magnitude drop in g(x) survives, the claim is much stronger. If it does not, the "coupling parameter" interpretation needs to be revised.

2. Whether the low-T fall of the MR is orbital. Claim 1, that the anisotropy lies in the inelastic channel, rests on the MR falling below about 60 K faster than ρ itself. Yet the paper documents a negative, non-orbital MR component in several places:
   - below x_FSR it is attributed to an isotropic spin effect (p. 3; Fig. 15);
   - at x = 0.159 in film S2 the MR is smaller than in S1 by a factor of 3.5, which the authors attribute to a negative MR component (Fig. 10c, d);
   - Fig. 15 and its caption treat the fall of γ1 below 75 K on both sides of x_FSR as having the same AFM origin, which suggests the same spin-scattering mechanism may operate above x_FSR.
   A negative, isotropic spin MR that grows on cooling would reproduce the key observation without any anisotropy in the inelastic channel. The only orbital test, the longitudinal MR in Fig. 8, is shown at 75 K alone, above the window that is modelled (20–50 K). The caption notes that a non-zero longitudinal MR appears at lower T.
   The authors should show the longitudinal (H ∥ I ∥ ab) MR at 20–50 K for several dopings in S1, including x = 0.159, and subtract it, or bound it, before fitting. They should also explain why S1 is free of the negative component that dominates S2 at the same nominal doping.

3. The Hall analysis that identifies the scattering as (π,π) spin fluctuations is only semi-quantitative. The step from "anisotropic T-linear scattering" to "spin fluctuations" rests on the Hall coefficient (pp. 7–8; Figs. 11–13), and four gaps weaken it:
   (a) R_H is never calculated as a function of T or x. Fig. 13 gives single numbers for four forms of Γ(φ), and the temperature and g(x) that go into Fig. 13d are not stated.
   (b) The CVC parameters are chosen by hand: the Gaussian ε(φ) with σ = 0.07, its amplitude, and c and ξ_AFM. The 1/(1−ε²) prefactor is dropped, and no sensitivity analysis is shown.
   (c) The Hall data in Fig. 11 come from other films (Refs. 5, 40), not from S1 or S2, and the two published datasets differ by about a factor 1.5–2 in magnitude.
   (d) The text says R_H "becomes negative at elevated T" for x > x_FSR. In Fig. 11 the curves for x = 0.15–0.18 in panel a and for 0.154 and 0.174 in panel b remain positive to 100 K, so only a downturn is shown.
   The authors should:
   - compute R_H(T) from 20 to 100 K for the same Γ(φ, T, x) used for the MR (Table 2 plus g(x)) at two or three dopings, with and without CVCs;
   - compare the results with measured R_H(T) at the same dopings;
   - show how the result depends on σ and on the ε amplitude;
   - state clearly that the Γ_N of Fig. 13d is exactly the Eq. (1) form. The text describes it as "peaked along the zone diagonals", whereas Eq. (1) with HS = 27.5° peaks at the hotspots.
   Ideally they would also measure the Hall effect on the S1 and S2 channels themselves. Without this, the antinodal charge-fluctuation scenario is excluded only by the sign of one computed number.

B. Should address

4. Correlation versus coupling, and finite g(x) where Tc = 0. The pairing claim rests on g(x) correlating with Tc (Fig. 5c). Three points weaken it:
   - Any quantity that falls monotonically across the overdoped side, such as A1, the AFM correlation length or the residual resistivity, would correlate with Tc in the same way.
   - g(x) does not go to zero where Tc does. It saturates at about 0.55 for x = 0.184–0.188 (Table 4, Fig. 5c), well past the point where Tc vanishes (about x ≈ 0.175 on the top axis). A McMillan-type picture, which the paper invokes through Allen's λ_tr ≈ λ_McM (Fig. 7), would predict a finite Tc for finite coupling unless pair breaking intervenes.
   - The abstract refers to λ_sf, but no dimensionless coupling is ever computed.
   With α1 = 0.75 ps^-1 K^-1 (Table 2), ħ/τ = 2πλ k_B T gives a peak λ of roughly 0.9 g(x). Averaging over the sin^12 form reduces this by about a factor of 4. These values could be compared with spin-fluctuation Eliashberg estimates of Tc.
   The authors should give an angle-averaged λ_sf(x) and discuss whether it is consistent with Tc(x), including why Tc vanishes while g stays finite (pair breaking by disorder, d-wave sensitivity to ρ0). Short of that, they should soften "identifies ... as the primary pairing glue" to a consistency statement.

5. Reproducibility between films and normalisation. Only two films were measured, and they overlap at a single doping. At that common doping:
   - the resistivities differ by a factor of 1.5 (Fig. 6a), and S2 is rescaled to match S1;
   - the MR differs by a factor of 3.5 (Fig. 10c);
   - the S1 resistivities are about 1/3 of those in Ref. 5 for similar x (Fig. 6b).
   Because g(x) for S2 is taken directly from A1 normalised to S1 (Table 4), the S2 half of Fig. 5c reproduces the known A1(x) trend and is not an independent MR-based determination. The "order of magnitude" drop therefore partly comes from joining two different methods. The authors should state the thickness and geometric-factor uncertainties and quote the range of g(x) from S1 alone (6.5 to 1.0). Ideally they would also show MR on a third, or a non-combinatorial, film at two or three dopings to establish that the MR magnitude, the observable that constrains the anisotropy, is reproducible.

6. The model is tested in full at only one doping. The T- and H-dependent comparison is made only at x = 0.159 between 20 and 50 K (Fig. 4). Other S1 dopings are compared at a single T (Fig. 10a, b). Two features of the data lie outside the modelled window and are not shown to be reproduced: the MR maximum at 50–75 K and its x-independent onset near 75 K (Fig. 2, Fig. 15), and the kink in dρ/dT near 60 K (Fig. 6e). Even at x = 0.159 the agreement is imperfect. The simulated dρ/dH at 20 K turns over above about 25 T (Fig. 4b4), whereas the data keep rising (Fig. 4a4). The simulated MR is also systematically below the data across S1 (Fig. 5b). The authors should show simulations of Δρ/ρ(T) and dρ/dH(H) over 20–100 K at two or three other dopings, for example x = 0.125 and 0.146, with the same parameters except g(x). They should also quantify goodness of fit, for example the residuals, for the Eq. (1) form against the Fig. 9 alternatives, so that "inferior" is quantitative.

7. The physical basis of the scattering ansatz. In Eq. (1) the hotspot term τ_HS^-1 is independent of temperature, yet in a spin-fluctuation picture hotspot scattering is the most strongly T-dependent channel. ν = 12 is borrowed from a hole-doped parameterisation (Ref. 19) and never varied. Assigning the anisotropy to the T² term as well is described as "for simplicity". The authors should:
   - justify an elastic hotspot term, for example as disorder-enhanced scattering near the AFM zone boundary;
   - show how g(x) and the fit quality change when ν is varied (for example 4–16) and when the anisotropy is confined to the T-linear term;
   - give units for the hotspot width in Table 2 and list ν and HS there.
   As it stands it is unclear which features are forced by the data and which by the choice of parameterisation.

8. Composition gradient and inhomogeneity within a channel. In a combinatorial film each voltage pair spans a finite Δx. The authors themselves raise thickness or composition variation as a possible origin of the residual H-linear MR (p. 8). That same gradient can also mix Hall voltage into the longitudinal signal and produce a linear-in-H MR, which is the very feature that motivates the hotspots (point 3 on p. 5). The authors should give Δx across each voltage pair and the uncertainty of the x assignment, and check that the H-linear slope does not scale with the local gradient. Antisymmetrising the MR with respect to field direction, if not already done, should be stated.

9. Superconducting fluctuations in the fitted window. For the low-x channels (Tc ≈ 20–25 K), data at 20–40 K, including the zero-field ρ(0, 40 K) that fixes g(x), lie close enough to Tc that paraconductivity and its field suppression can contribute a positive MR and depress ρ(0). The authors note fluctuation effects in the longitudinal MR at low T (Fig. 8 discussion). They should estimate the fluctuation contribution at 40 K for x ≤ 0.125, for example from the high-field ρ(H) or an Aslamazov–Larkin estimate, and show that g(x) at low x is not affected.

10. The extrapolation to hole-doped cuprates. In the closing discussion (p. 9), the success of the Boltzmann model in LCCO is said to provide "a strong counter-argument that such a parameterisation should already have been found, were it to exist" for p-doped cuprates. This does not follow from the data presented and should be phrased as a conjecture. Relatedly, the Planckian statement (p. 8) compares a peak anisotropic rate, which varies with x, with a bound on an averaged rate. The angle-averaged T-linear rate in units of k_B T/ħ should be given at each x, together with its uncertainty from the film-thickness question raised in point 5.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a figure showing g(x) with uncertainty bands from a global fit of ρ(H, T) at several temperatures and dopings, with τ_imp tied to the measured ρ0 and the hotspot parameters free. This directly addresses whether the order-of-magnitude drop is robust.
2. Measure the longitudinal MR and the Hall effect on the same S1 channels between 20 and 75 K. A combined fit of ρ_xx(H, T) and R_H(T) with one Γ(φ, T, x) would turn the Hall argument from qualitative into quantitative.
3. Convert g(x) into an angle-averaged dimensionless λ_sf(x) and compare it with Tc(x) through a spin-fluctuation Eliashberg or McMillan-type estimate. Discuss explicitly why Tc vanishes while g stays finite.
4. Move the key alternative-model comparison (Fig. 9), with quantitative residuals, into the main text, because the spin-fluctuation identification depends on it.

5. Verdict: major revision
