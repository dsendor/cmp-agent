Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper reports that a biased AFM tip moved along set paths ("trailing fields") shifts the boundary between regions of opposite net in-plane polarization in a PbTiO3/SrTiO3 trilayer with a polar-vortex phase on DyScO3. The boundary moves by up to about 2 µm, and the direction of motion depends on both the bias polarity and the direction the tip travels. The authors show the motion is reversible and that the moved state survives for 120 days. They support it with in-situ cross-sectional STEM biasing and phase-field simulations that link the motion to a change in the zigzag arrangement of the vortex cores. Ferroelectric domain writing with a tip is well established, but deterministic, reversible, direction-selective displacement of a boundary inside a vortex lattice is new and relevant to proposals for vortex-based memory. The physical mechanism behind the dependence on tip direction is less well established than the phenomenon itself.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The LPFM sequences are clear and systematic (Figs. 2a–h, 3a–p). They show monotonic, stepwise boundary displacement over hundreds of nanometres to about 2 µm, and reversal by changing either the bias polarity or the tip direction. This is a striking and useful level of control.
- The control appears in three separate regions (Figs. 2, 3 and 4), in both polarity/direction combinations.
- Retention is checked over 2, 8, 30 and 120 days (Fig. 4), which matters for the device claims.
- The in-situ STEM biasing (Fig. 2j) gives unit-cell-scale evidence that the vortices survive the field and that their zigzag arrangement changes. This gives a microscopic picture behind the PFM contrast.
- The phase-field simulations (Fig. 2i, k; Supplementary Fig. 9) reproduce stepwise motion of a boundary between flat and zigzag segments without destroying the vortex lattice.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

None. The central experimental claim, controlled and reversible boundary motion with long retention, is supported by the PFM sequences in Figs. 2–4 and the in-situ STEM in Fig. 2j. The gaps below narrow or qualify the claims but do not leave a main claim unsupported.

B. Should address

1. **The mechanism for the dependence on tip direction is not modelled or explained.** The most distinctive result is in Fig. 3: at fixed bias, reversing the tip direction (PD versus PU, ND versus NU) reverses the boundary motion. The boundary also moves perpendicular to the tip path, and by hundreds of nanometres per pass.
   - The phase-field simulations (Fig. 2k) model a cross-section with an electrode stepped along [100]. They contain no tip moving along the vortex tubes and no in-plane trailing field.
   - In the FEM, the asymmetry is put in by hand ("a small perturbation was applied to the tip boundary potential based on the probe's motion direction", Methods). It therefore cannot explain the asymmetry.
   - The schematics in Fig. 2l, m are qualitative.
   
   As written, the abstract's statement that the simulations "pinpoint the underlying physical mechanism" covers the zigzag reconfiguration but not the dependence on tip direction or the long-range displacement.
   
   The authors should do one of the following:
   - (a) Compute the actual in-plane field of a moving, biased tip. The moving source needs a physical origin, such as surface-charge deposition, screening lag or finite polarization relaxation. Then show in phase-field that a tip translated along the tubes (along Y in Fig. 1f) produces the observed sign of displacement.
   - (b) Narrow the mechanistic claim to the zigzag reconfiguration and state plainly that the dependence on tip direction is not yet explained.

2. **What the LPFM contrast and the "vortex boundary" mean needs to be established more firmly.** The vortex period is about 14 nm and the tip radius is about 25 nm, so the micrometre-scale LPFM phase domains in Figs. 1–4 must reflect the net in-plane polarization of the zigzag arrangement, not individual vortices. The paper never states this plainly. It also does not give:
   - the cantilever orientation relative to the vortex-tube axis (along [010]pc in Fig. 1f);
   - vertical PFM of the same areas;
   - a cantilever-rotation or vector-PFM check that the contrast comes from a single in-plane component of definite sign.
   
   Without these, a reader cannot tell which polarization component flips across the moved boundary. Nor can they rule out that tip writing creates a different state, such as a/c domains or a flux-closure arrangement, that merely shares the LPFM phase of the pristine purple domains.
   
   The in-situ STEM (Fig. 2j) addresses this only partly. It is a FIB lamella with altered boundary conditions, written with +17 V from a tungsten tip, and the boundary moves by tens of nanometres rather than micrometres.
   
   The authors should:
   - give the cantilever geometry and show VPFM plus LPFM at two cantilever orientations for one written region;
   - ideally show cross-sectional STEM or RSM satellite peaks from a region written by the AFM tip in the film itself, confirming that the vortex phase survives in the swept area.

3. **The coercive-bias evidence (Fig. 1b, c) is ambiguous and not linked to in-plane switching.**
   - The V_C map in Fig. 1b uses a colour scale of −50 to 200 mV, but the loops in Fig. 1c have coercive voltages of roughly ±1–2 V. "Average coercive bias" probably means (V+ + V−)/2, which is an imprint, not a coercive field. The text should define the quantity.
   - In Fig. 1c the "boundary" loop is mainly shifted relative to the "vortex" loop rather than narrower. This argues for a different imprint at boundaries, not a lower switching barrier.
   - The SS-PFM channel (vertical or lateral) is not stated. The phase axis suggests out-of-plane switching, whose relation to motion of an in-plane boundary is unclear.
   - How the 10 "representative points" were chosen is not described, and the spread is not shown.
   
   The authors should state the channel and the definition of V_C, show loop statistics (mean ± spread of coercive width and offset for boundary and interior pixels across the whole 50×50 map), and soften the claim if only the offset differs.

4. **Dependences that would establish "deterministic" control are missing.**
   - Displacement per pass varies widely: 256, 366, 148 and 400 nm for PD1 to PD7 in Fig. 2, and 660, 60 and 740 nm in Fig. 3.
   - Reversal is incomplete: 242 nm remains after ND6 in Fig. 2h, and 490 and 540 nm remain in Fig. 3h, p.
   - Methods state that the trailing field "can be varied by changing the magnitude of the applied voltage", but no data on voltage, velocity or number of passes are shown.
   
   The authors should show displacement per pass against tip bias (including a threshold), tip velocity (50 nm/s is the only value) and path spacing from the boundary. They should also summarize the statistics over all trials and regions, including the number of attempts and failures. This would turn three examples into a characterized effect and explain the step-to-step variation.

5. **Sample statistics and controls.**
   - All results appear to come from a single trilayer. The number of films and regions is not stated.
   - There is no reference sample without the vortex phase, for example a PTO film with a/c domains or a superlattice in a different phase, written under identical trailing-field conditions. Such a control would show whether direction-selective boundary motion is specific to the zigzag vortex state or a generic feature of tip-induced in-plane switching, as already known in BiFeO3 and other in-plane ferroelectrics.
   
   The authors should state how many samples and regions were measured and, ideally, add such a control. This bears directly on the novelty claim.

6. **Possible perturbation from imaging, and retention conditions.**
   - Imaging uses DART drive amplitudes of 0.6–1.25 V (Methods). Fig. 1b, c imply that boundary regions switch at lower bias than vortex interiors, so repeated imaging may itself move boundaries. The authors should show repeated scans without writing at the highest drive amplitude used, establishing that imaging alone does not displace the boundary.
   - For Fig. 4, storage conditions (temperature, humidity, ambient or vacuum) are not given, and the same region was rescanned several times.
   - In Fig. 4f, other unwritten domains also change contrast compared with Fig. 4a–c. A quantitative measure, such as boundary position against time with uncertainty, would support "no measurable relaxation".

7. **Language about topological protection.** Several statements attribute retention and the preserved state to topological protection, including "topologically protected motion" (p. 3) and "persists owing to the topological protection" (p. 6). No topological quantity (for example winding number or Pontryagin density) is computed before and after motion, in either the simulations or the STEM polar maps. Thermal or field robustness is not tested either. The authors should either compute the topological invariant across the boundary in the simulations and STEM maps, or describe the stability as metastability of the vortex lattice without invoking protection.

8. **The sample structure is inconsistent within the paper.** The main text, figure titles and phase-field Methods describe an STO/PTO/STO trilayer (20 uc STO/20 uc PTO/20 uc STO). The Methods sections "Growth of the trilayer", "X-ray diffraction" and "Reciprocal space mapping" describe a PTO/STO/PTO trilayer (20 uc PbTiO3/20 uc SrTiO3/20 uc PbTiO3). These structures differ in electrostatics and in where the vortices sit. The authors must state which structure was grown and correct the other descriptions.

C. Minor

9. **FEM parameters do not match the sample.** The FEM uses a 100 nm dielectric with isotropic εr = 100. The real film is about 24 nm thick (60 uc) on a conducting SRO layer and is strongly anisotropic. The authors should use the real stack or justify the simplification, and report the in-plane field magnitude under the tip at 5 V.

10. **Phase-field parameter choices.** The DMI-like coefficients D_N = 0.1 and D_B = 0.3 are given without units and without justification beyond "quantitatively consistent". Is the zigzag arrangement, or the boundary motion, sensitive to them? The authors should give units, sources and a brief sensitivity check. They should also give the bias magnitude and electrode geometry used in Fig. 2k.

11. **Quantities quoted inconsistently.**
    - The text gives "~1170–2000 nm after the seventh trailing field", while Fig. 2e shows 1170 nm.
    - The text gives "~928–1400 nm after ND6", but no 1400 nm displacement appears in Fig. 2.
    - The Discussion says "exceeding ~1700 nm".
    
    The authors should state which ranges come from which datasets.

12. **The PFM and STEM bias magnitudes differ.** The STEM writing used +17 V and the PFM writing ±5 V. The explanation, a different dielectric environment, is reasonable but qualitative. An estimate of the field in the PTO layer for each geometry would help.

13. **Fig. 1a caption.** The caption says that dark amplitude marks "the boundary of the vortex". It would be clearer to say that it marks walls between regions of opposite net in-plane polarization. LPFM amplitudes in pm should be described as uncalibrated, or the calibration given.

14. **Typos and units.** Examples are "piezoresponce", "hetrostructures", "-670 KHz" (should be ~670 kHz) and "0.9 J/cm2". The authors should also define the "-" prefix used for approximate values throughout, which reads like a minus sign.

15. **Data availability.** Data are available only "upon reasonable request". Depositing the PFM image stacks and the phase-field inputs would help reproducibility.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Add a systematic map of displacement per pass against tip bias, velocity and path offset from the boundary, with statistics across regions. This is the most direct way to justify "deterministic".
- Simulate a tip translated along the vortex tubes, with a physically motivated trailing field, to reproduce the reversal with tip direction in Fig. 3. Alternatively, narrow the mechanistic claim.
- Confirm the vortex phase in a region written by the AFM tip in the film, using cross-sectional STEM or local RSM, and add vector PFM to identify the polarization component that flips.
- Add a control film without the vortex phase, written under identical trailing-field conditions, to isolate what is specific to polar vortices.

5. Verdict: minor revision
