Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper reports STM/STS of pairs of in-gap states at a subset of atomic defects in the proposed excitonic insulator Ta2Pd3Te5 at 4.4 K. It assigns these defects to Pd atoms on Ta sites, which DFT finds carry a local charge dipole. It also argues that the in-gap states follow the excitonic gap when the gap is tuned by local strain and by tip-height (carrier-injection) control, and that a tight-binding "excitonic YSR" model with a dipole impurity reproduces the main features. If the identification holds, this would be the first real-space observation of impurity-bound states predicted for exciton condensates since Zittartz. That would be a notable result, and a local probe of excitonic order in a system whose excitonic character is still debated.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- Clear, high-quality STM/STS data. Five defect types (alpha, alpha', beta, beta', gamma) show reproducible pairs of filled- and empty-state in-gap resonances. Their spatial extent (~5–6 nm) is much larger than the atomic-scale defect (Fig. 2, Extended Data Figs. 3, 5, 6).
- A defect population census (Extended Data Fig. 1: 67 nontrivial, 308 trivial, 21 unidentified) and a matched "null" population: the majority Pd-vacancy defects show no in-gap state. This is a useful internal control.
- Two independent ways of reducing the gap: the strained stripe in Fig. 3a–d and tip-height tuning in Fig. 3e–g and Extended Data Figs. 7–8. In both, the in-gap states weaken or vanish when the gap collapses into a pseudogap, and the tip-height behaviour is repeated across all five defect types.
- DFT-simulated topographs are compared against several candidate defects (Extended Data Fig. 4), and a minimal model separates monopole from dipole impurities (single vs. pair of in-gap states).
- The idea is general and could seed work on impurity physics in other exciton condensates.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The paper does not distinguish an excitonic (pair-breaking) bound state from an ordinary defect level in a gapped semiconductor. The central claim is that the in-gap states arise from a YSR-like pair-breaking mechanism specific to the exciton condensate. Every experimental argument offered for this, however, would also hold for a conventional defect level in a band insulator:
   - A charged or dipolar impurity in any semiconductor binds donor-like and acceptor-like states split off the conduction and valence band edges.
   - Such states would also merge into the continuum when the gap collapses, as seen in the strained region (Fig. 3d) and in the tip-contact regime (Fig. 3e–g).
   - Their wavefunctions would also extend over several nanometres (effective-mass/hydrogenic states), well beyond the DFT defect orbital. The "6 nm vs <1 nm DFT" argument (p. 1553) therefore does not single out a many-body origin.
   - Their extent would also grow as the gap shrinks (Extended Data Fig. 9).

   The statement that the tip-height result "solidly confirms the excitonic origin" (p. 1554) is therefore stronger than the evidence supports. Two quantitative discriminants are available from data and calculations the authors already have:
   - (a) Scaling test. For a YSR-type state, the binding energy measured from the gap edge, Delta − |E±|, should scale with the gap Delta_EI at fixed impurity strength. A hydrogenic level sits at a roughly fixed binding energy below the band edge. Please plot (Delta_EI − |E±|)/Delta_EI and (Delta_EI − |E±|) against Delta_EI for each defect from Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 8, with uncertainties. Discuss which behaviour is observed, and whether the dipole-screening assumption (V0 reduced to 0.6 V0 in Fig. 4f) is needed to make the data consistent.
   - (b) Control calculation. Run the same Gaussian dipole potential (Methods, eq. 2) in a non-interacting band insulator with the same single-particle gap but no excitonic hybridization term Delta. That is, a gapped two-band model with the same band-edge masses. Show whether a pair of in-gap states also appears there, and how its energies and size depend on the gap.

   If the bound states exist only, or with a distinct scaling, when the gap is excitonic, the YSR analogy is substantiated. If not, the conclusion should be reframed: in-gap defect states whose behaviour is consistent with, but does not by itself prove, an excitonic mechanism.

B. Should address

2. Tip-induced band bending and defect charging could produce the in-gap features and their tip-height dependence. Ta2Pd3Te5 is gapped at 4.4 K, and the authors themselves invoke tip fields "on the order of 10 MV" (units are missing; see item 12) to dope the surface.
   - In gapped materials, tip-induced ionization of a defect level gives sharp peaks or dips in dI/dV and ring-like features in maps. Their bias position and spatial radius change with setpoint and tip height.
   - The growth of the in-gap-state footprint with tunnelling current in Extended Data Fig. 9 (30 → 200 pA), and the peak/dip line shapes at ΔZ = −0.2 to −0.3 nm in Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 8c, look like this kind of charging physics.
   - The abrupt, horizontal discontinuity at ΔZ ≈ −0.25 nm, at nearly the same height for all five defects and for the pristine area (Fig. 3e; Extended Data Figs. 7a, 8a), should also be explained. It could be a genuine transition, a tip change, or the onset of mechanical tip–sample interaction.

   Please show:
   - setpoint (bias and current) dependence of the in-gap peak energies at fixed tip–sample separation;
   - dI/dV line cuts across a defect over a wider bias range, to look for charging rings;
   - an estimate of the tip-induced band bending (for example from the shift of the gap edges relative to E_F, or from I(z)/apparent barrier height measurements);
   - whether the ΔZ sweeps are reversible (retract after approach) and repeatable with a different tip.

3. Defect identification and the dipole assignment, including inconsistencies in Extended Data Table 1. Extended Data Fig. 4 states that the Pd substitution, the Ta vacancy and the Ta–Pd interchange all reproduce the observed topography, so the assignment rests on formation energies and on the DFT charge distributions. Several problems need attention:
   - The table is internally hard to follow. Changing from the default to "Pd-rich/Ta-rich" alters only the Te-vacancy energy (2.455 → 1.655 eV), which contains neither Pd nor Ta. The Pd-vacancy energy is unchanged under Pd-rich conditions but becomes negative (−0.577 eV) under Te-rich/Ta-rich conditions. The chemical potentials, their reference phases, the defect charge states, the supercell size and the finite-size corrections are not given.
   - The growth mixture (Ta:Pd:Te = 2.0:4.5:7.5, Methods) is Pd- and Te-rich. In that column the Ta vacancy (0.148 eV) and Pd_Ta (−0.948 eV) are both favourable. A negative formation energy would imply a very high Pd_Ta concentration, which needs comment. On this table it is also unclear why Pd vacancies (77.8% of defects) dominate under Pd-rich growth.
   - Please give the charge/dipole analysis (as in Fig. 4b,d and Supplementary Fig. 12) for the Ta vacancy and the Ta–Pd interchange as well. According to the paper's own mechanism, any defect with a dipole should host in-gap pairs, so the exclusion of these candidates matters.

4. The monopole-vs-dipole prediction is not tested experimentally. The model predicts one in-gap state for a monopole and two for a dipole (Supplementary Fig. 13), which is the most specific prediction of the theory. Every in-gap defect reported shows a pair, and the defects without in-gap states are attributed to neutral Pd vacancies. Please state whether any defect with a single in-gap state was observed among the 21 "not identified" defects. An intentional monopole would be a strong test, for example an alkali adatom deposited at low coverage or a defect whose charge state can be switched by the tip. Without such a test, the dipole-specific part of the interpretation rests only on DFT.

5. Statistics of the in-gap energies are missing. Fig. 2 and Fig. 3g each show one representative defect per type. Please report:
   - the distribution of E+ and E− over many defects of each type (the census gives 67 nontrivial defects in one 80 × 80 nm² frame);
   - the spread between nominally identical defects;
   - whether the energies depend on the local environment, such as proximity to other defects or to the strained stripe;
   - how many cleaves, samples and tips the key spectra were reproduced on.

   This is needed to judge whether the small differences between α, α', β and β' (and the assignment of mirror pairs) are meaningful.

6. The strained-stripe argument rests on few defects and an indirect strain estimate. The strain value (ε ≈ 2.4%) is inferred from a 0.3 nm topographic corrugation (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Fig. 10), and the text says only "a few" nontrivial defects lie in the stripe. Please:
   - give the number of defects;
   - show how the strain was extracted from the atomic lattice (for example by Lawler–Fujita drift correction and a strain map), as opposed to the apparent height;
   - rule out the alternatives that the stripe is a subsurface stacking fault, a local change in carrier density, or a region where the defects' charge state differs.

   Absence of in-gap states there is consistent with the gap closing, but also with the defect being neutralized by extra carriers.

7. The excitonic character of the gap is assumed, not established. The paper takes the excitonic-insulator nature of Ta2Pd3Te5 from the literature (refs 33–36), and then states that the in-gap states "manifest the excitonic nature" of the phase (Conclusion). The second statement is circular unless concern 1 is resolved. The excitonic origin of the gap in Ta2Pd3Te5 is still debated against structural or hybridization (topological) gap scenarios. The discussion should acknowledge this and state what the in-gap states would look like if the gap were not excitonic.

8. There is no temperature dependence. All STM data are at 4.4 K, yet the abstract highlights condensates "persisting up to room temperature". Even a partial temperature series, for example 4.4 K and ~77 K, or as high as the instrument allows, showing the in-gap states following the gap would link them to the excitonic order more directly than tip-induced tuning. If this is not possible with the Joule–Thomson STM, please say so.

9. The model parameters are not connected to the material, and the comparison is qualitative. The 2D isotropic square-lattice model (t = 0.5 eV, μ = −1.5 eV, Δ = 50 meV, a = 0.25 nm, V0 = 0.24 eV) is not derived from the quasi-1D Dirac band structure of Ta2Pd3Te5 (CB1/VB1 crossing plus a spectator VB2). Please:
   - justify the parameter choices, including V0 and the Gaussian widths, and show how robust the in-gap pair is to them;
   - state whether the predicted state is anisotropic along and across the chains, as the data suggest (Extended Data Fig. 3);
   - give the Fermi velocity used in the 6.5 nm size estimate.

   The screening of the dipole to 0.6 V0 in Fig. 4f is introduced by hand to match the trend, and should be described as such.

C. Minor

10. The dipole moment "0.14 × 10⁻¹² C m for a Pd atom" (p. 1554) is many orders of magnitude too large. One elementary charge times 1 Å is about 1.6 × 10⁻²⁹ C m. Please correct the value or the units.
11. In Fig. 3g the y-axis is labelled "Bias (mV)" but spans ±0.10, so presumably volts. In the Fig. 2 caption, the filled- and empty-state energies both render as E′ ("E′ (top) and E′ (bottom)").
12. In Methods the tip field is given as "on the order of 10 MV" with no length unit. The claim that the excitonic Stark effect is "an order of magnitude smaller" than carrier doping needs a short derivation or reference.
13. Methods says the DFT lattice constants were "taken from experiments48". Ref. 48 is an STM edge-state paper. Ref. 37 (Tremel) seems intended.
14. Please reconcile the gap values: ~100 meV from ARPES at 80 K (text, Fig. 1d), the STS gap in Fig. 1e, and "about 140 to 60 meV" in the tip-height tuning at ΔZ = 0. State where on the ΔZ axis the normal STS condition sits relative to the 140 meV value.
15. The STS acquisition details are missing: lock-in modulation amplitude and frequency, setpoint for the spectra and maps, and normalization procedure ("normalized dI/dV" in Figs. 2–3). These matter for resolving peaks only ~20–40 meV from the gap edges.
16. The pseudogap interpretation ("preformed excitons", p. 1553) in the strained stripe and the tip-contact regime is speculative. It should be labelled as one possibility. The unexplained in-gap state of α′ inside the pseudogap (Extended Data Fig. 8 caption) deserves a sentence in the main text.
17. The assignment of γ to a subsurface defect is based only on weaker contrast and intensity (Extended Data Fig. 6). Please flag it as tentative or support it with DFT for a second-layer Pd_Ta.
18. The asymmetry argument (Fig. 4g) introduces a different, multi-lobed charge distribution and unequal hoppings t_c = 1.0, t_v = 0.5 eV, while eq. (1) uses a single t. Please state clearly which asymmetry source is needed to reproduce Fig. 3e,g.

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Add the Delta-scaling analysis of the bound-state energies and the non-excitonic band-insulator control calculation (concern 1). Together these would turn a qualitative analogy into a discriminating test.
- Add setpoint-dependence data and reversibility checks for the tip-height sweeps, to exclude tip-induced charging and tip changes (concern 2).
- Test the monopole prediction with a deliberately introduced charged adsorbate or a tip-switchable defect charge state (concern 4).
- Report statistics of the in-gap energies over many defects and samples, and clean up the defect formation-energy table with its chemical potentials and charge states (concerns 3, 5).

5. Verdict

Verdict: major revision
