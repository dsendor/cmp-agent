Review outline:

**1. Significance and novelty**

The authors image the time-resolved expansion of an indirect-exciton (IX) cloud in an H-stacked MoSe2/WSe2 heterostructure. In a window of low temperature (T ≲ 10 K) and intermediate excitation power (P_ex ~ 0.1–1 mW), they report an effective diffusivity D ~ 10^3 cm²/s. That is two to four orders of magnitude above earlier time-resolved values for TMD heterostructures (0.1–2 cm²/s). They argue that the expansion is better described by a ballistic (constant-velocity) model than by diffusion, and that this is consistent with Bose–Hubbard superfluidity in the moiré lattice. If the result holds, it would be important. It would give a direct kinetic basis for the long-range cw IX transport the same group reported earlier, and it would be experimental input on exciton superfluidity in moiré lattices. As presented, however, the transport analysis cannot tell the proposed physics apart from several conventional explanations, and the superfluidity connection rests on order-of-magnitude estimates.

**2. Potential reasons for acceptance**

- Direct time-resolved imaging of IX cloud expansion (APD, ~0.1 ns, ~1 µm resolution) is a real step beyond cw decay-length measurements, which cannot by themselves separate transport from lifetime effects.
- The data set is systematic in temperature and excitation power (Figs. 3, 4, S3, S6). The efficient-transport regime is a bounded region of the (T, P_ex) plane, not a single observation, and its nonmonotonic power dependence is a distinctive feature.
- The lifetime τ(P_ex, T) is measured independently and used in the simulations (Fig. S3). The robustness of the fits to the fitting window is checked (0–3 ns against 0–5 ns, Figs. S5, S6).
- The effect is reported to survive repeated thermal cycling to room temperature (SI, Optical measurements).
- The topic is timely, and the link to earlier cw observations of long-range IX transport and spin transport in the same sample is valuable.

**3. Concerns**

**A. Must address**

1. **The diffusive model used as the null hypothesis leaves out interaction-driven (nonlinear) transport, which the authors themselves invoke.**
   - *Where:* Eq. 1 uses a constant, density-independent D. The comparisons in Fig. 2a, Fig. 5 and the "Comparison with diffusive transport" paragraph (p. 3–4) all use it. Yet the SI ("Estimates of IX mean free time…") writes D = D0(1 + n u0/k_BT).
   - *Why it matters:* With u0·n ≈ δE ≈ 3 meV and k_BT ≈ 0.15 meV at 1.7 K, this expression gives a ~20-fold enhancement at 1.7 K, falling to ~2 at 30 K.
     - Classical dipolar-repulsion-driven expansion therefore predicts an effective diffusivity that *decreases* with temperature (Fig. 3a) and grows with density.
     - When the lifetime shortens and the density saturates at high P_ex, it can also turn over (Fig. 3b; τ drops sharply with P_ex in Fig. S3).
     - Nonlinear diffusion with D ∝ n also produces sharp-fronted profiles whose width grows faster than √t, closer to linear, which a constant-velocity model would fit better.
     - So the main argument that the transport "is inconsistent with diffusive transport" (p. 4) and that its T dependence rules out diffusion is not established. The χ² preference for Eq. 2 over Eq. 1 may only show that constant-D diffusion is the wrong diffusive model.
   - *What to do:* Simulate ∂n/∂t = ∇[D0(1 + u0 n/k_BT)∇n] + Λ − n/τ(n). Use the measured τ(P_ex, T) and the calibrated δE(n), with D0 as the only free parameter (or D0 fixed by the low-density data). Compare it with the measured I(x,t) and d_{1/e}(t) across the full (T, P_ex) map. Only if this model also fails can the claim of non-diffusive transport be made.

2. **The observable d_{1/e}(t) is ill-defined in the efficient regime, and neither model reproduces its time dependence.**
   - *Where:* d_{1/e} comes from an exponential fit to I(x) over x = 0–9 µm, the region from the excitation spot to the HS edge (Fig. 1 caption).
   - *Why it matters:*
     - In the efficient regime d_{1/e} reaches ~22 µm (Fig. 1b–d). That is more than twice the fit window and comparable to or larger than the MoSe2 monolayer extent (~20 µm, SI). The profile at 2.5–11 ns in Fig. 1a is almost flat over the window. The decay length is then fixed by a small residual slope and is very sensitive to:
       - noise and background subtraction;
       - signal pile-up or reflection at the HS edge;
       - spatial variation of the IX emission efficiency.
     - The simulations treat the system as one-dimensional (p. 2), but neither the boundary condition at the HS edge (reflecting, absorbing, open) nor the lateral width of the HS is stated. Expansion along y and reflection at x ≈ 9 µm would both change d_{1/e} directly.
     - At 1.7 K and 0.5 mW, d_{1/e}(t) visibly *accelerates* (convex; Figs. 1b, 2). Neither the √t-like diffusive curve nor the linear ballistic curve captures this. Fig. 2b underestimates the last points by ~5 µm, and so does Fig. S5b for t > 2 ns. Accelerating growth is hard to reconcile with ballistic propagation at constant v. It suggests a boundary effect, a changing emission efficiency, or a density/temperature-dependent process.
   - *What to do:*
     - State the boundary conditions, the HS width and the fitting procedure.
     - Fit the full I(x,t) maps rather than one derived length, or use a moment-based width (mean-square displacement) restricted to times before the cloud reaches the edge.
     - Show the residuals.
     - Show 2D (x, y) images, or at least y-profiles, to support the 1D approximation.

3. **The link to Bose–Hubbard superfluidity is not supported by evidence specific to a superfluid.**
   - *Where:* "Comparison of IX transport with BH theory" (p. 4), abstract and summary.
   - *Why it matters:* The case rests on four points.
     - **(i) Ballistic-like transport.** Hot, interaction-driven or phonon-wind-driven classical transport would also produce it, and it is not unique to superfluids.
     - **(ii) Filling estimate.** N ~ 1/2 comes from the mean-field capacitor formula. That formula is known to underestimate density, by factors of several in coupled quantum wells, because of correlations. It also uses an assumed ε ≈ 7.4, and δE is not shown as a function of P_ex. The uncertainty in N spans the whole range 0 < N < 1, so the "superfluid at N ~ 1/2, insulator at N ~ 1" assignment is not quantitative.
     - **(iii) Circular T_c argument.** J ~ 1.6 K is obtained *from* the observed 10 K vanishing temperature through T_c ~ 4πNJ. The moiré amplitude is then inferred from J, so "agreement" with the literature moiré depth is not independent.
     - **(iv) Disorder versus hopping.** The authors stress that the disorder (~10–20 meV from linewidth) far exceeds both k_BT and the interaction energy. The same disorder exceeds J ≈ 0.14 meV by two orders of magnitude, a regime where Bose–Hubbard theory predicts a Bose glass rather than a superfluid. The paper uses the strong disorder to argue against diffusion while ignoring its consequence for the BH phase diagram.
   - *What to do:* Either provide evidence specific to a superfluid, or reduce the claim to "anomalously fast, non-diffusive transport" and remove "superfluid" from the abstract and conclusions. Evidence specific to a superfluid could be:
     - first-order spatial coherence of the IX emission (shift-interferometry);
     - a density-calibrated filling, for example from an independent n calibration via reflectance or doping-dependent spectra;
     - an analysis showing why J ≫ disorder effectively holds in this sample.

4. **Extrinsic mechanisms for an apparently fast-spreading IX signal are not ruled out.**
   - *Where:* Optical measurements (SI) and the d_{1/e} analysis.
   - *Why it matters:* The spectral selection is a long-pass filter at 850 nm, integrating all emission. Excitation is resonant with the WSe2 DX at powers up to 4 mW. Several conventional effects could produce a growing PL footprint without IX transport:
     - **(a) Hot excitons.** Hot IXs from excess energy and laser heating of the lattice would make the effective temperature in the bright regime unknown, and hot-exciton transport can be fast and quasi-ballistic at early times.
     - **(b) Laser and PL tails.** Laser light scattered or guided in the ~70 nm hBN/TMD stack would generate IXs far from the spot. Guided or reabsorbed PL would do the same.
     - **(c) DX transport before conversion.** DX or electron/hole transport in the WSe2 or MoSe2 layers, or in monolayer regions, could precede IX formation.
     - **(d) Time-dependent emission profile.** Center emission saturates through exciton–exciton annihilation while the periphery keeps rising; this flattens the profile without carriers moving.

     The DX profile is shown only down to ~10^-2 of its peak. A tail of the generation at 10^-2 to 10^-3, combined with saturation of emission at the center, could mimic a large d_{1/e}.
   - *What to do:*
     - Show the laser (reflected) profile and the DX profile on a log scale down to the noise floor, under the same conditions.
     - Image the PL spectrally resolved, at least checking that the expanded signal has the IX spectrum and the same peak energy as at the spot.
     - Report an IX temperature, from the lineshape or the high-energy tail, against P_ex and against distance.
     - Vary the excitation energy (for example, resonant with the MoSe2 DX, or below the WSe2 DX).
     - Show the expansion with the spot placed far from the HS edge and at different positions, to test geometry-related light propagation.

**B. Should address**

5. **The χ² statistic is not a proper goodness-of-fit measure, and no uncertainties are given on D, v or d_{1/e}.**
   - *Where:* χ² = Σ(m_t − s_t)²/(ν s_t) (p. 3, Fig. 5).
   - *Why it matters:* This χ² has units of length, contains no measurement uncertainty, and weights points by the simulated value rather than the variance. Its absolute size (0–1.5) therefore has no meaning, and "large" against "small" cannot be judged. Two one-parameter models compared this way only show which curve is closer, not whether either is acceptable. None of Figs. 1–4 has error bars.
   - *What to do:*
     - Report uncertainties on d_{1/e}, from the fit covariance or from repeated scans.
     - Use a variance-weighted χ² with the reduced χ² per degree of freedom, and give confidence intervals for D and v.
     - Show that the difference between models is statistically significant, given that neither fits within error (concern 2).

6. **Single sample and single excitation geometry.**
   - *Where:* The SI says the effect was realized in one sample, and the data appear to come from one excitation position near the HS edge.
   - *Why it matters:* The lack of other samples is acknowledged, but position dependence within this sample is not shown. Exciting at several locations, including where the HS extends on both sides of the spot, would show whether the effect is a property of the HS or of one region or edge. Expansion toward −x and +x should be compared. The profiles at x < 0 in Fig. 1a fall quickly, so it is unclear what lies there.
   - *What to do:* Map the effect over several spots. Show whether the expansion is symmetric where the geometry allows it.

7. **The density, filling and twist-angle calibrations are not given with uncertainties.**
   - *Where:* p. 4 and SI, Heterostructure.
   - *Why it matters:*
     - δE ≈ 3 meV is quoted for "the efficient IX transport regime", but no δE(P_ex) or δE(t) is shown, even though the density changes by an order of magnitude across Fig. 3b and during the pulse.
     - The twist angle δθ = 1.1° comes from optical edge alignment, which typically carries ~1° uncertainty, so b = 17 nm is uncertain by up to a factor of ~2. Its relation to the H stacking (deviation from 60°) and the role of the 0.2% lattice mismatch in the moiré period are not discussed.
     - The authors avoid SHG over damage concerns. Without an independent angle measurement, N could be several times smaller or larger than 1/2.
   - *What to do:*
     - Show the IX energy shift against P_ex and against time, with the derived n and N on a secondary axis of Fig. 3b, including the uncertainty from b and from the capacitor formula.
     - Consider SHG on a non-critical region, or after the transport measurements.

8. **The temperature and power dependences and the 2D maps need support.**
   - *Where:* Fig. 3a, Fig. 3c/4c, Eq. 1 and SI.
   - *Why it matters:*
     - Fig. 3a has a sharp minimum (D ≈ 10 cm²/s) at a single temperature near 30 K, followed by a recovery at 40–50 K. This feature is not discussed and may reflect the fit's sensitivity rather than physics.
     - The color maps in Figs. 3c/4c are interpolated from a sparse grid. The measured points should be marked, and the white regions explained (not measured? fit failed?).
     - The simulations use a constant τ and D in time, although the SI notes that the density changes during the pulse. In the efficient regime τ is ~3 ns, comparable to the 5 ns window, so how τ(n(t)) is handled strongly affects the extracted D.
   - *What to do:* Mark the data points on the maps and explain the white regions. Show the raw d_{1/e}(t) curves at 20, 30 and 40 K. Test how sensitive D is to using τ(n) instead of a constant τ.

9. **The mean free path estimate is internally inconsistent with the ballistic interpretation.**
   - *Where:* SI, "Estimates of IX mean free time and mean free path".
   - *Why it matters:*
     - τ_m ≈ 0.2 ns and l_m ≈ 2 µm are obtained from classical diffusive formulas with the IX mass "~ free electron mass" (the computed MoSe2/WSe2 IX mass is closer to ~1.0–1.3 m0). The velocity used in l_m = vτ_m is not specified.
     - l_m ≈ 2 µm is much smaller than the ~20 µm expansion, so the transport is *not* ballistic over the measured range by the authors' own estimate. This conflicts with the claim that "IXs move with no scattering over the range ~ l_m" and indicate "long-range ballistic transport" (p. 3).
     - The fitted v ≈ 10^6 cm/s at 1.7 K is about twice the thermal velocity √(k_BT/m) ≈ 5×10^5 cm/s, and it decreases with T while the thermal velocity grows.
   - *What to do:* Compare v(T) with the thermal velocity and with the dipolar drift velocity expected from the density gradient. Reconcile l_m with the transport distance.

10. **The comparison with literature diffusivities is made at mismatched conditions.**
    - *Where:* p. 2–3, "D up to ~0.1 cm²/s in Refs. [52, 53] and ~2 cm²/s in Ref. [55]".
    - *Why it matters:* Those values come from different temperatures, densities, stackings and twist angles, and some come from microscopic methods with different time windows. The "orders of magnitude" headline depends on comparing like with like.
    - *What to do:* Tabulate the literature values with their T, density and stacking. Show this sample's D under comparable conditions (e.g. low P_ex, 1.7 K: D ~ 15 cm²/s per Fig. 3b), which is itself already above several literature values. Discuss Ref. [63] (imaging of IX superfluidity) and Ref. [62] (extended coherence) explicitly.

11. **The generation term Λ(x) and the IX formation dynamics.**
    - *Where:* Eqs. 1–2 take Λ(x) as the DX PL profile in WSe2, assumed close to the laser profile.
    - *Why it matters:* IX formation involves charge transfer and possible DX/carrier motion before transfer. At high P_ex, DX diffusion or exciton–exciton annihilation broadens the effective generation profile. The early-time offset of d_{1/e} (~3–4 µm at t ≈ 0) and its power dependence (Fig. 1d at 4 mW starts higher) suggest the effective source width depends on power.
    - *What to do:* Measure the DX profile at each P_ex and use it as Λ. Report the fitted d_{1/e}(t = 0) against power.

**C. Minor**

12. Eq. 2, ∂n/∂t = −v∇n, describes one-directional drift, not isotropic ballistic expansion. State how it is implemented, for example two counter-propagating populations or v·sign(x), and why a Crank–Nicolson diffusion solver (SI ref. [7]) is appropriate for the advection equation.
13. The Fig. S7 caption gives P_ex = 5, 50 and 4000 µW, but the panel labels read 5, 500 and 4000 µW.
14. The main text says the disorder amplitude is "~10 meV, estimated from the PL linewidth". The SI gives ~14 meV (lowest density) and ~20 meV (efficient regime). Harmonize, and explain why the linewidth, which may be inhomogeneous or include moiré-subband structure, measures the disorder amplitude.
15. The pulse edges are "0.3 ns edge sharpness" in the main text but "0.1 ns rise, 0.3 ns fall" in the SI.
16. Fig. 2 compares 500 µW and 25 µW, but Fig. 1c shows 5, 50, 250 and 500 µW. Show the 25 µW d_{1/e}(t) data, or use a power shown in Fig. 1c.
17. The Fig. 1a normalization (profiles normalized at each t, or absolute?) and the background subtraction are not stated. Also, profiles at 11 ns (after the excitation pulse ends? the pulse is 80 ns) are shown in Fig. 1a while the fits use 0–5 ns. Clarify which times enter the analysis.
18. The choice of ε ≈ 7.4 (hBN out-of-plane) for the capacitor formula should be justified, given the TMD layers between the charges.
19. "Superfluid" appears in the abstract and conclusion as "consistent with"; until the specific evidence of concern 3 exists, suggest wording such as "non-diffusive, anomalously fast transport whose T and n dependence is qualitatively reminiscent of BH predictions".
20. Several literature references (e.g. [21], [63]) are not discussed although they bear directly on the claims; Ref. [27] is cited for the binding energy and the superfluidity temperature but not for its predicted density range.

**4. Suggestions for improvement**

1. Replace the d_{1/e}-based χ² comparison with a global fit of the full I(x,t) data, with uncertainties. Make interaction-driven nonlinear diffusion (with measured τ(n) and δE(n)) the main competing model, alongside constant-D diffusion and ballistic transport.
2. Add spectrally resolved transport imaging and an IX effective temperature against P_ex. Show the excitation and DX profiles on a log scale to the noise floor, to exclude hot-exciton and light-propagation artifacts.
3. Add a coherence measurement (shift-interferometric first-order coherence) in and out of the efficient-transport region of the (T, P_ex) map. This would be the most direct way to support the superfluidity connection.
4. Measure several excitation positions in this sample, and ideally a second sample, even if it shows only a weaker version of the effect. Mark the measured points on the (T, P_ex) maps.
