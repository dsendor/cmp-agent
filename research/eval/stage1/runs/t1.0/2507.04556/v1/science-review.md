**A. Must address**

1. **Interaction-driven (dipolar-repulsion) expansion and nonlinear losses are not modelled, and the authors' own numbers imply they are large.** Eq. 1 uses a constant, density-independent D and a linear loss n/τ. That constant-D model is the null hypothesis in Fig. 2a, Fig. 5 and the "Comparison with diffusive transport" paragraph (p. 3–4). Yet the SI section "Estimates of IX mean free time and mean free path" writes D = D0(1 + n·u0/k_BT), and the main text gives δE = n·u0 ≈ 3 meV in the efficient-transport regime. At 1.7 K, k_BT ≈ 0.15 meV, so the enhancement factor is ≈ 20, falling to ≈ 2 at 30 K. Classical dipolar-pressure expansion therefore predicts an apparent D that is large at low T, falls with increasing T and grows with density: exactly the trends the paper (p. 3–4) cites as evidence against diffusion. The rise of D with P_ex up to about 0.5 mW (Fig. 3b) follows the same pattern. The turnover at higher power needs no superfluid either: there the lifetime shortens sharply (Fig. S3), the density saturates, new species appear (Fig. S7), and heating may set in. A pressure-driven dipolar gas with n·u0 ≈ 3 meV and m ≈ m0 has an expansion speed c ≈ (n·u0/m)^1/2 ≈ 2×10^6 cm/s, the same order as the fitted "ballistic" v ≈ 7×10^5–10^6 cm/s (Fig. 4, Fig. S4). Nonlinear diffusion with D ∝ n gives sharp-fronted profiles whose width grows nearly linearly in time, so a constant-velocity model would fit it better than constant-D diffusion. The χ² preference for Eq. 2 may therefore only show that constant-D diffusion is the wrong diffusive model. In addition, exciton–exciton annihilation and density-dependent recombination saturate emission at the centre while the periphery keeps rising. This flattens the profile and increases the apparent 1/e width with no transport at all, an effect documented in TMD halo and expansion studies (e.g. Ref. 38). The authors acknowledge the constant-D simplification as a "subject of future works", but the anomalous D is the central result and cannot be separated from these effects without modelling them. The authors should simulate ∂n/∂t = ∇[D0(1 + u0 n/k_BT)∇n] + Λ − n/τ(n) − γn², using the calibrated δE(n), the measured τ(P_ex, T), and γ fixed from the power dependence of the spatially integrated decay, with D0 the only free parameter (or D0 fixed from the low-density data). They should fit this model to the measured I(x,t) and d_1/e(t) across the full (T, P_ex) map and show whether a modest, T-independent D0 (such as the 10–30 cm²/s seen at 30–50 K) reproduces Figs. 1–3. They should also check that the spatially integrated PL scales linearly with power in the efficient regime. Until this is done, "anomalously high diffusivity" and "ballistic" are not demonstrated, and the constant-D quantity from Eq. 1 is only an effective spreading rate.

2. **The d_1/e observable is extracted in a small, bounded sample, and the sample boundaries are not in the model.** d_1/e comes from an exponential fit over x = 0–9 µm, "from the excitation spot to the HS edge" (Fig. 1 caption), with the HS edge at about x = 9–10 µm (Fig. S1b). Yet the values in Figs. 1b–d and 2 reach 20–22 µm, more than twice the fit window and comparable to or larger than the MoSe2 extent (~20 µm, SI). When the fit window is shorter than the fitted decay length, d_1/e mainly measures how flat the profile is inside a finite box, and it is set by a small residual slope that is very sensitive to noise, background subtraction, pile-up or reflection of IXs at a reflecting edge, losses at an absorbing edge, and spatial variation of the emission efficiency. The latest profiles in Fig. 1a (2.5–11 ns) are nearly flat over the window, with an upturn at x ≈ 9–11 µm, and emission extends to x ≈ 12 µm, possibly from regions outside the MoSe2/WSe2 overlap. The SI says only the t = 0–3 ns window stays "within the HS area", yet the main-text D and v (Figs. 3, 4) come from 0–5 ns fits that include points beyond it. The simulations are one-dimensional, but their spatial domain and the boundary condition at the HS edge (reflecting, absorbing, open) are not stated, nor whether the simulated d_1/e is extracted with the same 0–9 µm window and fit procedure as the data. The HS is only ~10 µm wide in y (Fig. S1b), comparable to the cloud size, so the expansion is 2D for the first ~1–2 ns and a 1D model can overestimate D by up to a factor of ~2 at early times; the lateral edges also matter. The position of the excitation spot relative to every HS edge is not given, and the HS edge is not marked on Fig. 1a (in Fig. S1b the MoSe2 boundary at y = 0 appears to lie nearer x ≈ 12–15 µm). The authors should state the domain, boundary conditions, HS width and fit procedure; include the real HS geometry and spot position in the simulations (2D, or at least 1D with the measured edge position) and show how sensitive the results are to the boundary condition; show 2D (x, y) images or y-profiles to justify the 1D approximation; fit the full I(x,t) maps rather than one scalar, with residuals; report D and v only from the window before the cloud reaches the edges; and add a model-independent observable, such as the arrival time (rise delay or half-rise time) of I(t) at fixed x versus x, which is linear with slope 1/v for a ballistic or drift front, quadratic for diffusion, and flat for waveguided emission.

3. **The comparison of diffusive and ballistic models does not support "agrees with long-range ballistic transport".** First, Eq. 2, ∂n/∂t = −v∇n, is a one-directional advection equation: in 1D it translates the whole profile in +x at speed v rather than spreading it symmetrically from a source. Fig. S4b shows the "ballistic" simulation shifting the profile bodily to +x, while elsewhere a profile extends to both sides, so it is unclear what was actually solved, how Eq. 2 is applied for x < 0, and why a Crank–Nicolson diffusion solver (SI ref. [7]) suits an advection equation. A constant drift away from the spot is what a force produces (dipolar pressure, Concern 1; a strain or potential gradient towards the HS edge; phonon wind) and does not require the absence of scattering. Isotropic ballistic motion needs counter-propagating ±v populations (or v·sgn(x)) or a distribution of velocities. Second, the χ² in Fig. 5 is Σ(m − s)²/(ν s): a Pearson-type normalisation by the model value with no measurement uncertainty, units of length, and ν counting time points rather than degrees of freedom. No error bars on d_1/e appear in Figs. 1–4. Its absolute values (0–1.5) have no statistical meaning; χ²_D and χ²_v are comparable (~0.1) at most settings (Fig. 5a,b), the distinction rests on a few points, and χ²_v = 0.35–0.5 at 1.7 and 6 K is not small. Third, neither model fits: at 1.7 K and 500 µW, d_1/e(t) bends upward after ~3 ns (Figs. 1b, 2). Neither the √t-like diffusive curve nor the linear ballistic curve follows it; Fig. 2b and Fig. S5b (t > 2 ns) undershoot the last points by ~5 µm. Accelerating growth is hard to reconcile with constant-velocity propagation and points instead to edge effects (Concern 2), a changing emission efficiency or a profile flattened by annihilation (Concern 1). The authors should state exactly which ballistic model was solved; compare the data with a genuinely ballistic kinetic model (e.g. Boltzmann/free-flight with an isotropic velocity distribution, the measured τ and a finite mean free path as a parameter); give uncertainties on d_1/e from the fit covariance and repeated scans and use them in a variance-weighted reduced χ² or likelihood comparison with confidence intervals and residual plots; match the number of free parameters (each model also allowing a free offset and time zero); analyse the mean-squared displacement σ²(t), which grows as t² for ballistic and t for diffusive transport; and say what physical process produces the superlinear d_1/e(t).

4. **The PL footprint is used as the density profile, and heating, hot-exciton, light-propagation and species effects are not excluded.** d_1/e comes from spectrally integrated PL through an 850 nm long-pass filter, so I(x,t) ∝ n(x,t) is assumed. Excitation is resonant with the WSe2 DX at 1.689 eV, ~300 meV above the IX (~1.39 eV), at up to several mW focused to a spot a few µm across; the spot size and power density are not given. The local lattice and exciton temperatures near the spot can then be far above the 1.7 K bath. Hot IXs leave the light cone and emit less, so emission efficiency varies with position and time and can flatten or even dip the profile at the centre (the "inner ring" physics of Ref. 9). Hot-exciton transport can be fast and quasi-ballistic at early times, and phonon wind and hot-carrier halos produce fast, directional exciton expansion in TMDs (e.g. Ref. 38). The efficient regime appears only at the lowest bath temperatures and intermediate powers, and is fastest in the first few ns of the 80 ns pulse, both compatible with transient hot-IX or phonon effects. Laser light scattered or guided in the ~70 nm hBN/TMD stack, or guided and reabsorbed PL scattered out at layer edges, would create emission far from the spot; the DX profile is shown only down to ~10^-2 of its peak, and a generation tail at 10^-2–10^-3 combined with centre saturation could mimic a large d_1/e. Finally, Fig. S7 shows the spectrum changing with power (narrow localized lines at low power, a higher-energy "double-occupancy" line at high power), so different species may dominate at different positions and times; changes in d_1/e could reflect the emitting species or radiative efficiency rather than transport, and the near-flat long-time profiles are especially sensitive to this. The authors should give the spot size, peak power density and WSe2 absorption, and estimate the local lattice heating at 0.5 mW; show the laser (reflected) and DX profiles on a log scale to the noise floor under the same conditions; measure spectrally resolved (or band-filtered) profiles so that the IX energy shift (a density proxy), linewidth and high-energy tail (temperature proxies) and the identity of the expanding species can be followed in x and t, and the expanded signal confirmed to have the same IX spectrum as at the spot; and test whether the fast expansion persists at matched IX density with lower excess energy (resonant with the MoSe2 DX, or near the IX), or with shorter pulses or lower duty cycle.

5. **The Bose–Hubbard superfluid interpretation is not independently tested and is in tension with the paper's own numbers.** The abstract and conclusions state consistency with BH superfluidity ("Comparison of IX transport with BH theory", p. 4). Ballistic, scattering-free transport is not specific to a superfluid; hot, interaction-driven or phonon-driven classical transport would also produce it, and no superfluid-specific signature is shown: no first-order spatial or temporal coherence (e.g. g^(1) from shift-interferometry, as this group has done in GaAs), no critical velocity, no vortices, no sharp onset at T_c. In Fig. 3a D varies smoothly from 1.7 to ~10 K. The T_c argument is circular: J ~ 1.6 K ≈ 0.14 meV is inferred from the observed ~10 K scale via T_c ~ 4πNJ, the moiré amplitude is inferred from J, and "agreement" with literature moiré depths follows by construction. The disorder (~10 meV main text; 14–20 meV linewidths in the SI) exceeds J by about two orders of magnitude, a regime where the disordered BH model predicts a Bose glass, not a superfluid; the paper uses the strong disorder to argue against diffusion while ignoring its consequence for the BH phase diagram, and the SI's percolation argument (superfluid puddles growing as disorder falls) is qualitative. For J ≈ 0.14 meV and b = 17 nm, the maximum band group velocity ~2Jb/ħ ≈ 7×10^5 cm/s, yet the fitted v reaches 10^6 cm/s. At a near-60° (H-stacked) 1.1° twist, atomic reconstruction into domains is expected (the SI acknowledges reconstruction), which may invalidate a rigid moiré BH description. The authors should either supply superfluid-specific evidence (coherence measurements in and out of the efficient-transport region; a quantitative disordered-BH phase diagram using the measured disorder; the expected v for the inferred J; an independent estimate of the moiré amplitude and J; D plotted against filling with propagated error bands), or remove "superfluid" from the abstract and conclusions and reword to, e.g., "non-diffusive, anomalously fast transport whose T and n dependence is qualitatively reminiscent of BH predictions", moving the BH discussion to an outlook.

**B. Should address**

6. **The mean-free-path estimate contradicts the "long-range ballistic" statement.** The main text (p. 3) argues that the high D implies anomalously high τ_m and l_m and states that IXs "move with no scattering over the range ~l_m", indicating "long-range ballistic transport". The SI, however, obtains τ_m ≈ 0.2 ns and l_m ≈ 2 µm from classical diffusive relations (which it says describe a "classical diffusive transport regime"), after dividing D by the interaction factor (1 + n·u0/k_BT) ≈ 20, and with an IX mass "~ free electron mass" where the computed MoSe2/WSe2 IX mass is ~1.0–1.3 m0; the velocity used in l_m = vτ_m is not specified. An l_m of ~2 µm is about ten times smaller than the ~20 µm expansion, so by the authors' own estimate the transport is diffusive over the measured range, and the division by the interaction factor supports interaction-enhanced diffusion (Concern 1) rather than ballistic motion. The fitted v ≈ 10^6 cm/s at 1.7 K is also about twice the thermal velocity √(k_BT/m) ≈ 5×10^5 cm/s, and decreases with T while the thermal velocity grows. The authors should reconcile l_m with the transport distance or withdraw the ballistic interpretation, give the numbers and the classical-formula caveat in the main text, and compare v(T) with the thermal velocity and with the dipolar drift velocity expected from the density gradient.

7. **The density, filling and twist-angle calibrations lack uncertainties.** N ~ 1/2 rests on the mean-field plate-capacitor density from δE ≈ 3 meV, which underestimates n by factors of several in correlated dipolar liquids (as in coupled quantum wells) and is questionable in a moiré lattice with strong on-site repulsion; it also uses ε ≈ 7.4 (hBN out-of-plane) without justification, given the TMD layers between the charges. δE ≈ 3 meV is not tied to a specific time, position or spectral feature in Fig. S7, and no δE(P_ex) or δE(t) is shown, although the density changes by an order of magnitude across Fig. 3b and in space and time during the pulse, so assigning one N to "the efficient regime" is not meaningful. b = 17 nm follows from δθ = 1.1° estimated from flake edges, typically uncertain by ±0.5–1°; with b ∝ (δθ)^-1 this changes N by a factor of ~2–4. SHG was deliberately not done, and the role of the 0.2% lattice mismatch in the moiré period is not discussed. The uncertainty in N plausibly spans the whole range 0 < N < 1, so the "superfluid at N ~ 1/2, insulator at N ~ 1" assignment is not quantitative. The authors should show the IX energy shift against P_ex and against time, with derived n and N on a secondary axis of Fig. 3b including the uncertainty from b and from the capacitor formula; give an uncertainty on δθ and N; specify where and when δE was measured; consider SHG on a non-critical region or after the transport measurements, or an independent density calibration (reflectance, doping-dependent spectra); and acknowledge the range of N compatible with the data.

8. **The conclusion rests on one sample, apparently one excitation position, and one direction of expansion.** The SI states that efficient transport "was realized in one sample" and attributes its absence elsewhere to disorder, though this sample's linewidth (14–20 meV) is not narrow. Only expansion from x = 0 towards the +x edge is analysed; profiles at x < 0 in Fig. 1a fall quickly and it is unclear what lies there. A strain or thickness gradient, a bubble, the graphene electrode remnant near x = 2 µm, or the flake boundaries could create a built-in drift towards one edge, which is exactly what Eq. 2 describes. 10. **The source term and lifetime inputs need justification.** Λ(x) in Eqs. 1–2 is taken as the WSe2 DX PL profile, assumed close to the laser profile, but it already includes DX diffusion before charge transfer, and the laser spot size is not given. IX formation is assumed local and instantaneous, whereas IXs may form after DX or electron/hole transport in the WSe2 or MoSe2 monolayers, which extend beyond the overlap (edges at about 30 against 20 µm). At high P_ex, DX diffusion or annihilation broadens the effective generation profile; the early-time offset of d_1/e (~3–4 µm at t ≈ 0) and its power dependence (Fig. 1d at 4 mW starts higher) suggest the effective source width depends on power. τ is the "initial decay time" after the pulse ends (Fig. S3) but is used as a constant during the pulse, when the decay is generally non-exponential and τ depends on local density; in the efficient regime τ ≈ 3 ns, comparable to the 5 ns window, so this choice strongly affects D. The authors should state the laser spot profile, measure the DX profile at each P_ex and use it as Λ, show that results do not depend on the choice of Λ (laser against DX profile), report fitted d_1/e(t = 0) against power, and test the sensitivity of D to a position- and density-dependent τ(n) or the nonlinear loss term of Concern 1.
