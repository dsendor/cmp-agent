Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors use time-resolved PL imaging to measure how the indirect-exciton (IX) cloud expands in a MoSe2/WSe2 heterostructure. In a window of low temperature (below about 10 K) and intermediate excitation power, they report fast growth of the cloud's 1/e decay distance d_1/e: nearly linear in time, reaching about 20 µm within 5 ns. Fitting this growth with diffusion, ballistic and drift-diffusion models gives "anomalously high" IX diffusivity, velocity and mobility (D up to about 10^3 cm²/s, µ about 10^4 cm²/eVs), even though disorder is substantial (PL linewidth about 10 meV). The authors argue that the high mobility, its disappearance with temperature and its non-monotonic density dependence are qualitatively consistent with IX superfluidity in a Bose–Hubbard picture. Direct kinetics measurements of IX transport in TMD heterostructures are valuable, and a robust, fast transport regime would be significant. However, the evidence that the regime is intrinsic is not yet strong, and the link to superfluidity is weaker still.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- Measuring the transport kinetics d_1/e(t) directly, rather than steady-state cw profiles, is the right way to tell diffusive from drift-like or ballistic expansion, and the paper explains this point clearly.
- The temperature and power dependence is mapped systematically (Fig. 3, three models, roughly 2–40 K and 5 µW–4 mW). This gives a phase-diagram-like picture of where fast transport occurs.
- The three models agree on where the fast regime lies, so the location of that regime in (T, P_ex) does not depend on the choice of transport model.
- The authors report that the phenomena reproduce after thermal cycling to room temperature.
- Comparing the measured kinetics with earlier vdW heterostructure studies (Fig. S12) is useful context.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. d_1/e is extracted well outside the window used to fit it. Page 2 states that d_1/e comes from a least-squares exponential fit of I(x, t) "in the region from the excitation spot to the HS edge, x = 0–9 µm". Yet the headline values of d_1/e reach 20–25 µm (Fig. 1b–d, Fig. 2). An exponential decay length two to three times the fitting window is fixed only by a small fractional drop in intensity across the window. Such a length is very sensitive to:
   - noise;
   - background subtraction;
   - the slight curvature of the profile;
   - any edge effect where the heterostructure ends at x ≈ 9 µm.

   The 11 ns profile in Fig. 1a is nearly flat. Because the decay length of a flattening profile diverges, the "anomalously fast", nearly linear growth of d_1/e could be largely an artifact of the metric: the profile flattens as IXs fill a finite, elongated strip and may accumulate at its end.

   The authors should:
   - show the raw I(x, t) profiles for the fast-regime data in Fig. 2 (500 µW, 1.7 K) on linear and log scales;
   - report the fit uncertainty of d_1/e;
   - use a window-independent measure of expansion, such as the mean-square displacement ⟨x²⟩(t), the cloud centroid, or the arrival time of a fixed intensity fraction at fixed distances;
   - fit the full simulated I(x, t) to the measured I(x, t), not just d_1/e(t).

2. The role of the device geometry and the built-in potential gradient is not separated from intrinsic mobility. The simulations include a landscape U(x) that "drops ~10 meV on ~10 µm", approximated as a 1 meV/µm gradient (page 3, SI). With the extracted µ ≈ 10^4 cm²/eVs, this gradient alone gives a drift velocity µ∇U ≈ 10^5 cm/s ≈ 1 µm/ns, a sizeable fraction of the observed expansion rate. Fast "ballistic-like" growth along x therefore may mainly reflect directed drift down a steep potential slope toward the heterostructure edge. In that case the anomaly would be partly a property of this one device's landscape.

   To separate the two, the authors should:
   - measure expansion in the opposite direction from the spot, or excite at several positions along the strip, and compare up-slope with down-slope expansion;
   - show the spatial PL energy map used to infer U(x);
   - show how the extracted µ changes if U is varied within its uncertainty or set to zero.

3. The evidence for superfluidity is only qualitative and indirect, and the alternative explanations are not excluded. The abstract and conclusion state that the observations are "qualitatively consistent with theoretical predictions of IX superfluidity". The three signatures given, however, have ordinary explanations:
   - High mobility could come from drift down the potential gradient (concern 2), or from an artifact of the metric (concern 1).
   - The loss of the fast regime above about 10 K could come from phonon scattering, thermally activated trapping, or the temperature-dependent IX lifetime and emission efficiency that enter the fits.
   - The decrease at high P_ex could come from laser heating of the IX gas or lattice, exciton–exciton annihilation, or a density-dependent lifetime.

   The page 4 argument against diffusive transport is that k_BT and the 3 meV interaction energy are both smaller than the roughly 10 meV disorder. That argument equates the PL linewidth with the potential-landscape amplitude relevant to transport, which is not justified: the linewidth also contains homogeneous, phonon and interaction broadening. No measurement probes coherence or superfluid response directly. Missing examples include:
   - first-order coherence (shift-interferometry g1);
   - a critical velocity;
   - a sharp onset or threshold in T or n with the expected scaling;
   - a comparison of the onset temperature with an estimated BKT or Bose–Hubbard superfluid temperature for the stated density.

   The authors should either add such evidence or scope the claim to "fast, non-diffusive IX transport kinetics" and present superfluidity as one hypothesis among several. In the second case they should state which observation would distinguish superfluid transport from drift in the landscape combined with heating at high power.

B. Should address

4. None of the three models reproduces the fast-regime data, so the extracted parameters are not reliable. In Fig. 2, for 500 µW, every simulation saturates near 13–17 µm, while the data keep rising to about 23 µm at t ≈ 5 ns. The diffusion model also misses the early-time shape. Reporting D, v and µ from fits that fail systematically (Fig. 3) and calling them "anomalously high" overstates what the fits establish. The paper acknowledges that the approaches are "rough estimates" (pages 2 and 4), but the abstract's headline "anomalously high IX mobility" relies on them.

   The authors should:
   - give residuals or a goodness-of-fit measure;
   - give confidence intervals on each parameter in Fig. 3;
   - say which inputs are fixed (τ, U, u0, generation profile Λ) and how sensitive the result is to each.

   Fig. 3 has no error bars at all. Its colour maps are interpolated from a sparse grid, with a white unmeasured region; the measured (T, P_ex) points should be marked.

5. The drift-diffusion model is internally inconsistent with the claim of quantum degeneracy. Eq. 3 uses the classical Einstein relation D' = µk_BT and a mean-field interaction energy. The paper then argues that the IX gas is in a degenerate, Bose–Hubbard superfluid regime, where neither assumption holds. For example, the Einstein relation is modified for degenerate bosons, and the mean-field u0 is known to underestimate density (as the paper notes). The authors should say how a degenerate-gas transport model would change the extracted µ, or state that the extracted µ has no meaning in the regime they propose.

6. The mean free path is not quantified, and a simple estimate conflicts with "long-range ballistic transport". Page 4 states that τ_m ≈ µm (IX mass m) and that the resulting l_m "indicates long-range ballistic transport", but gives no numbers in the main text.
   - For µ ≈ 3×10^4 cm²/eVs and m ≈ m0, τ_m ≈ 17 ps.
   - With the thermal velocity at 1.7 K (about 5×10^5 cm/s), or with the fitted v of a few 10^5 cm/s, this gives l_m ≈ 0.1 µm.

   That is far shorter than the roughly 20 µm expansion, and comparable to or below the optical resolution. The authors should give their numbers and reconcile them with the phrase "long-range ballistic transport" and with the nearly linear d_1/e(t). If transport is diffusive on scales above about 0.1 µm, the linear growth must come from drift, not ballistic flight.

7. Density and filling calibration is too uncertain for the Bose–Hubbard comparison. The paper places the fast regime at N ≈ 1/2 IX per moiré site. This density comes from a mean-field estimate (n ≈ 2×10^11 cm⁻² from δE ≈ 3 meV), which the authors acknowledge underestimates n, possibly by several times. Further problems:
   - The twist angle and the moiré period, which are needed to convert n to N, are not given in the main text.
   - The reference energy for δE is not stated.
   - It is not shown whether δE is measured in the same time window (0–5 ns) as the transport.
   - The moiré potential is described as deep (page 4). In that case the Bose–Hubbard tunneling J would be small, and the expected superfluid temperature would need an estimate to be compared with the observed 10 K.

   The authors should give:
   - the twist angle and its uncertainty;
   - δE(P_ex) and n(P_ex), with the range from mean-field to correlated estimates;
   - an estimate of J and of the Bose–Hubbard superfluid transition temperature.

   They should also replot Fig. 3 against n, since the abstract speaks of density dependence.

8. Heating at high excitation power is not ruled out as the cause of the suppression. The decrease of D, v and µ above about 500 µW (Figs. 1d, 3 a2–c2) is attributed to Bose–Hubbard physics. The 4 mW average-in-pulse power on a focused spot could, however, heat the lattice or the IX gas, and the effect would then mirror the temperature dependence in Fig. 3 a1–c1. The authors should:
   - estimate the effective IX temperature versus P_ex, for example from the high-energy tail of the PL spectrum or by comparison with the T-dependence;
   - show whether the suppression at high P_ex coincides with a temperature rise to about 10 K or more.

9. Only one heterostructure and one excitation geometry are reported. All data appear to come from a single MoSe2/WSe2 sample, with a single excitation spot near one end of an elongated strip. Thermal-cycle reproducibility is stated, which helps, but the anomaly is not shown in a second device. A second sample, or at least other positions in the same sample with different local disorder and gradients, would show whether the fast regime is a generic IX property or specific to this landscape. This connects to concern 2.

10. The IX formation dynamics and the generation profile at early times need justification. The generation term Λ(x) is taken to be the DX PL profile in MoSe2, and IX formation is assumed to follow the laser pulse instantly. Several early-time effects would affect d_1/e(t) and the inferred velocity during the first nanosecond or so:
    - hot-IX expansion before thermalization;
    - IX formation time;
    - the 0.1–0.3 ns rise of the pulse;
    - the APD time resolution;
    - spatial resolution, since d_1/e ≈ 3 µm at t = 0.

    The authors should state the APD time resolution and the optical point-spread function, and show that the fits are insensitive to excluding the first nanosecond.

11. The comparison with previous IX transport kinetics (Fig. S12) needs matched conditions. The claim that d_1/e grows "significantly faster" than in earlier studies (page 3) should state, for each comparison, the temperature, density, encapsulation and geometry, so the comparison is like for like. The connection to the group's earlier long-range transport results in similar heterostructures (refs. 62, 66, 67) should also be discussed: was fast kinetics present but unmeasured there?

C. Minor

12. The excitation-pulse edges are inconsistent. Page 2 gives "0.3 ns edge sharpness", while the Methods give "0.1 ns pulse rise time, 0.3 ns pulse fall time". Please reconcile.

13. Fig. 1a labels the dashed fits only implicitly. The fitting window (0–9 µm) and the heterostructure edge position should be marked on the panel.

14. Figs. 2 and 3 caption the temperature and power but show only two powers in Fig. 2. Adding an intermediate-power and a high-temperature case to Fig. 2 would show how fit quality varies across the map.

15. The IX lifetime τ used in all three models is measured in the SI. Its values versus T and P_ex should appear in the main text, because the fitted parameters depend on τ and τ itself changes with T.

16. Eq. 2 (ballistic transport, v∇n with constant v) is a phenomenological 1D drift equation, not ballistic kinetics. A sentence noting this would avoid over-interpreting v as a ballistic velocity.

17. The mobility is quoted in cm²/eVs. A conversion to the cm²/Vs convention common in exciton-drift literature would help comparison.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Use a window-independent measure of cloud expansion (⟨x²⟩(t) or a full I(x, t) fit), shown with the raw profiles and uncertainties, to establish that the anomalous growth is real and not set by the fitting window or the edge of the heterostructure.
2. Separate drift down the potential gradient from intrinsic mobility by exciting at several positions, or by comparing expansion up and down the gradient.
3. Add a direct probe of coherence (for example shift-interferometry of the IX emission) in the fast-transport regime and outside it. This would turn "qualitatively consistent with superfluidity" into a testable statement.
4. Give the twist angle, density calibration, effective IX temperature versus power, and parameter error bars, so that the Bose–Hubbard comparison and the trends in Fig. 3 can be assessed quantitatively.
