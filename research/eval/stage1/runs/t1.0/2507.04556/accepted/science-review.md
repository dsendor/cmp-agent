A. Must address

1. d_1/e is reported far beyond the window it is fitted in, so it may track how flat the PL profile is in a bounded heterostructure rather than how far IXs travel. The Fig. 1 caption (page 2) says d_1/e comes from least-squares exponential fits to I(x,t) "in the region from the excitation spot to the HS edge, x = 0–9 µm". Yet the headline values reach about 15–25 µm (Figs. 1b–d, Fig. 2), two to three times the fit window. In Fig. 1a the profiles at t = 2.5 and 11 ns are nearly flat across 0–10 µm. A decay length longer than the fitted interval is set only by a small fractional drop in intensity across the window, so it is very sensitive to noise, background subtraction and normalisation, slight curvature of the profile, and the edge of the heterostructure at x ≈ 9 µm. Growth from 10 to 20 µm then reflects a change in a tiny slope, not a front moving 10 µm. Several processes flatten a profile bounded by the HS edge without long-range transport: IXs filling the finite strip and piling up at, or reflecting from, the edge or a confining landscape; IXs generated across the HS by scattered or reflected laser light or by waveguided DX/PL emission; and saturation or quenching near the spot (exciton–exciton annihilation, heating) that lowers the central intensity over time. Edge effects alone could produce the late-time upturn of d_1/e at 500 µW (Fig. 2, last three points), which none of the three models reproduces, and could make the growth look superlinear. Since the "anomalously fast d_1/e growth" and every D, v and µ in Fig. 3 come from these fits, the authors should: show the raw I(x,t) for the fast-regime data (500 µW, 1.7 K) and for the 25 µW series on linear and log scales, with the fit uncertainty of d_1/e, especially when d_1/e exceeds the window; use a measure that stays bounded in a finite domain, such as the mean-square displacement ⟨x²⟩(t), the cloud centroid, or the arrival time of a fixed intensity fraction at fixed distances, and show that it also grows ballistically; simulate Eqs. 1–3 on a domain of the real HS length with explicit boundary conditions (reflecting or absorbing edge, or edge-enhanced emission) and fit the full simulated I(x,t) to the measured I(x,t) rather than d_1/e(t); show I(x,t) beyond the HS edge and on the x < 0 side to quantify scattered-light background; show the integrated intensity versus t to separate redistribution from loss at the centre; and, if possible, repeat with the excitation spot farther from the edge or along a longer direction of the HS.

2. Drift down the built-in potential gradient is not separated from intrinsic mobility. The drift-diffusion model takes a landscape U(x) that "drops ~10 meV on ~10 µm", about 1 meV/µm along the measurement direction ("outlined in SI", page 3), and treats transport as 1D along an elongated strip toward the HS edge. Drift in a uniform gradient gives a constant velocity, so a nearly linear d_1/e(t) is exactly what downhill drift looks like. With µ ≈ 10^4 cm²/eVs, the gradient alone gives µ∇U ≈ 10^5 cm/s ≈ 1 µm/ns, a sizeable fraction of the observed expansion rate. The extracted µ then depends on how well the assumed U is known, and the anomaly may be a property of this one device's landscape. The suppression at higher T and higher density could also reflect changes in screening of U, IX energy renormalisation or a changed effective landscape, rather than a change in the transport state, and the landscape is not measured as a function of P_ex and T. The authors should: provide spatially and spectrally resolved PL, E(x,t), at the relevant P_ex and T, so that U(x) and its time evolution are measured with uncertainties rather than assumed, and show the map used to infer U(x); compare expansion uphill and downhill (opposite direction from the spot), along y, or from several excitation positions along the strip; and report µ and D with and without the U term and with U varied within its uncertainty.

3. The link to superfluidity rests on qualitative trends that conventional mechanisms also produce, and no coherence-specific measurement is given. The abstract, the "Comparison of IX transport with BH theory" section and the conclusion state that the high mobility despite ~10 meV disorder, its loss above about 10 K, and its non-monotonic density dependence are "qualitatively consistent with theoretical predictions of IX superfluidity", and the text frames the result as "inconsistent with the diffusive transport". Each signature has an ordinary explanation: high mobility could come from gradient drift (concern 2) or from the metric (concern 1); the rise from low density is the standard screening/delocalisation behaviour the authors themselves describe; the loss above about 10 K could come from phonon scattering, thermally activated trapping, or the temperature dependence of IX lifetime and emission efficiency that enter the fits, and does not by itself single out superfluidity; and the high-density decrease (Fig. 1d, 1–4 mW) could come from laser heating of the IX gas or lattice, exciton–exciton annihilation shortening τ, or a Mott crossover to an electron–hole plasma, any of which lowers the extracted mobility without a superfluid-to-normal transition. The BH comparison is itself only qualitative (superfluid for N between about 0 and about 1 per site, with N ~ 1/2 given without uncertainty; see concern 7). The authors should add a direct probe in the same (T, P_ex) window as the fast transport: first-order spatial coherence of the IX emission from shift-interferometry (g1), measured inside and outside the fast regime; a sharp threshold in T or n with error bars and the expected scaling; a critical-velocity or flow-suppression test; the dependence on disorder or moiré potential depth; or a quantitative BH estimate (J, U and the predicted transition temperature for this moiré period) compared with the observed 10 K. They should also discuss each alternative explicitly, using IX linewidths and lifetimes versus P_ex, and say which observation would distinguish superfluid transport from landscape drift combined with high-power heating. Without such evidence, the title, abstract and conclusion should be scoped to "efficient, non-diffusive IX transport kinetics", with superfluidity presented as one hypothesis among several.

B. Should address

4. None of the three models describes the fast-regime data, so the D, v and µ in Fig. 3 are not reliable, and they are reported without uncertainties. In Fig. 2 at 500 µW, the measured d_1/e(t) is convex, accelerating to about 22–23 µm at 5 ns, while all three simulated curves are concave and saturate near 13–17 µm; the models are also too steep at early times, and the diffusion model misses the early-time shape. Single-parameter least-squares fits therefore average over a systematic misfit. The paper concedes the approaches are "rough estimates" that "do not aim to provide an accurate description" (pages 2 and 4), yet the abstract's "anomalously high IX mobility" is quoted from them, and Fig. 3 reports values over two orders of magnitude with no error bars. Its colour maps (a3, b3, c3) are interpolated from a sparse, irregular grid with a white unmeasured region that may hide structure. An accelerating expansion is not expected from ballistic motion or drift either, and hints at a time-dependent process such as a growing background, heating, or a front or edge effect (concern 1). The authors should give residuals or a goodness-of-fit measure and confidence intervals on every fitted parameter; state which inputs are fixed (τ, U, u0, generation profile Λ) and how sensitive the results are to each; mark the measured (T, P_ex) points on the maps; discuss the cause of the acceleration; and fit restricted time windows, or use a model with a time-dependent parameter, to test whether a single µ is meaningful.

5. The effective IX temperature and laser heating are not established, so the high-power suppression may simply be heating. All quoted temperatures are cryostat temperatures, and the argument against thermally activated diffusion compares k_B T ≲ 1 meV with the disorder and interaction energies. The excitation is a focused 84 ns pulse at a 300 ns period, resonant with the WSe2 DX, reaching 0.5 mW in the fast regime and 1–4 mW where the effect vanishes (Fig. 1d, Figs. 3 a2–c2). The IX temperature, and probably the local lattice temperature, may sit well above 1.7 K. This bears on the "thermal energy too low to delocalize" argument, on the comparison with a ~10 K BH transition temperature, and on the Einstein relation in Eq. 3. Hot IX injection and lattice heating would modify both the T and P_ex dependence, and the high-P_ex suppression could mirror the high-T suppression in Figs. 3 a1–c1. The authors should estimate the IX temperature versus P_ex and time after pulse onset, for example from the high-energy tail of the IX spectrum or from the temperature calibration of linewidth or energy; estimate local lattice heating at 0.5–4 mW; and show whether the high-P_ex suppression coincides with an effective temperature of about 10 K or more once heating is included.

6. The claim of "long-range ballistic transport" conflicts with the paper's own numbers. The "d_1/e(t) growth kinetics" section (page 4) relates τ_m ≈ µm, l_m ∝ τ_m and states that the result "indicates long-range ballistic transport", but gives values only in the SI. For µ ≈ 3–5×10^4 cm²/eVs (Fig. 3c) and m ≈ m0, τ_m ≈ 17–30 ps. With the thermal velocity at 1.7 K (about 5×10^5 cm/s), or with the fitted v of a few 10^5 to 10^6 cm/s (Fig. 3b), l_m is about 0.1–0.2 µm and in any case below 1 µm: two orders of magnitude below the ~20 µm expansion, and at or below the optical resolution. If transport is diffusive on scales above about 0.1 µm, the nearly linear d_1/e(t) must come from drift, not ballistic flight. The authors should give τ_m, the velocity assumed and l_m in the main text, reconcile them with the d_1/e growth over 5 ns, or replace "ballistic" with "drift-like" or "near-linear".

7. The density and moiré-filling calibration is too uncertain to support N ≈ 1/2 in the BH comparison. The density n ≈ 2×10^11 cm⁻² comes from the mean-field "plate capacitor" formula (d_z ≈ 0.6 nm, ε ≈ 7.4) applied to δE ≈ 3 meV; the text acknowledges that correlations raise n for a given shift, by factors commonly 2–5 in the cited literature. The reference energy for δE is not stated, nor whether δE is measured in the same 0–5 ns window as the transport. Converting n to N needs the moiré period, but the twist angle, its uncertainty, the stacking (R or H), the encapsulation and gating, and any evidence of a moiré potential are not in the main text. With both inputs this uncertain, N ≈ 1/2 cannot be distinguished from N ≈ 0 or 1. Because the moiré potential is described as deep (page 4), the BH tunnelling J would be small, and the expected superfluid temperature needs an estimate before it can be compared with the observed ~10 K. The authors should give in the main text the twist angle with its uncertainty and how it was determined, the moiré cell area, the encapsulation and gating; give δE(P_ex) and n(P_ex) with the range from mean-field to correlated estimates, and show whether N ~ 1/2 survives; estimate J, U and the BH superfluid transition temperature; and replot Fig. 3 against n, since the abstract speaks of density dependence.

8. The drift-diffusion model assumes a classical gas, which contradicts the quantum-degenerate regime invoked to interpret it. Eq. 3 uses the classical Einstein relation D' = µ k_B T and a mean-field interaction energy. Both hold for a non-degenerate gas in local equilibrium, the opposite of the degenerate BH superfluid the paper proposes: the Einstein relation is modified for degenerate bosons, and the mean-field u0 is known to underestimate density. Eq. 3 also ties D' to the cryostat T (concern 5), and a local-equilibrium description is questionable if the mean free path is claimed to be long. The authors should state how µ changes if D' is left free or uses the degenerate-gas form, or state that the extracted µ has no meaning in the regime they propose.

9. The disorder amplitude is inferred only from the ~10 meV IX PL linewidth. That identification underpins the argument (page 4) that high mobility "despite substantial disorder" is anomalous and that transport is non-diffusive because k_B T and the 3 meV interaction energy are both below the disorder. The linewidth may also contain homogeneous, phonon and exciton–exciton (interaction) broadening, unresolved singlet/triplet, trion or moiré sub-bands, and spatial averaging over the gradient U(x); its relation to the static disorder IXs see along a 20 µm path is not established. The authors should show the spectra, the low-power linewidth and the linewidth versus T and P_ex, and preferably spatial maps of IX energy, to justify the identification; otherwise they should present 10 meV as an upper bound.

10. The IX lifetime and the proportionality of PL intensity to IX density are assumed, not shown. τ enters all three models and is measured "as outlined in SI", but τ(T, P_ex) is not shown in the main text. If τ shortens at high density (annihilation) or at high T, the window for expansion shrinks and produces the apparent "disappearance" without any change in mobility. Converting I(x,t) to n(x,t) also assumes uniform IX radiative efficiency along x and over time; near the HS edge, with gradients in local stacking, strain or dielectric environment, and with conversion between bright and dark (spin-forbidden) IX states or local quenching, that can fail, and d_1/e of the PL need not equal d_1/e of the density. The authors should show τ(T, P_ex) next to Fig. 3, show a spatial map of cw IX brightness and energy across the HS, and discuss whether PL intensity is proportional to IX density across the cloud and over time.

11. The generation profile and early-time dynamics are not justified. The generation term Λ(x) is taken from the MoSe2 DX PL profile, and IX formation is assumed to follow the laser pulse instantly. This ignores diffusion of WSe2 DXs and interlayer charge transfer before IX formation. Because excitation at 1.689 eV is resonant with the WSe2 DX, IXs form with excess energy, and hot-exciton or "halo" expansion before thermalisation (refs. 39, 42) gives fast early spread that weakens as the lattice warms. The IX formation time, the 0.1–0.3 ns pulse rise, the APD time resolution and the spatial resolution (d_1/e ≈ 3 µm at t = 0) all affect d_1/e(t) and the inferred velocity during the first nanosecond or so. The authors should state the APD time resolution and the optical point-spread function, test how the fitted parameters depend on the assumed Λ(x), and show that the fits are insensitive to excluding the first nanosecond.
