Review outline:

**1. Significance and novelty**

The manuscript reports two-terminal, field-free, bidirectional current-induced resistance switching in sputtered PtMn3/MgO/PtMn3 nanopillars (50–200 nm) on Si/SiO2, with TMR up to 363% at room temperature and switching current densities of order 10 MA/cm². The authors attribute the switching to a new "octupole-driven spin-transfer torque" (OTT), rationalized by DFT conduction-channel projections (intra- vs inter-sublattice imbalance, β1 > β2) and three-sublattice LLG simulations. If established, electrical write by tunneling current in an all-antiferromagnetic tunnel junction would be a significant milestone, the reciprocal of the octupole-driven TMR reported in 2023. However, the present data do not yet show that the resistance change is magnetic in origin or that the switching is driven by a spin-transfer torque, rather than by heating, in-plane current in the leads, or barrier effects.

**2. Potential reasons for acceptance**

- A timely and important target: current-driven writing of an AFMTJ through the tunnel current, in industry-compatible stacks on 150 mm Si wafers.
- Large two-terminal resistance changes (up to ~360%) with abrupt bidirectional switching in several devices and sizes (Fig. 2a, Extended Data Fig. 1), plus a 36-device TMR histogram (Fig. 2b).
- Switching disappears above ~360 K (Fig. 2c), consistent with the known triangular-to-collinear transition of Mn3Pt, which is suggestive of a link to the noncollinear order.
- An intuitive microscopic picture (sublattice-resolved conduction channels, Fig. 3) connecting a staggered torque to the octupole polarization, with a clear derivation (Methods Eqs. 4–8) of why equal intra- and inter-sublattice efficiencies give zero net torque.

**3. Concerns**

**A. Must address**

1. **The resistance change is never shown to be magnetic (TMR).** All the transport data are current-pulse loops (Fig. 2a,c; Extended Data Fig. 1). No magnetic-field-driven R(H) loop is shown on any of these devices, and no nonmagnetic reference junction is shown (for example Pt/MgO/Pt, or a junction with one PtMn3 electrode replaced by Pt or by a collinear antiferromagnet) under the same pulse protocol. This matters because the operating conditions are those where bipolar resistive (filamentary or oxygen-vacancy) switching of thin MgO barriers is common. From Fig. 2a (100 nm, R_AP ≈ 2.4 kΩ, switching at ≈ −0.85 mA), the voltage across the 2 nm barrier at the AP→P transition is ≈ 2 V, i.e. ~10 MV/cm. In Extended Data Fig. 1c, 100 nm devices are pulsed to 4–6 mA at R ≈ 0.3 kΩ (≈ 1.2–1.8 V). The STEM (Fig. 1c, p. 5–6) also shows that the MgO is not crystallized and has a rough bottom interface. Calling the effect "TMR" and assigning the two states to "P" and "AP" octupole alignment both need an independent magnetic reference. The authors should:
   - show, on the same devices, field-swept R(H) (or SOT-set states as in their earlier work, Ref. 13) reaching the same two resistance levels as the current pulses;
   - show the bias (read-voltage) dependence of the two states: TMR should fall with bias, whereas filamentary states typically show different I–V nonlinearity;
   - measure a nonmagnetic-electrode control junction with matched RA under identical pulses;
   - report the read current/voltage used (Methods gives only the 1 ms width).

2. **The switching polarity is inconsistent between devices, which is hard to reconcile with STT and undermines the mechanism.** In Fig. 2a, positive current switches low→high resistance (red branch) and negative current switches high→low. In Fig. 2c and Fig. 2d the opposite holds: AP→P at positive J, P→AP at negative J. Extended Data Fig. 1 contains both senses: the 50 nm left panel and the 100 nm fourth panel switch low→high at positive current, while the 75 nm left panel and the first three 100 nm panels switch high→low at positive current. For a spin-transfer torque in a fixed stack sequence, the polarity is set by the current direction relative to the fixed and free layers and should be the same for every device. The paper does not comment on this. The authors must explain it (e.g., device-dependent choice of which PtMn3 layer switches) and show that the polarity is not set by lead geometry or by the history of the barrier.

3. **Alternative switching mechanisms are not excluded: Joule heating, SOT from in-plane current in the Pt leads, and Oersted fields.** The stack has 5 nm Pt directly adjacent to both PtMn3 layers, and the bottom electrode carries in-plane current into and out of the pillar. Pt/PtMn3 SOT switching is established (Ref. 13, the authors' own work). The dissipated power is large (≈ 0.5–2 mW in Fig. 2a; up to ~10 mW at 6 mA in Extended Data Fig. 1c, in a pillar of ~10⁻¹⁵ L) for 1 ms pulses, and the transition temperature is only ~360 K (Fig. 2c). So thermally assisted switching near the transition, with an Oersted field or spin current from the leads deciding the final state, is a leading alternative, and it could also explain the polarity scatter in concern 2. The authors should:
   - estimate the device temperature during the pulse (for example from the resistance measured during the pulse against the R(T) calibration, which they have from Fig. 2c);
   - run in-plane current through the bottom lead only, without passing through the junction, to test for lead-SOT switching;
   - check whether the switching current scales with pillar area (J roughly constant) rather than with lead current;
   - compare stacks without the Pt layers adjacent to PtMn3 (e.g., a light-metal seed giving (111) texture) or with the Pt thickness varied;
   - show how the switching current depends on an applied in-plane field (Oersted or SOT-assisted switching should depend on it differently than STT);
   - compare switching at reduced base temperature, where heating to the transition is harder.

4. **Fixed and free layers are not identified, yet the model requires them.** The theory (Eqs. 4–8, Extended Data Fig. 5) and the barrier analysis (Extended Data Fig. 3: "100 nm × 7 nm") take the bottom 7 nm PtMn3 as the free layer and the top 10 nm layer as fixed. Neither layer is pinned (no exchange bias or synthetic structure). Both have the same composition, and their thickness, and hence ΔV, differs only by a factor ~1.4. In a symmetric STT picture both layers receive torques of opposite sense. The paper should give evidence for which layer switches and why the other is stable, for example from devices with strongly asymmetric thicknesses, or by setting each layer independently by field or by SOT. This also bears on concern 2.

5. **The theory is qualitative and does not connect to the measured junctions, so "confirmed by our DFT calculations" (p. 4) is overstated.**
   - The DFT gives bulk k∥-resolved conduction channels of PtMn3 (Fig. 3a,b). No junction transport or torque is calculated, e.g. a PtMn3/MgO/PtMn3 NEGF or scattering calculation of the sublattice-resolved torques.
   - The argument β1 > β2 rests on a schematic of "a select band" along Γ–K (Fig. 3c,d). No integrated values of β1 and β2 over the Fermi surface are given, and it is not shown that the net staggered polarization survives summation over all k∥.
   - The LLG simulation simply sets β_eff = β1 − β2 = 1 (Extended Data Table 1), i.e. the maximal value, and η = 0.53 is unsourced.
   - The mechanism relies on k∥ conservation in ballistic tunneling across epitaxially registered (111) Kagome planes (p. 7). The real devices have polycrystalline PtMn3 with an uncharacterized in-plane rotational relation between grains of the two electrodes, an amorphous MgO barrier, and visible roughness (Fig. 1c,d, p. 5–6). Both the k∥ selectivity and the alignment of octupoles across the barrier are therefore questionable assumptions.
   - The simulations report ps-scale switching at J = 10 MA/cm² but give no critical current, so the theory does not predict the measured J.

   The authors should compute β1 and β2 (or the octupole torque) quantitatively, in a junction geometry if possible, and use them in the LLG model to predict a critical current density to compare with experiment. Otherwise the theoretical claims should be reduced to a plausible mechanism.

**B. Should address**

6. **The switching current density is not "similar" across devices, and the figures disagree with "~10 MA/cm²".** From the pillar areas, the Extended Data Fig. 1 loops imply:
   - ~13 MA/cm² (1 mA) and ~75 MA/cm² (−6 mA) for the second 100 nm device;
   - ~50 MA/cm² for the 4 mA 100 nm device;
   - ~10–19 MA/cm² for the 200 nm devices.

   Fig. 2d shows J from ~10 to ~85 MA/cm². The abstract and p. 6 ("similar J values"; "~11 MA/cm²") should be supported by a statistical plot of J_c (both polarities) against diameter and against RA for all switching devices. Otherwise the stated range should be revised.

7. **The energy-barrier extraction is not robust, and its agreement with theory is circular.**
   - Reading the fitted lines in Fig. 2d gives a J_c0 of roughly 190 MA/cm² and Δ ≈ 27 kT for AP→P, and roughly 130 MA/cm² and Δ ≈ 50 kT for P→AP. The single value of 39 kT therefore appears to be an average of two quite different branches; the fit procedure and per-branch values should be given.
   - Δ depends on the assumed t0 = 1 ps (an antiferromagnetic attempt time, rather than the ~1 ns usually used for ferromagnets). The sensitivity to t0 should be shown.
   - The model assumes a monodomain free layer and a barrier reduced linearly by STT. This conflicts with the multilevel switching and the ~100–170 nm domains the authors themselves invoke, and with possible Joule heating, which also produces pulse-width dependence.
   - The diameter of the Fig. 2d device is not stated.
   - Most importantly, Methods (p. 23) states that K_eff = 1 kJ/m³ was obtained by "matching the numerics to Δ = 39 kT evaluated from OTT switching currents". The main-text statement (p. 7) that 39 kT is "consistent with our theoretical estimation" is therefore circular and should be removed or rephrased.

8. **Material parameters are inconsistent between the two models.**
   - The barrier calculation uses M_s = 1110 emu/cm³ (= 1110 kA/m), while the LLG model uses M_s = 153 kA/m (Extended Data Table 1).
   - The barrier calculation uses K_eff = 1 kJ/m³, while the LLG model uses K_u = 10 kJ/m³.
   - The exchange is J = 280 × 10⁶ J/m³ with J > 0 in the barrier model, but A_AB = −10 pJ/m with a 0.5 nm lattice constant in the LLG model. It is unclear whether these represent the same exchange field.

   The authors should use one consistent parameter set, justify it against literature values for Mn3Pt, and state which results depend on it. In particular, the simulated switching speed and threshold depend on K_u and M_s.

9. **The TMR statistics and RA values need fuller reporting.**
   - The headline 363% comes from one device. Fig. 2b shows that 23 of 36 devices have TMR < 100%, and the Fig. 2c device has ~50%. The abstract and introduction should state the typical and median values alongside the maximum.
   - RA, computed from R_P and the nominal diameter, varies systematically with size: ≈ 1.5 Ω·μm² for 50 nm (Extended Data Fig. 1a), ≈ 2–4 Ω·μm² for 100 nm, ≈ 5–7 Ω·μm² for 200 nm. This points to series resistance, lithographic size deviation, or pinholes, any of which affects both J and TMR.
   - Please give R_P, RA, TMR and J_c for every device, how many of the 36 showed switching, and how many wafers or dies they came from.
   - Since "record-high" TMR is claimed for a junction with amorphous MgO and polycrystalline electrodes, larger than in epitaxial PtMn3 AFMTJs, independent confirmation that this value is magnetic (concern 1) is especially important.

10. **The NV-magnetometry domain argument uses an unrepresentative sample and is not borne out by the device data.** The NV film is epitaxial PtMn3(100), co-sputtered on a MgO(100) substrate annealed at 850 °C (Methods, p. 19). The device layers are polycrystalline (111)-textured PtMn3 grown at room temperature on Pt. Domain sizes can differ greatly between the two. Also:
   - It is not explained how "~100–170 nm" was extracted from Extended Data Fig. 2.
   - Stray field from a (100) Mn-terminated surface may reflect surface or termination contrast rather than octupole domains.
   - The claim that only devices "comparable to or smaller than the expected domain size" switch abruptly is contradicted by Extended Data Fig. 1, where 200 nm devices switch abruptly and some 50–75 nm devices switch gradually.

   Either image domains in the device-type (111) film, or soften the claim.

11. **The temperature dependence is thin.**
   - Fig. 2c is one device at four temperatures, with nothing between 330 and 360 K.
   - It is not shown whether the TMR itself (e.g., from field-set states) vanishes at 360 K, or only the ability to switch.
   - The R_P level hardly changes; please also show R_P(T) and R_AP(T).
   - Finer temperature steps would help, together with J_c(T) approaching the transition, which should be compared with the expected scaling of the octupole order parameter.

   This would strengthen the link to the noncollinear phase. It would also help separate thermally assisted switching: if heating were driving the switching, J_c should fall steeply as the base temperature approaches ~360 K.

12. **Reproducibility and endurance are not quantified.** "Reversible, repeatable" (p. 6) needs:
   - the number of consecutive loops per device;
   - cycle-to-cycle J_c distributions;
   - the switching probability against pulse amplitude at fixed width;
   - retention of both states;
   - behaviour after thermal cycling (since devices were warmed to 400 K).

   Abrupt bipolar switching that degrades or drifts with cycling would also point to a barrier origin.

13. **The electrode microstructure and chemistry are under-characterized for a mechanism that depends on crystal symmetry.** Only local FFTs of two grains are shown (Fig. 1d). Please provide:
   - XRD or pole figures for the (111) texture fraction and the in-plane variant distribution;
   - the PtMn3 composition, since stoichiometry controls the phase and the transition temperature;
   - the L1₂ chemical order parameter;
   - interface intermixing or oxidation (e.g., EELS/EDS across the MgO interfaces).

   Without these, the assumptions of a T1g octupole state and of k∥-conserving tunneling (concern 5) cannot be assessed.

**C. Minor**

14. **DFT method details need correcting or justifying.**
   - Ref. 64 (Perdew et al., PRL 100, 136406) is PBEsol, not PBE. Please state which functional was used.
   - Justify U = 2 eV for metallic Mn3Pt and show that the conduction-channel anisotropy is robust to U.
   - The spin polarization is "extrapolated from VASP calculations and interpolated onto the 120×120×80 k-mesh", while the site projections come from the Wannier model. Explain how the two are made consistent.
   - State whether the magnetic structure was relaxed with SOC, and give the moments obtained.
   - Report the Wannier-model k-mesh and the convergence of the projected channels.

15. **Extended Data Fig. 5 needs clearer labelling.** The initial and final states in panels b,c are hard to reconcile with the stated initialization along −e_i (e.g., the solid m_y3 trace appears to start near +1 and end at −1). Please mark the initial and final configurations and confirm that the final state is the other energy minimum of Extended Data Fig. 3 (full reversal of m_i). The Methods text (p. 26) refers to green arrows "in Extended Data Fig. 1a"; this should be Extended Data Fig. 5a.

16. **The discussion contradicts itself on the theory.** "We expect that our results will motivate the research community to develop a theory of OTT" (p. 10) sits uneasily with "We uncover a novel microscopic mechanism, confirmed by our DFT calculations" (p. 4). The relation to existing theory of STT in noncollinear-antiferromagnet junctions (Ref. 46, Ghosh et al.; Refs. 44, 47) should be discussed explicitly, including what is new here.

17. **Figure details are missing.**
   - Fig. 2c and 2d: state the pillar diameter.
   - Fig. 2d: state how the error bars were obtained (number of loops; standard deviation or range).
   - Fig. 3c: say which band and energy the "select band" is, and how β1 and β2 are defined numerically.
   - Fig. 3b: give the colour bar's sign convention for N^(iσ), and say whether it is positive everywhere.

18. **Referencing and notation slips.**
   - Reference 34 is missing from the list, which jumps from 33 to 35.
   - Ref. 56 is cited in the main text before Refs. 51–55.
   - The symbol J is used both for current density and for exchange (Methods Eqs. 1–2), and Eq. 4 calls J "the applied current". Use distinct symbols and state units.
   - Ms in the barrier calculation is given in emu/cm³ but in kA/m in Extended Data Table 1. Use consistent units.

**4. Suggestions for improvement**

1. Add a single "control" figure combining, on the same devices: field- or SOT-set R(H) reaching the same levels as current switching; a nonmagnetic-electrode junction under identical pulses; resistance measured during the pulse to estimate the device temperature; and in-plane lead-current tests. This one figure would address most of the A-level doubts.
2. Resolve the fixed/free-layer question and the polarity scatter together. Strongly asymmetric electrode thicknesses (or pinning one electrode) would give a predicted sign of switching that can be checked across many devices.
3. Make the theory quantitative: compute integrated β1 and β2 (ideally in a PtMn3/MgO/PtMn3 junction), feed them into the LLG model with one consistent parameter set, and compare the predicted J_c with the measured distribution.
4. Report full device statistics (RA, TMR, J_c for both polarities, abrupt or multilevel, diameter, cycle count) in a table. Reframe the headline TMR as maximum and typical values, and the energy barrier as per-branch values with their t0 sensitivity.
