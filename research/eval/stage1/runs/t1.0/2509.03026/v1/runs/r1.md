Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports current-driven, field-free, bidirectional switching of nanopillar PtMn3(7 nm)/MgO(2 nm)/PtMn3(10 nm) all-antiferromagnetic tunnel junctions sputtered at room temperature on oxidized Si. The resistance changes by up to 363% at room temperature, at switching current densities quoted as about 10 MA/cm2. The authors attribute the switching to an "octupole-driven spin-transfer torque" (OTT): a sublattice-resolved, staggered Slonczewski-like torque that they motivate with bulk DFT conduction-channel maps and a three-sublattice LLG model. If the switching is shown to be spin torque from one noncollinear antiferromagnet acting on another through a tunnel barrier, it would be the electrical-write counterpart of the octupole-driven TMR reported in 2023, and an important result for antiferromagnetic spintronics and MRAM. As submitted, the experiments establish bipolar, current-driven resistance switching in these pillars. They do not yet establish that the two resistance states are octupole configurations, or that the drive is a spin torque rather than heating, dielectric (MgO) resistive switching or another current-driven mechanism.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The topic is timely and important. Electrical writing of an all-antiferromagnetic tunnel junction by its own tunnelling current is a widely sought goal.
- The devices are grown with industrial PVD on 150 mm Si wafers and patterned down to 50 nm. Switching is shown at several pillar diameters (Extended Data Fig. 1) and over 36 devices (Fig. 2b).
- The resistance changes are very large (up to 363%) and saturate cleanly in the best devices (Fig. 2a).
- The temperature series (Fig. 2c), in which switching disappears above about 360 K, ties the effect to a magnetic phase of PtMn3 and is a useful control.
- The theory section gives an intuitive, symmetry-based picture (intra- versus inter-sublattice spin currents, β1 > β2) that could be developed into quantitative predictions. The micromagnetic model shows that such a staggered torque can, in principle, switch the octupole deterministically.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. **The two resistance states are not shown to be magnetic (octupole) states.**
   - **What is missing.** Every TMR value in the paper (Fig. 2a–c, Extended Data Fig. 1) comes from current-pulse loops. There is no field-driven R(H) loop showing that the same high and low resistance levels can be reached with a magnetic field, as in the earlier PtMn3 and Mn3Sn AFMTJ work the paper cites (refs. 11–13).
   - **Why it matters.** The bias across the barrier during the pulses is very large. In Fig. 2a the high state is about 2.4 kΩ at |I| ≈ 0.8–1 mA, which is about 2 V across 2 nm of MgO. For the 100 nm devices in Extended Data Fig. 1c (about 1.3 kΩ at up to 6 mA), it exceeds 5 V. These fields are at or beyond typical MgO breakdown. Bipolar, reversible, multilevel resistance switching with large on/off ratios is the signature of oxygen-vacancy or filamentary resistive switching in thin oxides. The gradual multilevel loops in Extended Data Fig. 1 and the polarity that changes from device to device (concern 2) also fit that picture. The MgO is described as not crystallized and rough at the bottom interface (Fig. 1c), which makes this alternative more plausible.
   - **What the authors should do.**
     - Show R(H) loops, in-plane and out-of-plane, on devices that also switch with current, and show that the field-set states have the same resistances as the current-set states.
     - Show that a current-set state can be reset by field, and the reverse.
     - Show I–V and dI/dV curves and the bias dependence of the TMR in both states. Report the read current and bias.
     - Measure a control junction with nonmagnetic electrodes and the same MgO (for example Pt/MgO/Pt, or PtMn3 replaced by Pt on one side) under the same pulse protocol, and show that it does not switch.

2. **The drive is not shown to be a spin-transfer torque from the other PtMn3 layer.**
   - **Switching polarity is not reproducible.** For STT-like switching, the polarity is set by the current direction relative to the reference and free layers. In this paper it changes between devices:
     - Fig. 2a: positive current switches low→high.
     - Fig. 2c: positive current switches high→low.
     - Fig. 2d: AP→P at positive J.
     - Extended Data Fig. 1: both polarities appear within each diameter (for example, the first and fourth 100 nm panels are opposite, and the two 200 nm panels are opposite).
   - **Free and reference layers are not identified.** The paper does not say which PtMn3 layer is free and which is the reference. Neither layer is exchange-pinned, and they differ only in thickness (7 vs 10 nm). The energy-barrier estimate assumes the 7 nm bottom layer is free. In a symmetric, unpinned AFMTJ, why does only one layer switch, and why is the polarity random?
   - **Alternative mechanisms are not excluded.**
     - Joule heating: about 2.4 mW dissipated in a 100 nm pillar for 1 ms in Fig. 2a.
     - Heat-assisted reorientation close to the 360 K transition.
     - Thermoelectric (Seebeck/Peltier) asymmetry between the two electrodes.
     - Spin-orbit torque from in-plane current in the Pt seed and capping layers near the pillar. The group's own earlier work (ref. 13) switches PtMn3 by SOT from Pt.
     - Oersted fields.
   - **What the authors should do.**
     - Count, over the device population, how many devices switch with each polarity, and explain the result within the OTT picture.
     - Estimate the junction temperature during the pulse, for example by using R(T) of the barrier as a thermometer, or by comparing ns-pulse and ms-pulse results.
     - Measure the switching current versus applied field.
     - Fabricate and measure reference stacks under matched conditions: a single PtMn3 layer with a nonmagnetic counter-electrode, and a stack with one PtMn3 layer replaced by a collinear antiferromagnet (for example L10 PtMn). The effect should be absent or strongly reduced in these, and that is the key evidence that the torque comes from octupole-polarized tunnelling.
     - Make the reference/free assignment testable, for example with a thickness series of each layer.

3. **The theory does not describe the measured junction, and it predicts no torque magnitude.**
   - **Coherent-tunnelling assumption.** The OTT picture assumes ballistic transport with conserved k‖ ("in a crystalline AFMTJ operating in the ballistic transport regime", p. 7). Its key ingredient, the sublattice selectivity of conduction channels along Γ–K, depends on that coherence. The measured junctions have polycrystalline PtMn3 and a MgO barrier the authors describe as "did not indicate well-defined crystallization" with "visible roughness" (pp. 5–6). In that case k‖ is not conserved, and the directional selectivity of Fig. 3 would largely average out.
   - **What was calculated.** The calculation gives bulk PtMn3 conduction channels (Fig. 3a,b; Eq. 3). It does not compute transmission through MgO and it does not compute torque. β1 and β2 are never evaluated numerically.
   - **What was simulated.** The LLG simulation sets β_eff = β1 − β2 = 1 (the maximum, Extended Data Table 1) and η = 0.53 by hand. It switches in about 3 ps at 10 MA/cm2 with no thermal fluctuations, which is not compared with the ms-scale, thermally activated switching in Fig. 2d.
   - **Why it matters.** The claim that the mechanism is "confirmed by our density functional theory calculations" (p. 4) is therefore not supported quantitatively.
   - **What the authors should do.**
     - Compute β1 and β2, or the sublattice-resolved torque directly, for a PtMn3/MgO/PtMn3 (111) supercell. A NEGF/Kubo torque calculation would be best; at minimum, integrate the k‖-resolved channels over the Brillouin zone.
     - Discuss how the ratio survives partial k‖ randomization by an amorphous barrier.
     - Use the computed β_eff in finite-temperature simulations, and compare the predicted critical current and its dependence on pulse width with Fig. 2d.
     - Otherwise, present the theory as a plausible mechanism rather than a confirmation.

B. Should address

4. **Chemical order, stoichiometry and texture of the room-temperature-deposited PtMn3 are not shown.**
   - **What is missing.** The T1 noncollinear octupole state and its 360 K transition (used as evidence on p. 7) belong to chemically ordered L12 Mn3Pt near the 3:1 composition. All layers were deposited at room temperature without annealing (Methods), which usually gives a chemically disordered A1 phase with a different magnetic structure. The structural evidence is FFT patterns from two grains (Fig. 1d) showing (111) spots, which do not distinguish L12 from A1.
   - **What the authors should do.**
     - Provide evidence of L12 superlattice order: (100)/(110) reflections in XRD or electron diffraction, with an order parameter.
     - Provide the composition (EDS/RBS/XPS).
     - Provide wafer-level texture (XRD rocking curves or pole figures) for the actual device stack.
     - Provide magnetic characterization of the unpatterned stack, for example the anomalous Hall effect or the temperature dependence of the transition.

5. **The energy-barrier analysis (Fig. 2d, Extended Data Fig. 3) is ambiguous, and the "consistency" with theory is circular.**
   - **The two branches give different barriers.** Reading the two fitted lines in Fig. 2d gives intercept/slope ratios of very roughly Δ ≈ 25–30 for AP→P and Δ ≈ 45–55 for P→AP. The quoted 39 kT appears to be an average. The large asymmetry is not discussed. Taken at face value, the AP→P line reaches J ≈ 0 near ln(tp/t0) ≈ 26 (about 0.2 s), which implies an AP state that relaxes spontaneously in about a second. That conflicts with "moderately large thermal stability".
   - **Fit assumptions.**
     - Δ depends on the assumed t0 = 1 ps; give the sensitivity to t0.
     - The Koch et al. model (ref. 50) assumes no heating, but with ms pulses at mW power, heating likely dominates.
     - Which device was used (diameter), and how were the error bars obtained (number of repetitions)?
   - **Circular comparison with theory.** The "consistent with our theoretical estimation" statement (p. 7) is circular. The Methods set Keff = 1 kJ/m3 by matching the model to the measured Δ = 39 kT; this is not an independent prediction.
   - **Anisotropy values disagree.** This Keff is about three orders of magnitude below the giant single-ion anisotropy expected for L12 Mn3X (ref. 51). It also differs from Ku = 10 kJ/m3 used in the switching simulation (Extended Data Table 1).
   - **Monodomain assumption.** A macrospin barrier over the full pillar volume is hard to reconcile with the multidomain switching the authors themselves invoke.
   - **What the authors should do.** Report Δ for each branch separately, with uncertainties and the sensitivity to t0. Show results on more than one device. Either derive Keff independently or remove the claim of agreement.

6. **The claimed switching current density does not match the figures.**
   - **What the paper claims.** The abstract and p. 6 give J ≈ 11 MA/cm2 and say that all diameters switch "at similar J values".
   - **What the figures show.**
     - Fig. 2a (100 nm, about 1 mA) does correspond to about 12 MA/cm2.
     - Fig. 2d gives 50–80 MA/cm2 at 1 ms.
     - Extended Data Fig. 1 gives about 25 MA/cm2 for 50 nm (0.5 mA) and up to about 75 MA/cm2 for some 100 nm devices (6 mA).
   - **Why it matters.** The order-of-magnitude spread at fixed diameter matters both for the mechanism and for the efficiency claim.
   - **What the authors should do.** Plot Jc (both polarities) against diameter and RA for all devices, with medians and spread, and revise the headline number.

7. **The TMR values, and the "record-high" claim, rest on one device and on current-defined states only.**
   - **Spread of values.** Fig. 2b shows that most of the 36 devices have TMR below about 100%. The 363% value comes from a single device, and the device in Fig. 2c shows about 55%.
   - **What is not reported.**
     - The read bias.
     - The TMR bias dependence.
     - The series (lead and electrode) resistance in the two-terminal geometry.
     - The RA product of each device.
   - **What the authors should do.**
     - Report these quantities.
     - Show field-defined TMR (see concern 1).
     - Discuss why a junction with an amorphous barrier and polycrystalline electrodes would exceed the TMR of epitaxial Mn3Pt AFMTJs (ref. 12), when the proposed mechanism relies on coherent k‖-selective tunnelling.
     - Moderate "record-high" accordingly.

8. **The temperature evidence (Fig. 2c) is too sparse to tie switching to the noncollinear–collinear transition.**
   - **What is shown.** Fig. 2c has four temperatures (300, 330, 360, 400 K) on one device.
   - **What is missing.**
     - The switching currents at 330 K are not compared with those at 300 K.
     - The junction temperature during the pulse is not known.
     - The transition temperature of these particular films is not measured independently.
   - **Why it matters.** If the pillar heats past about 360 K during the pulse at room temperature, the switching may be heat-assisted rather than torque-driven. Loss of a metastable high state at 360 K is also consistent with a small Δ.
   - **What the authors should do.** Provide a finer temperature series (Jc(T) and TMR(T)) on several devices, an independent measurement of the film's transition (magnetometry or AHE), and a pulse-temperature estimate.

9. **The NV magnetometry is on a different film, and its conclusion is contradicted by the device data.**
   - **A different film.** The NV sample (Extended Data Fig. 2) is a co-sputtered, epitaxial (100) PtMn3 film on an MgO(100) substrate annealed at 850 °C. It differs in orientation, growth and microstructure from the (111) polycrystalline device layers. The domain sizes it gives cannot be carried over to the devices.
   - **Unclear contrast.** It is not shown that the stray-field contrast comes from octupole domains rather than from terminations, grains or roughness.
   - **Contradicted by the device data.** The inference that only pillars smaller than about 100–170 nm switch abruptly is not borne out by Extended Data Fig. 1, where some 200 nm pillars switch sharply and some 50–75 nm pillars switch gradually.
   - **What the authors should do.** Image the actual (111) device stack, ideally before and after current switching or in field, or soften the domain-size argument.

10. **The device statistics and reproducibility of switching are under-reported.**
    - **What is missing.** "Reversible, repeatable" is asserted (p. 6), but the paper does not report:
      - the number of consecutive cycles per device;
      - switching probability versus pulse amplitude;
      - the yield (how many of the measured devices switch, and how many show abrupt versus multilevel behaviour);
      - the polarity statistics (concern 2);
      - whether devices degrade or break down after repeated multi-volt pulses.
    - **What the authors should do.** Add these data. They bear directly on both the mechanism and the MRAM claims in the Discussion.

11. **The framing of novelty overstates the prior consensus.**
    - **The theory claim.** The abstract says STT in AFMTJs "has been assumed non-existent". The paper itself cites theoretical predictions of spin-transfer torque in noncollinear antiferromagnetic junctions (refs. 44–46, 60). Ref. 46 explicitly predicts robust STT in noncollinear AFM junctions.
    - **The experimental claim.** Current-induced switching in AFM-based tunnel junctions has also been reported (e.g. ref. 26).
    - **What the authors should do.** State precisely what is new relative to these works, in particular relative to ref. 46's torque mechanism, and temper "first experimental observation" until concerns 1–2 are resolved.

C. Minor

12. **Model parameters are inconsistent across the Methods.**
    - Ms = 1110 emu/cm3 (1110 kA/m) in the barrier calculation, against Ms = 153 kA/m in Extended Data Table 1.
    - Keff = 1 kJ/m3 against Ku = 10 kJ/m3.
    - Lattice constant a0 = 0.5 nm (the PtMn3 lattice parameter is about 0.38–0.39 nm).
    - Free-layer thickness d = 10 nm in the LLG model, but 7 nm in the barrier estimate.

    Please reconcile these or justify each choice.

13. **The DFT section has several gaps.**
    - It says PBE but cites ref. 64, which is PBEsol; please clarify which functional was used.
    - Justify U = 2 eV and show that the conduction-channel anisotropy is robust to U.
    - SOC is included, yet the mechanism is called non-relativistic. Show the maps without SOC to separate the contributions.
    - State whether the T1 configuration is the computed ground state.

14. **Fig. 3 is hard to read quantitatively.**
    - Fig. 3a,b and Extended Data Fig. 4 show absolute channel counts, from which the claimed "dominance" of one sublattice along each Γ–K axis is hard to judge. Add difference maps (sublattice i minus the average) and integrated β1, β2 values.
    - The Fig. 3c caption ("schematic representation of the Fermi surface") should explain what the triangles and arrow colours denote.

15. **Devices in Fig. 2 are not identified.** Give the pillar diameter and RA for Fig. 2c and Fig. 2d, and state how J was computed (nominal versus SEM-measured area).

16. **There is a cross-reference error and a gap in the reference numbering.**
    - The Micromagnetic Methods text refers to "Extended Data Fig. 1a with the green arrows"; this should be Extended Data Fig. 5a.
    - The reference list skips number 34.

17. **Eq. 9 is not dimensionally consistent as written.** The torque term T_i is placed inside the bracket multiplied by μ0, which mixes field and torque units; please write it consistently. Also state the sign convention for A_AB = −10 pJ/m.

18. **The NV Methods description of the filter is unclear.** The Gaussian filter (σ = 16 nm) is smaller than the 20 nm scan step, so the statement that it acts as near-identity needs clarification. Give the domain-size extraction from the power spectrum explicitly (Extended Data Fig. 2b).

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Make the magnetic origin unambiguous in one figure. On the same device, show the current-switching loop, field-driven R(H) reaching the same two levels, and a cross-reset (current-set → field-reset and field-set → current-reset). Add a matched nonmagnetic-electrode control junction measured with the same pulses.
2. Add a reference-stack and polarity study. Use thickness series of the top and bottom PtMn3 layers, and a junction with one PtMn3 replaced by a collinear antiferromagnet or by Pt. Report polarity statistics, to show that the torque requires octupole-polarized tunnelling and to identify which layer is free.
3. Turn the theory into a prediction. Compute β1 and β2, or the sublattice torques, in a (111) PtMn3/MgO/PtMn3 supercell. Then run finite-temperature three-sublattice simulations with that β_eff to predict Jc and its pulse-width dependence, and compare them with Fig. 2d. Discuss the effect of an amorphous barrier on k‖ selectivity.
4. Quantify heating. Use pulse widths from ns to ms and R(T)-based thermometry. This would separate torque-driven from heat-assisted switching and make the energy-barrier extraction reliable.
