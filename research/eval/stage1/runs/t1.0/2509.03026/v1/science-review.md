A. Must address

1. **The two resistance states are not shown to be magnetic (octupole) states rather than resistive switching of the barrier.** Every TMR value in the paper (Fig. 2a–c, Extended Data Fig. 1) comes from two-terminal current-pulse loops. No field-driven R(H) loop is shown on any device, and no nonmagnetic reference junction is measured, unlike the earlier field-switched PtMn3 and Mn3Sn AFMTJ work the paper cites (refs. 11–13). The operating conditions are those where bipolar resistive (filamentary, oxygen-vacancy or interface-redox) switching of thin MgO is common. In Fig. 2a (100 nm pillar) the high state is about 2.4 kΩ at a switching current of about 0.85–1 mA, i.e. about 2–2.4 V across a nominal 2 nm MgO (about 10 MV/cm, near breakdown and soft breakdown). In the 100 nm devices of Extended Data Fig. 1c, pulsed to ±4–6 mA, the bias is roughly 1.2–5 V depending on the resistance level, and several mW are dissipated. Square or gradual bipolar hysteresis, on/off ratios of several hundred percent, gradual multilevel loops (Extended Data Fig. 1), a broad device-to-device distribution (Fig. 2b: most devices below 100%, one at 363%) and a polarity that changes between devices (concern 2) are all signatures of barrier resistive switching. The STEM shows MgO that "did not indicate well-defined crystallization" with a rough bottom interface (Fig. 1c, pp. 5–6), which makes this alternative more plausible. Calling the effect "TMR" and assigning the states to P and AP octupole alignment needs an independent magnetic reference. The authors should: (a) show in-plane and out-of-plane R(H) loops (or SOT-set states as in ref. 13) on devices that also switch with current, reaching the same two resistance levels; (b) show that a current-set state can be reset by field and the reverse; (c) show I–V and dI/dV curves and the bias dependence of the TMR in both states (TMR should fall with bias, while filamentary states show different I–V nonlinearity), and report the read current and voltage (Methods gives only the 1 ms width); (d) measure a control junction with nonmagnetic electrodes and the same MgO and matched RA (e.g. Pt/MgO/Pt, or one PtMn3 replaced by Pt or by a collinear antiferromagnet such as L10 PtMn) under the identical pulse protocol and show that it does not switch. Without at least (a) or (d), "OTT switching of the CMO" is not established.

2. **The switching polarity is not consistent across nominally identical devices, which argues against spin-transfer torque.** For STT in a fixed stack the current direction that favours P or AP is set by which layer is free and by the sign of the torque, and the OTT model (Eqs. 4–8, Extended Data Fig. 5) predicts one polarity per stack. In the paper: Fig. 2a, positive current switches low→high and negative current high→low; Fig. 2c, positive current switches high→low; Fig. 2d labels positive J as AP→P, opposite to Fig. 2a. Extended Data Fig. 1 contains both senses within each diameter: the 50 nm panel and the fourth 100 nm panel switch low→high at positive current, while the 75 nm left panel and the first three 100 nm panels switch high→low; the two 200 nm panels are also opposite. Polarity that varies from device to device is typical of filamentary or thermally driven switching. The paper does not comment on it. The authors should state the current-sign convention, tabulate the polarity for all 36 devices, and explain the variation within the OTT picture (for example by showing which electrode switches in each case), showing that the polarity is not set by lead geometry or by the history of the barrier.

3. **Alternative switching mechanisms are not excluded: Joule heating near the 360 K transition, spin-orbit torque from the Pt leads, Oersted fields and thermoelectric effects.** The pulses are 1 ms long (up to 50 ms in Fig. 2d) at about 10–80 MA/cm² through a resistive barrier, dissipating about 0.5–2.4 mW in a 100 nm pillar in Fig. 2a and up to about 10 mW at 6 mA in Extended Data Fig. 1c. Millisecond pulses reach thermal steady state, and switching vanishes at 360 K, only about 65 K above the measurement temperature (Fig. 2c). Thermally assisted reordering of the free layer, with the final state chosen by an Oersted field, a thermal gradient, thermoelectric (Seebeck/Peltier) asymmetry between the electrodes, or exchange from the other layer, is a leading alternative and could also explain the polarity scatter of concern 2. In addition, 5 nm Pt sits directly against both PtMn3 layers, the bottom electrode carries in-plane current into and out of the pillar, and Pt/PtMn3 SOT switching is established in the authors' own work (ref. 13). The authors should: estimate the junction temperature during the pulse (e.g. resistance during the pulse against the R(T) calibration available from Fig. 2c); measure the switching current versus pulse width down to the ns regime, where heating is much smaller; pass in-plane current through the bottom lead only, without crossing the junction, to test for lead-SOT switching; check whether the switching current scales with pillar area (constant J) rather than lead current; compare stacks without Pt adjacent to PtMn3 (e.g. a light-metal seed giving (111) texture) or with varied Pt thickness; measure the switching current versus applied in-plane field (Oersted- or SOT-assisted switching should depend on it differently from STT); and compare switching at reduced base temperature.

4. **The OTT mechanism assumes coherent, k‖-conserving, sublattice-preserving tunnelling that the measured structure does not support.** The theory (p. 7; Fig. 3c–e) assumes "a crystalline AFMTJ operating in the ballistic transport regime" with conserved k‖ across epitaxially registered (111) kagome planes, and electrons that "preferentially retain their sublattice identity" between electrodes. Its key ingredient, the sublattice selectivity of conduction channels along Γ–K, depends on that coherence. The STEM (Fig. 1c,d, pp. 5–6) shows MgO without well-defined crystallization, visible roughness at the bottom interface, and polycrystalline PtMn3 with only predominant (111) texture and uncharacterized in-plane rotation between grains of the two electrodes. Across an amorphous barrier k‖ is not conserved, so the directional selectivity of Fig. 3 would largely average out; and for rotated grains there is no fixed correspondence between sublattice i in the fixed layer and in the free layer, so the β1 (intra) versus β2 (inter) distinction that drives the torque (Eqs. 5–8) is not defined grain by grain. The same issue applies to explaining a 363% TMR with such a barrier, larger than in epitaxial Mn3Pt AFMTJs (ref. 12). The authors should show that the mechanism survives these features—by a calculation in the k‖-non-conserving (diffusive or amorphous-barrier) limit, averaging over relative in-plane grain rotations, an explicit argument that the net staggered torque survives incoherent tunnelling, or structural evidence of in-plane grain registry across the barrier—or narrow the claim.

5. **The theory does not compute the torque, so "confirmed by our density functional theory calculations" (p. 4) is overstated.** The DFT gives bulk k‖-resolved conduction channels of PtMn3 (Fig. 3a,b; Eq. 3) and a schematic argument for "a select band" at a single k-point along Γ–K (Fig. 3c,d). No transmission through MgO and no torque is calculated, and β1, β2 and βeff = β1 − β2 integrated over the Brillouin zone are never evaluated; it is not shown that the net staggered polarization survives summation over all k‖. The LLG simulation sets βeff = 1, the maximum possible value, and η = 0.53, with no source (Extended Data Table 1). The simulation switches in about 3 ps at 10 MA/cm² at zero temperature, gives no critical current, and is not compared with the ms-scale, thermally activated switching of Fig. 2d, so the statement that it is "consistent with the experimental observations" is not quantitative. The authors should compute β1 and β2, or better the sublattice-resolved non-equilibrium torque for a PtMn3/MgO/PtMn3 (111) junction including interfaces (e.g. NEGF/Kubo; at minimum, integrate the k‖-resolved channels over the BZ), use the resulting βeff and η in finite-temperature simulations, and compare the predicted critical current and its pulse-width dependence with Fig. 2d. Otherwise the theory should be presented as a plausible mechanism rather than a confirmation.

6. **The chemical order, composition, texture and magnetic state of the device PtMn3 are not characterized.** The noncollinear T1(g) octupole state and its 360 K transition (used as evidence on p. 7) belong to chemically ordered L12 Mn3Pt near 3:1 stoichiometry. The device layers are deposited at room temperature without annealing (Methods), which usually gives a chemically disordered A1 phase with a different magnetic structure. The only structural evidence is local FFTs of two grains (Fig. 1d) indexing fundamental (111) spots, which do not distinguish L12 from A1; superlattice (100)/(110) reflections would. The composition of the device PtMn3 is not reported, and the NV sample is a different film (concern 12). The 360 K disappearance in Fig. 2c is the only magnetic evidence for the device films. Since both the octupole assignment and the k‖-conserving mechanism (concern 4) depend on crystal symmetry, the authors should provide, for the device stack itself: XRD or electron-diffraction evidence of L12 superlattice order with an order parameter; composition (EDS/RBS/XRF/XPS); wafer-level texture (rocking curves or pole figures, including the in-plane variant distribution); interface intermixing or oxidation across the MgO (EELS/EDS); and a magnetic or magnetotransport signature of noncollinear order in the unpatterned stack, such as the anomalous Hall effect in a Hall bar of the same PtMn3/Pt or the temperature dependence of the transition.

B. Should address

7. **Which PtMn3 layer is free and which is fixed is not established.** Neither layer is exchange-pinned or part of a synthetic structure; they have the same composition and differ only in thickness (7 nm bottom, 10 nm top; ΔV differs by about 1.4) and interface quality. In a symmetric STT picture both layers receive torques of opposite sense. The barrier analysis (Extended Data Fig. 3b, "100 nm × 7 nm") assumes the 7 nm bottom layer is free, while the LLG model uses d = 10 nm for the free layer (Extended Data Table 1). The authors should state which layer they believe switches and give evidence—for example a thickness series or strongly asymmetric electrode thicknesses, or setting each layer independently by field or SOT—and explain why the other layer does not switch at similar current density when its anisotropy is the same in the model. This bears directly on the polarity scatter (concern 2).

8. **The energy-barrier analysis (Fig. 2d, Extended Data Fig. 3) is ambiguous, and its agreement with theory is circular.** The two branches in Fig. 2d have clearly different slopes and intercepts: reading the fitted lines gives roughly Jc0 ≈ 190 MA/cm² and Δ ≈ 25–30 kT for AP→P, and Jc0 ≈ 130 MA/cm² and Δ ≈ 45–55 kT for P→AP; the quoted 39 kT appears to be an average, and the asymmetry is not discussed. Taken at face value, the AP→P line reaches J ≈ 0 near ln(tp/t0) ≈ 26 (about 0.2 s), implying an AP state that relaxes spontaneously in about a second, at odds with "moderately large thermal stability". Δ depends on the assumed t0 = 1 ps (rather than the ~1 ns usual for ferromagnets), which is not justified. The Koch et al. model (ref. 50) assumes a monodomain free layer with a barrier reduced linearly by STT and no heating, which conflicts with the multidomain/multilevel switching and ~100–170 nm domains the authors invoke and with likely heating from ms, mW pulses. The device (diameter) and the error-bar definition (number of repetitions, standard deviation or range) are not given. Most importantly, Methods (p. 23) states that Keff = 1 kJ/m³ was obtained by "matching the numerics to Δ = 39 kT", so the claim on p. 7 that 39 kT is "consistent with our theoretical estimation" is circular. This Keff is also about three orders of magnitude below the single-ion anisotropy expected for L12 Mn3X (ref. 51) and differs from Ku = 10 kJ/m³ in the switching simulation. The authors should report Δ for each branch with fit uncertainties and t0 sensitivity, identify the device and show more than one device, and either derive Keff independently or remove the claim of agreement.

The authors should give a table and plot of Jc (both polarities) against diameter and RA for all switching devices, with medians and spread, state how J was computed (nominal versus SEM-measured area), and revise the headline number.

10. **The TMR values and the "record-high" claim rest on one device and on current-defined states only.** Fig. 2b shows 23 of 36 devices below 100%; the 363% comes from a single device and the Fig. 2c device shows about 50–55%. The read bias, the TMR bias dependence, the series lead and electrode resistance in the two-terminal geometry, and each device's RA are not reported. 11. **The temperature evidence (Fig. 2c) is too sparse to tie switching to the noncollinear–collinear transition.** Fig. 2c is one device at four temperatures (300, 330, 360, 400 K), with nothing between 330 and 360 K. Switching currents at 330 K are not compared with 300 K; R_P(T) and R_AP(T) are not shown; it is not shown whether the TMR itself (e.g. of field-set states) vanishes at 360 K or only the ability to switch; and the transition temperature of these films is not measured independently. Irreversible changes after cycling to 400 K (barrier degradation, interdiffusion) could also remove the hysteresis, and loss of a metastable high state at 360 K is also consistent with a small Δ; if the pillar heats past ~360 K during pulses at room temperature, switching may be heat-assisted. The authors should show that switching and TMR return on cooling to 300 K, give a finer series of Jc(T) and TMR(T) on several devices (Jc should fall steeply approaching 360 K if heating drives switching; compare with the expected scaling of the octupole order parameter), measure the film's transition independently (magnetometry or AHE), and estimate the pulse temperature.

12. **The NV-magnetometry domain argument uses an unrepresentative film, lacks controls, and is contradicted by the device data.** The NV sample (Extended Data Fig. 2; Methods p. 19) is co-sputtered epitaxial (100) PtMn3 on MgO(100) annealed at 850 °C, differing in orientation, growth and microstructure from the unannealed polycrystalline (111) device layers grown on Pt, so its domain sizes cannot be carried over.
