A. Must address

1. The headline domain height of ≈20 nm (19 ± 2 nm) seems to be set mainly by the fixed final FM volume fraction and the scenario assumption, not measured independently by the sonography. In scenario III the Methods impose D_FM(t) = V*_FM, with V*_FM = 0.46 fixed from the integrated Bragg intensities. With d_layer = 44 nm this already gives ≈20 nm, or ≈18 nm if the 5.5 nm "inactive" bottom layer is excluded (44 − 5.5 ≈ 38.5 nm). The χ² minimisation in Fig. 4c is then over V*_FM, the same quantity, and the quoted ±2 nm also comes from the χ²(V*_FM) curvature. Fig. S2 varies the domain height directly, but only at t = 9 ps. So the sonography appears to choose among discrete parameterisations (I–V), and the number then follows from the volume fraction plus Eqs. (2)–(3); once a continuous near-surface layer is assumed, the "out-of-plane domain size of 19 nm" is largely a restatement of the measured FM fraction. This matters because the abstract claims that modelling the strain response "identifies the nucleation of domains of approximately 20 nm height", which requires the height to be a fitted quantity constrained by the strain signatures. The authors should fit D_FM and A_FM (with V_FM = D_FM·A_FM) as independent parameters over the full sonogram (all delays), ideally together with the depth position of the FM region, and also fit the strain dynamics alone, ignoring integrated intensity. They should show the two-dimensional χ²(D_FM, A_FM) map with its confidence region, and state explicitly which numbers the strain pulse determines independently and which follow from V*_FM and the scenario choice.

2. The statistical basis for preferring scenario III over I and IV is not established. In Fig. 3a,c,d the deviations (grey areas) of scenarios I, III and IV look similar in size. In Fig. 4c the minima of the global residuals for III, IV and I differ by only roughly 10–20% (≈0.085 versus ≈0.09–0.10). The residual χ² = Σ|ΔI|² is unweighted, with no noise model and no count of degrees of freedom, so it is not a reduced χ² and its differences cannot be read as significance. Scenario IV is also represented by a single arbitrary choice, D_FM = 1.3 V*_FM ("As an example, we chose…", Fig. 3 caption and Methods), rather than a scan over the mixed family between D_FM/V*_FM = 1 (scenario III, A_FM → 1) and 1/V*_FM (scenario I). Yet the text calls the comparison "quantitative" and says III "optimally describes" the data, and the central claim (near-surface columns merging into a continuous ~19 nm layer) rests on this discrimination. If the χ² difference between III and IV (or I) is within what noise, the 10% intensity threshold or the uncertainty in the fixed model inputs can produce, neither the out-of-plane-versus-mixed conclusion nor the domain height is determined. The authors should weight χ² by the measured shot-to-shot, train-to-train or delay-to-delay uncertainty in I(t, q_z) (e.g. from the unpumped references or repeated delays, i0-normalised), report reduced χ² values with confidence intervals, and give a model-selection criterion (likelihood ratio or information criterion, accounting for different numbers of effective parameters). They should scan D_FM continuously instead of using the factor 1.3 (or justify that factor), state whether III, IV and I differ significantly at 7.7 mJ cm⁻² and at each fluence of Fig. S4, and plot residual maps ΔI(t, q_z) for each scenario, alongside Fig. S1, to show which delays and features actually discriminate between them.

3. The extracted domain height may be degenerate with the optical penetration depth, which was tuned in the model. The Methods state that "only the optical penetration depth is optimized to account for the different incident angle". In scenario III, D_FM is set by the depth to which the excitation exceeds the transition threshold; the text says so itself ("D_FM can be approximated by the depth until which the optical excitation overcomes the equilibrium transition threshold"). The thermal stress that launches the strain pulse follows the same absorption profile. The fitted penetration depth and the fitted 19 nm FM depth are therefore not independent: both shape the same near-surface strain profile. Without separating them, the claim that the sonogram measures a "domain height", as distinct from the absorption depth that the model already encodes, is not established. The authors should state the optimised penetration depth and how it was obtained, including whether it was optimised under the same scenario that is then selected; compare it with the value expected from FeRh optical constants at 800 nm and ~25° incidence; and show a two-dimensional χ² map over (penetration depth, D_FM), or at least the χ² minimum in D_FM and the III/IV ranking for several fixed penetration depths.

4. The ±2 nm uncertainty leaves out model systematics, and the scenario discrimination depends on fixed or ad hoc inputs whose influence is not tested. The quoted error reflects only the χ² curvature in V*_FM (Fig. 4c). The model fixes the nucleation time constant τ = 8 ps (from earlier work), the 1.32 GPa transformation stress applied instantaneously at nucleation, the sound velocity of 5.0 nm ps⁻¹ (ref. 44, assumed equal in both phases), Grüneisen parameters and the two-temperature model, and unchanged thermoelastic coupling in the FM phase. It also assumes the bottom 5.5 nm of FeRh does not contribute to the Bragg peak, "for an overall better agreement". Each of these shifts strain-pulse transit times and hence D_FM (Eq. 1): a 5–10% change in v_s alone corresponds to ~1–2 nm. The dead layer changes both the effective thickness and the acoustic path that separates surface from backside nucleation (III versus V, I versus III). The Methods say the conclusions "do not depend decisively" on these details but show no evidence. The authors should vary each input within its literature or calibration uncertainty (dead layer 0 to ~8 nm; τ including the slower ~50 ps component; transformation stress; v_s and density in the FM phase), refit, recompute χ² for I, III and IV, and report a systematic error budget alongside the statistical one. Table S1 should mark which parameters were free and which fixed in each fit, and the abstract's "approximately 20 nm" should carry the combined uncertainty.

B. Should address

5. Lateral fluence inhomogeneity within the X-ray footprint. The 50 µm X-ray focus at 21.9° incidence covers ≈50/sin 21.9° ≈ 130–135 µm along the beam, comparable to the 136 × 142 µm FWHM pump spot, which is itself elongated on the sample at ≈25° incidence. The probed region therefore spans a sizable range of local fluence, so V*_FM and the threshold depth vary across the footprint. An incoherent sum over this fluence distribution produces an in-plane distribution of D_FM that resembles the mixed scenarios (A_FM < 1), broadens the Bragg peaks, and could bias the single D_FM extracted. It bears directly on the low-fluence assignment to scenario IV (A*_FM = 0.66 at 3.9 mJ cm⁻²), which could simply reflect footprint edges below threshold, and on the interpretation as a laterally heterogeneous transition temperature. The authors should report the footprint and projected pump-spot geometry with orientations, state whether 7.7 mJ cm⁻² is peak or footprint-averaged fluence, clarify how the 3.9–11.7 mJ cm⁻² range was calculated from 1–3 µJ at 25° incidence, estimate the fluence spread over the probed area, and include the footprint-weighted fluence distribution in the model (e.g. a fluence-averaged version of scenario III with D_FM varying with local fluence), or show that it is negligible.

6. Sample integrity, the 5.5 nm dead layer, and reproducibility. The Methods attribute the non-contributing bottom 5.5 nm to "interface diffusion of FeRh and W due to too strong laser excitations at a high-repetition rate", so the sample was altered irreversibly by the measurement itself. The paper shows no static diffraction or reflectivity before and after the beamtime and no check that the response was stable during acquisition. Interdiffusion could also make the FM fraction or transition temperature depth-dependent near the substrate, which bears on the I/III/V discrimination, and the assumption directly sets the film geometry and acoustic reflection times. With only one sample, these checks are the only evidence that the result is not tied to a damaged region. The authors should give evidence for the 5.5 nm figure (post-mortem XRR or TEM, or a fit with this thickness free), say whether the sonograms used were recorded before or after the damage and in which order fluences were measured, show reproducibility (same fluence at start and end, repeated delay scans, a fresh spot for 7.7 mJ cm⁻²), check whether the static Bragg peaks (position, width, AFM/FM ratio before time zero) drifted, and say whether the top Pt interface is also affected.

7. Phase-boundary acoustics and phase-dependent material properties are neglected without quantitative justification. The model treats the AFM/FM boundary as acoustically transparent and ignores phase-dependent sound velocity, density, acoustic impedance, electron–phonon coupling, thermal expansion and any electronic (non-thermal) stress that differs between phases. The authors argue these are small in FeRh (refs. 40, 44) but give no numbers. A phase-dependent v_s or elastic constant would change the transit times in Eq. (1) and produce partial reflections at the phase boundary, exactly where D_FM is read out and the kind of feature that separates the scenarios. The authors should quote the literature differences in elastic constants between phases, estimate the reflection coefficient at the AFM/FM interface and its effect on the modelled peak positions, and show at least one model run that includes these effects.

8. In-plane clamping of finite columns and the FM reference lattice constant. The FM strain is referenced to q_z = 4.196 Å⁻¹, "the expected lattice constant of the FM phase in case of a vanishing in-plane expansion", so the full 0.6% volume change goes into c, and the nucleation stress is modelled as a 1D expansive stress. For ~20 nm-high columns about 25 nm wide (the mosaic block size) embedded in an AFM matrix, in-plane clamping is partial, so the out-of-plane expansion lies between the uniaxial and isotropic limits. Partial relaxation changes the static FM out-of-plane lattice parameter, the stress launched into the film, and therefore the FM peak position, which the paper treats as a sensitive measure of D_FM (Fig. 4a discussion). The 1D model also cannot capture lateral strain coupling between neighbouring columns narrower than the film thickness; whether different lateral positions in the incoherent average are treated as acoustically independent is not stated. The authors should discuss the expected or observed in-plane relaxation (static data from ref. 30), show how the selected scenario and D_FM change for a partially relaxed FM reference and transformation stress, state the acoustic-independence assumption, and back the claim that sonography "is not necessarily sensitive to the shape of the domains" with a calculation for at least one non-columnar or partially relaxed geometry. The sensitivity of Fig. 2c to the FM reference choice should be noted.

9. Nucleation kinetics: a single, depth-independent time constant is assumed while the Discussion invokes depth-dependent pathways. Eq. (3) and the model fix τ = 8 ps everywhere, with the stress appearing instantaneously upon nucleation and randomly distributed nucleation delays. The Discussion, however, explains depth-dependent rise times from earlier UXRD work by an 8 ps and a 50 ps pathway (ref. 37) acting at different depths. If slower nucleation occurs deeper, D_FM(t) is no longer the simple form assumed in scenarios I–V and the fitted depth over 0–35 ps could be biased. The authors should refit τ, test a two-component or depth-dependent nucleation rate, and show the conclusion holds for kinetic models other than a single exponential; otherwise they should temper the claim that the data "relate" to these pathways.

10. The technique's own validity condition is only marginally met, which limits the "direct" and time-resolved claims. The Discussion says sonography requires the heterogeneity "to change slower than the strain pulse propagation". Here the transit time is 44 nm / 5 nm ps⁻¹ ≈ 8–9 ps, while the FM phase forms with τ = 8 ps; the strain pulse is launched by the same excitation that drives the transition, and the analysis focuses on t ≈ 9 ps (Fig. 4a), when the FM fraction is still growing. The simple decoding of Eq. (1) (d_phase = Δt_phase/v_s) therefore cannot be used directly, and the result rests entirely on the forward model and its kinetic assumptions (concerns 4 and 9). D_FM(t) and A_FM(t) are set analytically by the model rather than extracted. The authors should state this limitation clearly in the main text, discuss how far it limits the picosecond time resolution claimed in the abstract, and temper statements such as "directly quantifies the transient out-of-plane dimension".

11. No independent validation of the phase-localisation step. All results come from one 44 nm film in one geometry. The thermoelastic parameters were calibrated in earlier experiments (refs. 18, 29) at different incidence angles and possibly different sample conditions given the damage in concern 6, and that calibration tests the thermoelastic model, not the localisation. Even the lowest fluence in Fig. S3 (3.9 mJ cm⁻²) is above threshold, so there is no matched below-threshold control. The authors should, if possible, add a sub-threshold sonogram from the same run, a measurement on a second FeRh thickness (e.g. thicker by a larger margin than the penetration depth, to test whether D_FM tracks the excitation depth rather than film-specific structure), or a structure with a known two-phase or two-lattice depth profile (a bilayer, or a static thermal-equilibrium coexistence state). At minimum they should run a synthetic recovery test: generate sonograms from known D_FM/A_FM, add realistic noise, and show that the fitting procedure recovers the input and separates III from IV.

13. Early-delay FM strain: missing error bars, fit bias and the 10% threshold. The FM strain is shown only once the FM peak exceeds 10% of the integrated intensity (Fig. 2c), yet the scenario comparison in Fig. 3 relies strongly on the 4–10 ps window, where the FM peak is weak, overlaps the AFM peak, and pseudo-Voigt fits may be biased; the earliest delays are also where nucleation-depth information should be strongest. The strain transients in Fig. 2c and Fig. 3 have no error bars. 14. Heat accumulation within the pulse train and the effective initial temperature. Six pump pulses at 56.25 kHz (17.8 µs spacing) in 10 Hz trains are averaged, from a 300 K base temperature with T_c ≈ 370 K. Residual heating from earlier pulses would raise the initial temperature and change V*_FM and the threshold depth. The unpumped references and Fig. S7 are cited only as showing "negligible fluctuations". The authors should estimate the inter-pulse quasi-static temperature rise and show the sonogram, or at least the static peak position and V*_FM, separately for each pulse index in the burst. Fig. 2c also shows a slightly negative AFM strain before and near t = 0, which should be explained (reference-position issue or residual heating).

15. The scenario set leaves out a time-dependent domain depth. Except in scenario I, where a continuous layer grows into the depth, D_FM is constant once nucleated. A natural alternative is columns whose height grows with time, e.g. FM-phase propagation at a finite front velocity from a near-surface nucleus. The conclusion that the depth is set by the excitation profile "rather than a propagation of the FM phase into the depth" requires testing such a model explicitly. The 35 ps window is also short compared with the 50 ps component mentioned, limiting sensitivity to slow depth growth; this should be stated.
