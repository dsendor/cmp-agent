Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors use an on-chip picosecond photoconductive-switch transport platform to drive ~2 ps current pulses through 20 nm NbN and ~50 nm YBa2Cu3O7 films. The idea is that vortex motion and self-heating are too slow to act on this timescale, so the intrinsic depairing current J_c* becomes measurable. In NbN they report a sharp drop in the transmitted pulse at ≈2.2 J_c (7 K), and its temperature dependence matches a Landau-type estimate and a dirty-limit Usadel/BCS simulation. In YBCO they see a gradual suppression, which they attribute to the d-wave gap. Directly measuring the depairing current in transport is a long-standing goal, and a clean, general method for it would interest a broad audience. The central inferences, though (that the threshold is depairing and not vortex or thermal dissipation, the absolute current calibration, and the d-wave attribution), need stronger support than the paper now gives.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The experiment is new and technically demanding: calibrated incident, reflected and transmitted picosecond pulses on superconducting films, with reference data in the normal state (20 K, 110 K) taken in the same geometry.
- The NbN result is clear and striking (Fig. 4a,b). Transmission is flat up to ~2 J_c and then collapses abruptly. The local maximum in J_T,peak versus J_IN,peak (Fig. 4b inset) gives an operational definition of a threshold current.
- The threshold has a different temperature dependence from the d.c. J_c (Fig. 5a), which argues that it is not simply the pinning-limited J_c.
- The s-wave/d-wave contrast is qualitatively suggestive, and the microscopic dirty-limit simulation (Fig. 5b) reproduces the sharpness of the NbN drop.
- The method could be applied to other superconductors, including light-driven or metastable states (refs. 27, 29).

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. **The vortex-exclusion argument ignores the self-field of the pulse and edge current crowding.** The Discussion rules out vortices on two grounds: zero-field cooling below 1 µT, and a maximum vortex displacement of "a few tens of nanometres". Neither addresses the relevant mechanism.
   - At the threshold the pulse carries J ≈ 220 GA m⁻² through a 10 µm × 20 nm cross-section, so I ≈ 44 mA. In a thin strip whose width far exceeds the Pearl length, this current produces a large self-field concentrated at the edges, and the Meissner current density there is much higher than the cross-sectional average J = I/S used throughout the paper.
   - Vortex entry over the edge barrier is set by this local edge current density, not by the ambient field. Zero-field cooling therefore does not exclude pulse-induced vortex entry.
   - Vortices entering at the edges need to move only ~ξ to tens of nm to create a dissipative edge layer. Fast vortex chains and phase-slip lines (Larkin–Ovchinnikov-type instability, refs. 7, 17, 23) are known to produce resistive responses on short timescales in NbN.
   - The text is also internally inconsistent: vortex speeds of "tens of nanometres per picosecond" (introduction) over a ~2 ps pulse with longer tails (Fig. 3) give displacements comparable to the dissipative length scales that matter.

   The authors should:
   - estimate the transverse current distribution in the strip (Pearl length at 7 K, edge-to-centre current ratio) and state whether the reported J_c* is an average or a local value;
   - compare the edge current density with the Bean–Livingston/edge-barrier entry criterion;
   - ideally, measure strips of different widths (e.g. 1, 3 and 10 µm). If J_c* is intrinsic, the average J_c* should become width-independent once the width is below the Pearl length. A vortex or edge-entry threshold would scale differently.

   Without this, attributing the threshold to depairing rather than edge vortex entry is not secure.

2. **Depairing and heating-driven switching are not separated, and the dynamics after the threshold are not shown.** The evidence for "instantaneous depairing" is the transmitted peak amplitude (Fig. 4a,b) and the resemblance of the 6.5 J_c waveform to the 20 K waveform (Fig. 3c). An abrupt drop in transmitted amplitude can also come from runaway heating within the pulse, such as a hotspot or a thermal instability seeded at the leading edge. NbN is the archetypal fast hotspot material: electron–phonon times are ~10 ps, and nonequilibrium quasiparticles form in a few ps. The paper does not:
   - estimate the energy dissipated per pulse once J > J_c*, or the resulting electron temperature in a 20 nm film;
   - show whether the 515 nm launching beam or its scattered light reaches the NbN strip;
   - show any time-resolved recovery of the superconducting response.

   The authors should do a double-pulse experiment: a strong pulse followed by a weak probe pulse at variable delay. The recovery timescale would separate pair breaking plus quasiparticle recombination from heat escape to the substrate, and would show whether the strong-pulse state is a hot normal state or a nonthermal depaired state. They should also give an energy-balance estimate for the 2.2–6.5 J_c range, and show that the threshold does not depend on the 50 kHz repetition rate or average laser power on the switches.

3. **The absolute current calibration, which sets the headline factor of 2.2, has no uncertainty.** J is obtained from E·w = S·J·Z0 with Z0 ≈ 50 Ω, and the absolute field scale comes from a calibration described only in Supplementary Information section 5. This raises several questions:
   - Is the absolute photoconductive sampling-switch response known to better than a factor of ~2?
   - Is the uniform cross-sectional current density S = w·t valid, given the thin-film current distribution in concern 1?
   - Does the impedance discontinuity at the NbN section and the ~3 Ω contacts change the relation between the measured transmitted field and the current inside the film?

   Fig. 5a, the only quantitative comparison with theory, has no error bars on either J_c or J_c*. The "≈2.2 J_c" ratio and the "excellent agreement" with the calculated J_c*(T) both depend on this calibration.

   The authors should give the full calibration chain with uncertainties, show error bars in Fig. 5a, and cross-check the picosecond current scale against an independent reference. For example, they could check that the normal-state 20 K transmission and reflection amplitudes match the d.c. normal-state resistance (~150 Ω in Fig. 2a) through transmission-line theory.

B. Should address

4. **The analytic J_c* model contradicts the simulation used to support it.** In Fig. 5a the J_c*(T) calculation uses the clean-limit Landau criterion, ħk_F·v_s = Δ, giving J_c* = 2ħe n_s/(πmξ). But the paper itself treats NbN as strongly disordered: the BCS simulation is done "in the dirty limit relying on the Usadel equation". In the dirty limit the depairing current is set by the Kupriyanov–Lukichev/Usadel pair-breaking parameter, not by a quasiparticle Doppler shift equal to Δ, and its temperature dependence and prefactor differ. The Discussion's conclusion that the threshold "originates from the competition between quasiparticle energy shift and the superconducting gap" therefore does not follow from the dirty-limit model that reproduces the data.

   The authors should:
   - state which n_s(T), ξ(T) and effective mass were used, and whether any were adjusted to match the data;
   - compare the data with the dirty-limit Kupriyanov–Lukichev J_dp(T) computed from the measured resistivity, Tc and diffusion constant;
   - reconcile the physical picture in the Discussion with that model.

5. **The microscopic simulation has fitted parameters, and its agreement is only partial.** The Dynes broadening is "set to guarantee numerical convergence and to reproduce the experimental results". The simulation also fails to reproduce the tail above J_c*, which is attributed to unmodelled relaxation channels. It is unclear which features of Fig. 5b are predictions and which are fits. The authors should:
   - list the free parameters;
   - show how sensitive the threshold position and sharpness are to the Dynes parameter;
   - state whether the simulation predicts the absolute value of J_c*, or only the shape once the current axis is normalized to J_c.

   The comparison with tDGL is not a fair discriminant if tDGL is used outside its range of validity at 7 K. The claim that the sharpness is a fingerprint of s-wave dirty-limit BCS should rest on a prediction that a plausible alternative does not reproduce.

6. **The YBCO result does not establish that a depairing threshold is absent.** The conclusion that d-wave superconductors have "no well-defined intrinsic current density threshold" rests on one temperature (50 K, T/Tc ≈ 0.59; NbN is at T/Tc ≈ 0.47). It also rests on a drive range that may stay far below the YBCO depairing current. The estimated J_dp for YBCO at 50 K is of order 10³ GA m⁻², but Fig. 4d reaches only J_T ≈ 200 GA m⁻² and Fig. 4c only ~8 J_c.

   The gradual suppression could instead come from:
   - the nonlinear Meissner effect, a nodal d-wave effect in the kinetic inductance below depairing;
   - twin boundaries or weak links in a twinned film on CeO2-buffered sapphire;
   - vortex entry, since λ is larger and the edge barrier weaker in YBCO;
   - thermal smearing at the higher reduced temperature.

   The authors should:
   - show YBCO at lower temperature (e.g. 10–20 K) and NbN at comparable reduced temperature (T/Tc ≈ 0.6–0.8). This would test whether NbN stays sharp and YBCO stays gradual at matched T/Tc.
   - state the estimated YBCO J_c* relative to the maximum current reached;
   - characterize the YBCO film's twinning and grain structure. The word "monocrystalline" in the main text needs support.

   Also, the YBCO data used for the main-text claim appear in Fig. 4c,d, but the device and calibration details are in Supplementary Information section 6, so the reader cannot check whether the geometry is the same.

7. **Only one device per material is shown, and J_c*(T) rests on four points.** The paper does not say how many NbN and YBCO devices were measured, whether the ≈2.2 J_c ratio reproduces across devices or cooldowns, or what the spread is. Fig. 5a has four temperatures with no error bars. The authors should report J_c* and J_c for at least two to three devices per material, and add points closer to Tc, where the clean-limit and dirty-limit predictions for J_c*(T) differ most.

8. **Only part of the measured information is used.** The analysis relies on peak amplitudes alone. The full incident, reflected and transmitted waveforms (Fig. 3c) contain the time-dependent complex impedance, which would show directly whether the response at threshold is a collapse of kinetic inductance (loss of superfluid density) or a rise in resistance.

   The 7 K reflected pulse also has an inductive shape (Fig. 3b). The current dependence of L_kin below J_c* is a standard, model-specific signature of how n_s is suppressed by supercurrent. In s-wave theory it should rise in a characteristic, weak way before the abrupt drop, which would itself test the depairing interpretation.

   The authors should extract L_kin(J) and R(J) from the waveforms for J below and above J_c*, for both materials.

9. **The fold-back in Fig. 4a and the time structure within the pulse need clarifying.** The "slight leftward tilt" and the local maximum in J_T,peak (Fig. 4b inset) mean that the transmitted peak current falls as the drive increases. If depairing happens at the instant J reaches J_c* on the rising edge, the transmitted peak is set by when in the pulse the collapse occurs. The quoted J_c* would then depend on the pulse rise time and on how depairing is defined.

   The authors should explain how J_c* is extracted (the maximum of J_T,peak, the onset of the drop, or something else). They should also check, for example by varying the pulse width or slew rate if possible, that the threshold does not depend on pulse duration as a kinetic or heating threshold would.

C. Minor

10. The paper gives two different vortex-displacement estimates: "tens of nanometres per picosecond" in the introduction and "a few tens of nanometres" in total in the Discussion. These should be made consistent, and the vortex velocities and pulse duration used should be stated explicitly.
11. The introduction says H_c "is not experimentally accessible". The thermodynamic critical field of a type-II superconductor is routinely obtained by integrating the magnetization curve or from specific heat, so this statement should be softened.
12. Fig. 4a,c: the caption says filled circles and squares mark positive and negative pulse polarity, but they cannot be told apart in the figure. Please state whether the threshold is the same for both polarities, which would rule out rectification or thermoelectric artefacts.
13. Fig. 4b caption: "starts dropping at ~220 GA m⁻²" appears with a minus sign or dash ("-220"). Please clarify the sign convention. Also give the d.c. J_c used for normalization (Fig. 2a gives "approximately 100 GA m⁻²", while Fig. 5a suggests ≈110 GA m⁻² at 7 K).
14. Fig. 2: define how J_c is extracted from the R(J) curves (resistance criterion), and say whether the d.c. measurements used pulsed or continuous current, since self-heating affects J_c.
15. Methods: the sputtered NbN on MgO at 350 °C is called monocrystalline in the main text. Please give the XRD or TEM evidence (in Supplementary Information section 1) in the main text, or soften the wording.
16. The statement that the platform could support "generating ultrashort, strong magnetic field pulses" and "superconducting electronics operating closer to intrinsic current limits" needs qualifying. It requires knowing how fast the depaired state recovers, which is not shown.
17. Typographical error: "normal sate" (page 2).

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Measure NbN strips of several widths (above and below the Pearl length) and model the transverse current distribution, to separate intrinsic depairing from edge vortex entry.
2. Do a strong-pulse/weak-probe experiment to measure how the superconducting response recovers after a supra-threshold pulse, and add an energy-balance estimate of heating.
3. Compare the NbN J_c*(T) with the dirty-limit Kupriyanov–Lukichev prediction from independently measured parameters, with error bars and calibration uncertainty included.
4. Extend the YBCO measurements to low temperature and to currents approaching the estimated d-wave depairing current, and extract L_kin(J) for both materials to test the nonlinear-Meissner (nodal) behaviour directly.
