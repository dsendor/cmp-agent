Review outline:

**1. Significance and novelty**

The authors use on-chip picosecond current pulses from photoconductive switches to drive NbN and YBa2Cu3O7 (YBCO) thin films far above their d.c. critical current density J_c. The pulses act on timescales too short for vortex entry and macroscopic Joule heating, so the films can be driven towards the intrinsic depairing current J_c*. In NbN they report a sharp drop in transmitted supercurrent at about 2.2 J_c (about 220 GA m^-2 at 7 K), with a temperature dependence that agrees with a microscopic estimate and a dirty-limit Usadel simulation. In YBCO they report a gradual suppression, which they attribute to the d-wave gap. Probing depairing directly in wide films, rather than in nanowires, would be useful both for superconducting physics and for applications, and the platform is novel. The quantitative claims, however, depend on the absolute current calibration, on the current distribution across the strip, and on how the transmission drop is interpreted. The d-wave conclusion in particular is not yet supported.

**2. Potential reasons for acceptance**

- The experimental approach is new and elegant. On-chip picosecond transport on a coplanar waveguide allows current densities several times J_c in a 10 µm wide film without destroying the sample.
- The NbN data are clear. Fig. 4a,b show a linear superconducting regime, an abrupt transition, and a linear regime whose slope matches the 20 K normal state. The response is symmetric for both current polarities.
- J_c and J_c* in NbN are tracked over temperature (Fig. 5a), and the two scale very differently, which is consistent with pinning-limited J_c and intrinsic J_c*.
- A microscopic (Usadel/BCS) simulation of the transmission (Fig. 5b) reproduces the sharp drop better than time-dependent Ginzburg–Landau (tDGL) theory, which gives a physically meaningful discriminator.
- The topic is of broad interest: intrinsic current limits, and spectroscopy of the gap structure by transport.

**3. Concerns**

**A. Must address**

1. **The d-wave interpretation of the YBCO data is not established, and the currents reached may lie below the expected depairing current.**
   - *What the paper says.* The Discussion states that in d-wave superconductors "J_c* is not a well-defined quantity and is therefore not observable", and the abstract says the gradual suppression in YBCO "reflect[s] its d-wave symmetry".
   - *The theory does not say this.* Microscopic treatments of supercurrent-carrying nodal superconductors (the nonlinear Meissner effect and depairing in d-wave BCS) still give a supercurrent J(v_s) that has a maximum, so a depairing current exists. Nodal quasiparticles add a low-v_s nonlinearity on top of it; they do not remove the threshold.
   - *The currents reached look too low.* With λ ≈ 150–250 nm and ξ ≈ 1.5–2 nm, the Ginzburg–Landau estimate for YBCO gives a depairing current of order 10^3 GA m^-2 at low temperature, and several hundred GA m^-2 at T/T_c ≈ 0.6. The largest transmitted current in Fig. 4d is about 230 GA m^-2, so the experiment may simply not reach J_c*. The high-current data in Fig. 4c have also not reached the normal-state reference.
   - *Alternative explanations are not excluded.*
     - Current crowding at the edges: a 50 nm film has a Pearl length 2λ²/d of roughly 1–2.5 µm, much smaller than the 10 µm width, so the edges reach depairing well before the average current density does.
     - Twin and grain boundaries and other inhomogeneity in YBCO grown on CeO2/sapphire. These films are called "monocrystalline" without supporting structural data.
     - The higher reduced temperature: 50 K/85 K ≈ 0.59, compared with 0.47 for NbN.
     - A mismatched normal-state reference: 110 K is not the normal state at 50 K, because YBCO's normal-state resistivity depends strongly on temperature.
   - *What to do.*
     - (i) Give an explicit estimate of J_c*(50 K) for YBCO from independently measured λ and ξ, and compare it with the currents reached.
     - (ii) Measure YBCO at lower T/T_c (for example 10–20 K), and ideally NbN at matched T/T_c ≈ 0.6–0.8. Fig. 5a suggests NbN data near 0.8 T_c exist; show whether its drop stays sharp there.
     - (iii) Model the frequency-dependent current distribution across the strip for both films.
     - (iv) Support the d-wave attribution with a d-wave microscopic calculation of the transmission, not only the qualitative tDGL in SI section 11.4.
   - Otherwise, the YBCO statement should be reduced to an observation.

2. **The absolute current density, and its distribution across the strip, are not established, yet both headline numbers depend on them.**
   - *How the current is obtained.* J(t) = E(t)·w/(S·Z_0), with Z_0 ≈ 50 Ω, S taken from a nominal "approximately 20 nm" thickness and a 10 µm width, and E calibrated through the photoconductive sampling switch (SI section 5).
   - *Why it matters.* The ratio J_c*/J_c ≈ 2.2 combines this optoelectronic calibration with a completely separate d.c. calibration. The agreement in Fig. 5a with the calculated J_c*(T) is absolute. Every uncertainty in Z_0 (not measured or simulated for the loaded line), in the thickness, and in the E-field calibration therefore enters directly.
   - *Current distribution.* At the frequencies in a roughly 2 ps pulse, current in a thin strip is not uniform. Even for NbN (λ ≈ 400 nm, so the Pearl length is about 16 µm) the edge current density can exceed the average. Depairing then starts at the edges, and the extracted "J_c*" is an average over a non-uniform profile.
   - *What to do.*
     - (i) Give a full error budget, and add error bars to Figs. 4b and 5a.
     - (ii) Measure or simulate Z_0 for the actual structure with the sample in place.
     - (iii) Measure the film thickness directly (X-ray reflectivity, TEM or AFM).
     - (iv) Compute the transverse current distribution at the relevant frequencies and state how it affects the extracted J_c*.
     - (v) Ideally, measure a narrower strip (w smaller than the Pearl length) to show that J_c* does not depend on width.

3. **The transmission drop has not been shown to be depairing rather than a nonlinear kinetic-inductance response, heating, or optical excitation.**
   - *Kinetic inductance.* The criterion for J_c* is a local maximum of the transmitted peak current (Fig. 4b inset). As J approaches J_c*, the kinetic inductance L_kin rises sharply. That alone increases reflection and lowers the transmitted peak, without any dissipation.
   - *Heating.* During the strong-drive pulse the film is resistive. Joule heating of the electron system within the pulse, and possibly residual heating from the 50 kHz train, can produce a similar drop and the slow "tail".
   - *Optical excitation.* Light from the 515 nm launching beam, scattered onto the NbN near the middle switch, would create quasiparticles.
   - *What to do.*
     - (i) Analyse the full waveforms of reflected and transmitted pulses (Fig. 3c shows only two drive levels) to extract the inductive and resistive parts of the sample impedance as a function of drive. Show that a resistive component appears at J_c*.
     - (ii) Vary the pulse duration or rise time. An intrinsic threshold should be independent of duration, whereas heating scales with deposited energy.
     - (iii) Add a control with the launching beam on and the switch bias off, to rule out optical quasiparticle injection.
     - (iv) Estimate the energy deposited per pulse and the resulting electron temperature, using the electron–phonon time of NbN.

**B. Should address**

4. **The microscopic J_c* formula mixes clean-limit and dirty-limit quantities, and it is not clear which inputs are fitted.**
   - The estimate J_c* = 2ħe n_s/(π m ξ) uses the clean-limit Landau criterion ħk_F·v_s = Δ and the BCS coherence length ξ = ħv_F/(πΔ). NbN, however, is a strongly disordered, dirty-limit superconductor: its measured ξ (about 5 nm, from H_c2) is the dirty Ginzburg–Landau length. The authors themselves use dirty-limit Usadel theory for Fig. 5b.
   - In the dirty limit, depairing is described by Kupriyanov–Lukichev, not by a Landau criterion.
   - The claimed "excellent agreement" in Fig. 5a depends on how n_s(T), ξ(T) and m were chosen (SI section 9).
   - *What to do.* State explicitly which parameters are independently measured and which are fitted. Compare with the Kupriyanov–Lukichev dirty-limit J_d(T) computed from measured λ(T), ρ_n and H_c2. Quantify the discrepancy near 0.8 T_c (roughly 20–30% in Fig. 5a) rather than calling the measured values "slightly smaller".

5. **The Usadel simulation is partly tuned to the data.**
   - The Dynes broadening is "set to guarantee numerical convergence and to reproduce the experimental results" (page 4). That makes it a fit parameter.
   - The simulation also fails to reproduce the high-current tail.
   - *What to do.*
     - Report the value of the Dynes parameter and compare it with tunnelling or optical estimates for NbN.
     - Show how sensitive the sharpness and position of the drop are to it.
     - Clarify which features of Fig. 5b are predictions and which are fitted.
     - Show the tDGL curve with the same freedom, so that the BCS-versus-tDGL discrimination is fair.

6. **Reproducibility across devices is not documented.**
   - The paper does not say how many NbN and YBCO devices were measured, or whether the 2.2 J_c threshold and the YBCO gradual response are reproduced across devices and cooldowns.
   - *What to do.* Report the number of devices, show the spread of J_c* (and of J_c*/J_c) between devices, and include at least a second YBCO device.

7. **Numbers in the figures do not appear consistent with each other.**
   - *NbN.* Fig. 4a extends to |J_T,peak|/J_c(7 K) ≈ 8. With J_c ≈ 100 GA m^-2 (text, Fig. 2a), that is about 800 GA m^-2, but Fig. 4b shows J_T,peak only up to about 550 GA m^-2.
   - *YBCO.* Fig. 4c extends to about 7 J_c(50 K), which is about 350 GA m^-2 for "slightly below 50 GA m^-2", while Fig. 4d reaches only about 230 GA m^-2.
   - *J_c(50 K).* Fig. 2b seems to place the 50 K transition nearer 55–60 GA m^-2 than below 50.
   - *What to do.* Reconcile the normalizations: say which J_c value is used in each panel and whether Figs. 4a and 4b come from the same data set.

8. **The context of prior depairing measurements is incomplete.**
   - Depairing-limited d.c. critical currents have been reached in narrow strips and nanowires (w below the Pearl length and ξ-scale constrictions; for example ref. 14 and related work), and pulsed and microwave measurements near J_d exist.
   - The statement that "direct transport measurements of J_c* remain challenging" should be set against this work, with a clear account of what the picosecond technique adds: wide films, and no requirement for nanopatterning.

9. **The "2.2 times J_c" headline is not an intrinsic quantity.**
   - The abstract leads with J_c*/J_c ≈ 2.2, but J_c is set by pinning and is sample-dependent, so the ratio is not a material property.
   - *What to do.* Lead with the absolute J_c*(T) and its ratio to the theoretical J_d.

**C. Minor**

10. Page 4 says that at 0.8 T_c, J_c is "nearly an order of magnitude smaller" than J_c*. Fig. 5a shows a factor of about 6. Please adjust the wording.

11. The vortex argument (displacement of a few tens of nanometres per pulse) assumes speeds of at most a few tens of km s^-1. Two points need comment:
    - Kinematic vortices and phase-slip lines can move faster.
    - Current crowding at the edges, together with the pulse self-field, may allow local vortex entry within a thin edge layer.

    State the self-field at the strip edge at J_c* and compare it with the edge-barrier entry field.

12. The claim that the YBCO and NbN films are monocrystalline (SI section 1) is important to the interpretation. Please show the X-ray diffraction or rocking-curve evidence and the twinning state in the main text or Methods.

13. Fig. 4a explains the "slight leftward tilt" by a reduced transmitted amplitude. A sketch of the transmitted-versus-incoming relation, with the multivalued region marked, would make this clearer. In Fig. 4b, mark the J_c* point used in Fig. 5a, and state how J_c* is defined at higher temperatures where the local maximum may be less pronounced.

14. Report the actual sample temperature under illumination, that is, the steady-state heating from the 50 kHz train chopped at 1 kHz. Comparing J_c with J_c* at a common temperature assumes this heating is negligible.

**4. Suggestions for improvement**

1. Add a geometry series (several strip widths, including w below the Pearl length) and a pulse-duration series. Together these would show that J_c* is intrinsic and independent of current crowding and heating.
2. Extend the YBCO measurements to low temperature and to higher drive, with a quantitative d-wave microscopic model. This would turn the gap-symmetry claim from qualitative to testable.
3. Extract the complex sample impedance from the full time-domain waveforms as a function of drive. This would separate the kinetic-inductance nonlinearity below J_c* from the onset of dissipation.
4. Provide an error budget for the current-density calibration, and compare J_c*(T) with the Kupriyanov–Lukichev dirty-limit J_d using independently measured λ(T) and H_c2(T).
