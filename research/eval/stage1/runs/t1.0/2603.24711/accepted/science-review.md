A. Must address

1. The paper does not show that the NbN threshold is current-driven depairing rather than heating, direct pair breaking by the pulse, or another ultrafast dissipative channel. The identification of the sharp drop at ≈2.2 J_c (≈220 GA m^-2 at 7 K) with J_c* rests on a single pulse width (~2 ps), an argument that vortices cannot move far enough, the transmitted peak amplitude (Fig. 4a,b), the resemblance of the 6.5 J_c waveform to the 20 K waveform (Fig. 3c), and agreement with a model. Several alternatives are not excluded. (i) Runaway heating within the pulse: once the film becomes resistive, Joule heating of the electron system, a hotspot or a thermal instability seeded at the leading edge can produce an equally abrupt drop and the slow post-threshold tail; NbN is the archetypal fast hotspot material (electron–phonon times ~10 ps, nonequilibrium quasiparticles within a few ps). (ii) Direct pair breaking by the pulse spectrum: a 2 ps pulse has spectral weight up to roughly 0.5–1 THz, while for NbN with T_c ≈ 15 K, 2Δ(0) ≈ 4.5 meV ≈ 1.1 THz, and 2Δ is smaller at 7 K; pair breaking by absorption of the high-frequency components and fast electron heating once quasiparticles form are known for intense THz fields in NbN (ref. 10). The authors should: show the pulse spectrum against 2Δ(T); measure the threshold as a function of pulse width or rise time (at least two widths), since an intrinsic depairing threshold should be insensitive to duration over a picosecond window and cross over to d.c. J_c only at the vortex/thermal timescale, whereas a threshold tracking deposited energy, fluence or spectral content near 2Δ points elsewhere; estimate the energy dissipated per pulse and the resulting electron temperature in the 20 nm film across the 2.2–6.5 J_c range; and perform a strong-pulse/weak-probe experiment at variable delay, whose recovery timescale would separate pair breaking plus quasiparticle recombination from heat escape to the substrate and show whether the strong-pulse state is a hot normal state or a nonthermal depaired state.

2. The vortex-exclusion argument ignores the self-field of the pulse and edge vortex entry. The Discussion (pp. 1813–1814) rules out vortices by zero-field cooling below 1 µT and by vortex speeds of "a few tens of km/s" giving displacements of "a few tens of nanometres". Neither addresses the relevant mechanism. At threshold the pulse carries I ≈ J_c* w d ≈ 2.2×10^11 A m^-2 × 10 µm × 20 nm ≈ 44 mA; in a thin strip this produces a large self-field concentrated at the edges, likely well above H_c1, and the edge current density exceeds the cross-sectional average. Vortex entry over the edge barrier is set by this local edge current density, not by the ambient field, so zero-field cooling does not exclude pulse-induced entry. Vortices entering at the edges need move only ~ξ to tens of nm to create a dissipative edge layer, and kinematic vortices, fast vortex chains and phase-slip lines (Larkin–Ovchinnikov-type instability, refs. 7, 17, 23) can move faster than assumed and produce resistive responses on short timescales in NbN. The authors should state the self-field and edge current density at J_c* and compare them with the Bean–Livingston/edge-barrier entry criterion; give a quantitative upper bound on the voltage that edge vortex motion over ~2 ps (and the longer tails in Fig. 3) could produce, compared with the ~140 Ω normal-state response; and preferably add a control under a known applied perpendicular field or after field cooling, with pre-existing vortices present. If the picosecond threshold is intrinsic it should be nearly field-insensitive while d.c. J_c is strongly suppressed, which would directly test the central premise. A strip-width series (concern 3) would also discriminate edge entry from depairing.

3. The absolute current density and its distribution across the strip are not established, yet the "2.2 J_c" headline and the absolute agreement with the calculated J_c*(T) in Fig. 5a depend on them. J(t) is obtained from the sampled photocurrent through E(t)·w = S·J(t)·Z_0 (p. 1812), with Z_0 ≈ 50 Ω (not measured or simulated for the loaded line), S from a nominal "approximately 20 nm" thickness and ≈10 µm width, and the absolute field scale from a calibration described only in SI section 5. The ratio J_c*/J_c combines this optoelectronic calibration with a completely separate d.c. calibration. Specific problems: (i) the sampling switch has a finite response time, so the measured peak of a ~2 ps pulse is a convolution and the true peak current is probably higher than J_T,peak; the bandwidth limit should be stated and its effect deconvolved or bounded; (ii) it is unclear whether the photoconductive sampling response is known to better than a factor of ~2; (iii) the impedance discontinuity at the NbN section and the ~3 Ω contacts may change the relation between the measured transmitted field and the current inside the film; (iv) at picosecond frequencies the current in a thin strip is not uniform: for NbN (λ ≈ 400 nm, Pearl length 2λ²/d ≈ 16 µm, comparable to the width) the edge current density can exceed the average, so depairing would start at the edges and the extracted "J_c*" is an average over a non-uniform profile (or lower than the local value); (v) Fig. 5a, the only quantitative comparison with theory, has no error bars on J_c or J_c*, so "slightly smaller" and "excellent agreement" cannot be judged. The authors should give the full calibration chain with an uncertainty budget and add error bars to Figs. 4b and 5a; measure or simulate Z_0 for the actual structure with the sample in place; measure the film thicknesses directly (X-ray reflectivity, TEM or AFM) and give exact strip dimensions with uncertainties for each device; compute the transverse current distribution at the relevant frequencies (Pearl length at 7 K, edge-to-centre ratio) and state whether J_c* is an average or a local value; cross-check the absolute scale independently, e.g. compare the 20 K normal-state transmission and reflection with the d.c. normal-state resistance of the same strip (Fig. 2a gives ~140–150 Ω, predicting T = 2Z_0/(2Z_0+R) ≈ 0.42; state whether this matches the 0.4 level in Figs. 3b and 4a), or use ns-long pulses where the switching current should revert to d.c. J_c; and ideally measure strips of several widths (e.g. 1, 3 and 10 µm, including w below the Pearl length) to show that the average J_c* becomes width-independent, as an intrinsic threshold should, while an edge-entry threshold would scale differently.

4. The d-wave interpretation of YBCO is not supported, yet it is half of the abstract's conclusion. The Discussion states that in d-wave superconductors "J_c* is not a well-defined quantity and is therefore not observable", and the abstract says the gradual suppression "reflect[s] its d-wave symmetry". (i) This goes beyond theory: microscopic treatments of supercurrent-carrying nodal superconductors still give a J(v_s) with a maximum, so a depairing (superflow-instability) current exists; nodal quasiparticles add a low-v_s nonlinearity (the nonlinear Meissner effect, reducing n_s by order J/J_dep) but do not remove the threshold. (ii) The currents reached look far below the YBCO depairing current: a Ginzburg–Landau estimate with λ ≈ 150–250 nm and ξ ≈ 1.5–2 nm gives ~10^3 GA m^-2 at low temperature and still several hundred GA m^-2 at T/T_c ≈ 0.6, while Fig. 4d reaches only J_T ≈ 200–230 GA m^-2 and Fig. 4c ~7–8 J_c, without reaching the normal-state reference; the authors must show quantitatively that an intrinsic d-wave effect can drop E_T from 0.8 to near the normal-state level at these currents. (iii) A single temperature, 50 K (T/T_c ≈ 0.59), is compared with NbN at 7 K (T/T_c ≈ 0.47); thermal smearing broadens the transition even in an s-wave film at higher reduced temperature. (iv) Extrinsic routes are not excluded: twin boundaries, low-angle grain boundaries and weak links in commercial c-axis films on CeO2-buffered sapphire, T_c inhomogeneity (the d.c. R(J) in Fig. 2b is already gradual), edge current crowding (Pearl length ≈ 1–2.5 µm for a 50 nm film, far below the 10 µm width, so edges reach depairing well before the average) and vortex entry (larger λ, weaker edge barrier). (v) The normal-state reference is taken at 110 K, but YBCO's normal-state resistivity is roughly linear in T, so a normal strip at 50 K would have lower resistance and higher transmission; "converges to the value characteristic of the normal state" compares unlike states. (vi) The YBCO device and calibration details are only in SI section 6, so the reader cannot check that the geometry matches. The authors should: give an explicit J_c*(50 K) estimate for YBCO from independently measured λ and ξ and compare it with the maximum current reached; measure YBCO at several lower temperatures (e.g. 10–20 K, where nodal effects should dominate) and show NbN at matched T/T_c ≈ 0.6–0.8 (the SI section 7 data at 0.67 and 0.83) beside the YBCO curve, ideally on a common J/J_dep(T) axis; estimate R_n(50 K) and show the expected fully depaired level; provide structural evidence for "monocrystalline" (p. 1814): XRD rocking curves, φ-scans, twin-domain characterization; model the current distribution in the YBCO strip; and support the d-wave attribution with a d-wave microscopic calculation of the transmission rather than the qualitative tDGL of SI section 11.4. Otherwise the YBCO statement should be softened to an observation.

B. Should address

5. The analytic J_c* estimate (p. 1814) uses a clean-limit criterion for a dirty-limit material, and its inputs are unclear. J_c* = 2ħe n_s/(π m ξ) follows from the Landau criterion ħk_F·v_s = Δ with ξ = ħv_F/(πΔ). NbN films 20 nm thick are strongly disordered; the measured ξ (~5 nm from H_c2) is the dirty Ginzburg–Landau length, and the paper itself uses the dirty-limit Usadel equation for Fig. 5b. In the dirty limit depairing is governed by the pair-breaking parameter (Kupriyanov–Lukichev), not by a Doppler shift reaching Δ, with a different temperature dependence and prefactor. The Discussion's conclusion that the threshold "originates from the competition between quasiparticle energy shift and the superconducting gap" therefore does not follow from the model that reproduces the data. The authors should state which n_s(T) and ξ(T) were used (SI section 9), whether they were measured independently or adjusted, whether n_s is the pair or electron density (which fixes the factor 2e in J_c* = n_s v_s (2e)), whether m is free or effective and its value, and how many free parameters there are against the four J_c* points; compare the data with the parameter-free Kupriyanov–Lukichev J_dep(T) computed from measured ρ_n, T_c, diffusion constant, λ(T) and H_c2(T), included in Fig. 5a; quantify the discrepancy near 0.8 T_c (roughly 20–30% in Fig. 5a) rather than calling the data "slightly smaller"; and reconcile the physical picture in the Discussion with the dirty-limit model.

6. The Usadel simulation is partly tuned to the data, and the Usadel–tDGL comparison discriminates less than claimed. The Dynes broadening is "set to guarantee numerical convergence and to reproduce the experimental results" (p. 1814), making it a fit parameter for the shape in Fig. 5b, and the simulation fails to reproduce the high-current tail, attributed to unmodelled relaxation channels. tDGL is not valid at T/T_c ≈ 0.47, so its failure does not confirm the BCS picture. The authors should report the Dynes Γ and compare it with tunnelling or optical estimates for NbN; show how sensitive the position and sharpness of the drop are to Γ; list all free parameters and say which features of Fig. 5b are predictions and which are fits, including whether the absolute J_c* is predicted or only the shape after normalizing to J_c; and give tDGL the same freedom, or rest the "fingerprint of s-wave dirty-limit BCS" claim on a prediction that a plausible alternative does not reproduce.

7. The full time-domain waveforms are not used, so kinetic-inductance nonlinearity and the onset of dissipation are not separated. The analysis relies on peak amplitudes, and Fig. 3c shows waveforms only at 0.2 J_c and 6.5 J_c. As J approaches J_c*, the kinetic inductance rises sharply, which alone increases reflection and lowers the transmitted peak without dissipation; the inductive shape of the 7 K reflected pulse (Fig. 3b) shows this channel is active. The current dependence of L_kin below J_c* is a model-specific signature of how n_s is suppressed by supercurrent (in s-wave theory, a weak characteristic rise before the abrupt drop), and for YBCO it would separate nodal (∝ J) suppression from weak-link behaviour. The authors should show transmitted and reflected waveforms through the transition (1.5–3 J_c) to show when within the pulse depairing occurs; extract L_kin(J), hence n_s(J), and R(J) from the waveforms below and above J_c* for both materials; show that a resistive component appears at J_c*; and compare the measured current-induced suppression of superfluid density with the Usadel prediction before the collapse, a largely parameter-free test.

8. The extraction of J_c* and the fold-back in Fig. 4 need clarifying. Fig. 4a uses J_T,peak as the abscissa; because J_T drops at the transition the curve becomes multivalued (the "slight leftward tilt"), and the local maximum of J_T,peak in the Fig. 4b inset means the transmitted peak falls as drive increases. If depairing occurs when J reaches J_c* on the rising edge, the transmitted peak is set by when in the pulse the collapse occurs, so the quoted J_c* could depend on rise time and on the definition used. The authors should state how J_c* is extracted (maximum of J_T,peak, onset of the drop, or other) and how it is defined at higher temperatures where the local maximum may be less pronounced; plot against J_IN,peak alongside, or against an estimated in-strip current that accounts for reflection; add a sketch of the transmitted-versus-incoming relation with the multivalued region marked; mark in Fig. 4b the J_c* point used in Fig. 5a, tying the inset label "J_c*" to a stated value with uncertainty; and check that the threshold does not depend on pulse width or slew rate (see concern 1).

9. The sample temperature under illumination and possible optical quasiparticle injection are not established. The 515 nm beams illuminate switches close to the strip (Fig. 3a), so scattered light or substrate heating could create quasiparticles or raise the film's effective temperature; residual heating from the 50 kHz train (chopped at 1 kHz) could also contribute to the drop and the tail. Comparing J_c with J_c* at a common temperature assumes this heating is negligible. The authors should establish the effective sample temperature with the beams on, e.g. from the kinetic-inductance response or d.c. R(T) with beams on and off; add a control with the launching beam on and the switch bias off; show that the threshold does not depend on repetition rate or average laser power; and discuss whether post-depairing heating shapes the tail the BCS simulation misses, considering electron–phonon heating in the model.
