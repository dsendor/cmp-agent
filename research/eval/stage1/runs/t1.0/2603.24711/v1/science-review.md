## A. Must address

1. **The sharp NbN drop is not shown to be intrinsic depairing, and the proposed mechanism (vortices "inertially frozen" during a 2 ps pulse) is untested.** The central claim (abstract; Discussion p. 9) is that DC transport is limited by vortex motion and heating, while a ~2 ps pulse is too short for vortices to move, so the intrinsic Jc* is reached. The evidence is a kinematic order-of-magnitude estimate (vortex velocities of tens of km/s × 2 ps ≈ tens of nm), zero-field cooling, the NbN/YBCO contrast, and a comparison of two limits only (ms pulses, Fig. S6, and ~2 ps pulses). None of these tests the mechanism directly, and other routes to a sharp collapse are not excluded. (a) In a zero-field-cooled strip 10 µm wide, narrower than the Pearl length (≈12–13 µm at 7 K, SI S8.3), there are no pinned vortices to depin; the DC Jc is more likely set by edge-barrier-controlled vortex entry and the resulting thermal runaway than by the "Lorentz-force depinning" of Fig. 1b and p. 3. This changes what the ratio Jc*/Jc means, and the narrative should be corrected. (b) Electrothermal runaway inside the pulse: a 45–47 mA pulse (the threshold current in Fig. S12) through R_n ≈ 140 Ω dissipates I²Rτ ≈ 0.5–0.6 pJ in 2 ps, into ~6 µm³ of film, whereas the condensation energy of the 10 × 30 × 0.02 µm³ strip is B_c²V/2μ0 ≈ 0.04 pJ (B_c ≈ Φ0/(2√2πλξ) ≈ 0.13 T from the authors' λ and ξ). Once resistive (ρ ≈ 9×10⁻⁷ Ω m), a 2 ps pulse at ~2×10¹¹ A/m² deposits ~10⁵ J/m³, enough to raise the electron temperature by tens of kelvin or above Tc. A small seed of dissipation (a weak spot, an edge phase slip, or a kinematic vortex, which moves much faster than the Abrikosov vortex speeds cited in refs. 7, 18–22) could therefore self-amplify into a sharp collapse, so that the "threshold" reflects the weakest point plus feedback rather than uniform bulk depairing. (c) The gradual approach to the normal state above threshold (Fig. 4a, 2.5–6 Jc) is not reproduced by the adiabatic BCS model (Fig. 5b, S20) and is suggestive of heating. The authors should add at least one, and preferably several, discriminating experiments: (i) threshold versus pulse duration or shape (2 ps to tens or hundreds of ps, via a different switch gap or material, a filter, a dispersive line or a pulse train), showing a crossover from Jc* toward Jc on the timescale predicted by vortex entry and transit; an intrinsic threshold should not depend on duration, a thermal or nucleation threshold should; (ii) a field-cooled control with a known vortex density (perpendicular field), showing that DC Jc falls strongly while Jc* stays near the depairing value; (iii) threshold versus a small perpendicular field (depairing should depend weakly on field, edge vortex entry strongly); (iv) a two-pulse pump–probe experiment showing how quickly superconductivity recovers after a supra-threshold pulse (quasiparticle-recombination versus thermal recovery); (v) devices of different width (including w ≪ Λ_Pearl), pinning or edge quality (e.g. an ion-irradiated strip, cf. ref. 21), where Jc should change while Jc* stays fixed. The vortex argument should also be made quantitative, by showing that the voltage across the strip just above threshold (of order volts) would require an impossibly large vortex flux rate for the displacements allowed, and the electron-temperature rise within the pulse should be estimated. Without these, "Jc* ≫ Jc because vortices are inertially immobile" remains a hypothesis, and the observation could equally be a pulse-length-dependent instability threshold.

2. **The quantitative agreement with "microscopic BCS" depairing (abstract: "well described by microscopic dynamics based on BCS theory"; Fig. 5a, "excellent agreement") rests on an internally inconsistent formula and partly tuned parameters, so it is not a discriminating test.** The main text (p. 10) and SI Eq. 9 apply the clean-limit Landau criterion ħk_F v_s = Δ and write Jc* = 2ħe n_s/(π m ξ); with n_s = m/(2e²μ0λ²) this is Jc* = Φ0/(π²μ0λ²ξ). The identity Δ/(ħk_F) = ħ/(πmξ) holds only for the clean BCS (Pippard) coherence length ξ0 = ħv_F/(πΔ), but the ξ(0) = 5.24 nm inserted is the dirty-limit GL length from Bc2 (WHH, Eqs. 2–3), which for disordered NbN is much shorter than ξ0. The authors stress that NbN is in the strongly disordered dirty limit (p. 11, SI S10), the limit their own Usadel simulation assumes; there the Landau criterion does not set depairing, pair breaking is gradual and the film becomes gapless before the current maximum. The prefactor is therefore uncertain by tens of percent or more: with λ = 350 nm and ξ = 5.24 nm, the paper's formula gives ≈250–260 GA/m² at T → 0 (matching the dashed line), while the GL expression Φ0/(3√3πμ0λ²ξ) is smaller by 3√3/π ≈ 1.65, giving ≈160 GA/m², below the measured 220 GA/m² at 7 K. The Kupriyanov–Lukichev / Clem–Kogan result (ref. 11), j_d(0) ≈ 1.491 Δ0^(3/2)/(eρ_n(ħD)^(1/2)), estimated from the paper's own numbers (R_n ≈ 140 Ω over ~3 squares → ρ_n ≈ 90 µΩ cm; Bc2 slope ≈ 1.2 T/K → D ≈ 0.9 cm²/s; Δ0 ≈ 1.76 k_B Tc), gives several hundred GA/m², noticeably above the dashed curve. Agreement to ~10% with one particular prefactor thus does not test BCS. The temperature dependences are also ad hoc: λ(T) in Eq. 10 is the two-fluid (1 − t⁴)^(−1/2) form, not the dirty-BCS one, and ξ(T) in Eq. 11 uses a non-standard (tanh, nested-root) form that differs from Eq. 27, ξ(T) = ξ(0)[Δ(0)/Δ(T)]^1/2. In addition, the Usadel simulation (Fig. 5b, S20) has adjusted inputs: the Dynes parameter η is said in the main text (p. 11) to be set "to reproduce the experimental results", while the SI says it is fixed at 8% of Tc^BCS for numerical stability and later refers to a "Dynes β of zero"; Tc^BCS is rescaled; and the normal-state resistivity is "set to match the transmittance at T > Tc" rather than taken from the DC R_n. The main text also says the measured Jc* lies slightly below the Fig. 5a estimate while the Usadel simulation is said to reproduce the threshold; these two routes should be reconciled. The authors should derive Jc*(T) consistently in the dirty limit (Kupriyanov–Lukichev, or their own Usadel route, SI Eq. 29, which is not used for Fig. 5a) from the independently measured ξ(0), λ(0) or ρ_n, D and Tc; plot it in Fig. 5a with propagated uncertainties and with GL and Kupriyanov–Lukichev curves for reference; overlay the Usadel threshold at all five temperatures, not only 7 and 12 K; list every parameter that is fitted versus fixed; and show how the threshold position and sharpness depend on η (e.g. 2%, 5%, 8%). If agreement holds only for a tuned η, the abstract should say "consistent with" rather than "well described by".

3. **The d-wave interpretation of the YBCO data is not supported by a calculation, a temperature dependence or a control, the theoretical statement behind it is incorrect, and simpler explanations are not excluded.** The paper states (p. 7, p. 10, Discussion) that in a d-wave or anisotropic-gap superconductor "there is no well-defined intrinsic current density threshold" and Jc* "is not a well-defined quantity". This does not hold: in a d-wave superconductor nodal quasiparticles make the superfluid density fall linearly with superfluid velocity (the nonlinear Meissner effect), but Doppler-shift, Eilenberger and Usadel calculations still give a supercurrent j(v_s) with a maximum, softened but present, which is the depairing current; nodes mainly change the low-current nonlinearity and the T dependence. Gap anisotropy alone does not obviously turn a sharp collapse into a nearly linear decrease that starts at J ≈ Jc(DC) and has not saturated at 8 × Jc. Moreover, the YBCO depairing current is usually estimated at several thousand GA/m² at low temperature, whereas the largest transmitted current in Fig. 4c,d is ≈300 GA/m², so the absence of a maximum most plausibly means it was never reached. The size of the suppression (a ~50% drop in E_T at ~300 GA/m², J/J_d ~ 0.1) looks much larger than the intrinsic nonlinear Meissner effect would give. Extrinsic explanations are natural: the broad resistive transition (~82–90 K, Fig. S10b); the reduced Tc (85 K, suggesting oxygen deficiency); an implied λ(50 K) ≈ 0.7 µm (from the quoted Pearl length P_L(50 K) = 21 µm with d = 50 nm, SI S8.3), several times typical values for good YBCO films and pointing to reduced or inhomogeneous superfluid density; twin domains, low-angle grain boundaries or weak links (Josephson-like nonlinear inductance), which YBCO on r-sapphire/CeO2 normally has, with XRD (Fig. S2) not excluding them despite the "monocrystalline" description (p. 10); phase slips at boundaries, which the authors themselves invoke for polycrystalline films (ref. 26); a distribution of local Jc* values; vortex entry at T/Tc ≈ 0.6; or heating in a sample whose normal resistance depends on temperature (concern 4). The result rests on one device at one temperature (50 K, ≈0.59 Tc, versus 0.48 Tc for NbN at 7 K). The authors should provide: (i) a d-wave calculation of transmittance versus J_T,peak (nonlinear Meissner effect plus depairing, e.g. quasiclassical in the same framework and lumped-circuit model as the NbN Usadel calculation), compared quantitatively with Fig. 4c,d, with its temperature dependence; (ii) YBCO data at several temperatures, including well below 50 K, where the d-wave and inhomogeneity pictures predict different trends, close to Tc, where all models should converge, and at T/Tc matched to NbN; (iii) the crystallographic direction of the current relative to the Cu–O bonds, and ideally a comparison of current along the nodal [110] and antinodal [100] directions, which predicts a specific anisotropy; (iv) evidence on film homogeneity and twinning (e.g. the λ implied by the measured kinetic inductance, the Tc width); (v) ideally a second YBCO device, a cleaner untwinned d-wave film, or a disordered s-wave control with a comparably broad transition, to show that inhomogeneity alone does not produce the gradual curve. Alternatively, reach much higher current densities (narrower or thinner bridges). Without these, the abstract's "reflects its d-wave symmetry" and the closing "new approach to probing gap symmetry" should be removed or heavily qualified, and the conclusion narrowed to "YBCO shows no sharp threshold" or presented as a qualitative contrast.

4. **In YBCO the 110 K "normal-state" reference is not the normal-state response at 50 K, so the conclusions of "incomplete suppression of superconductivity" and a "gradual approach to the normal state" may follow from the choice of baseline.** Fig. 4c,d and SI S6 compare the 50 K data with the 110 K response and state that at 6 × Jc the transmittance (≈43%) exceeds the 110 K value (≈23%). Unlike NbN, whose R(T) is flat above Tc (Fig. S5a), YBCO has a strongly temperature-dependent, roughly linear normal-state resistance (Fig. S10a). In the lumped series-element picture T ≈ 2Z0/(2Z0 + R): R ≈ 230 Ω at 110 K gives T ≈ 0.30, while extrapolating R(T) linearly to 50 K gives roughly 100–140 Ω and T ≈ 0.42–0.5. The 43% at 6 × Jc (SI S6) and ~0.37–0.4 at 8 × Jc (Fig. 4c) are therefore close to what a fully resistive (or heated) strip at 50 K would give, rather than evidence of partial depairing. The authors should estimate the normal-state transmittance expected at 50 K, for example by passing the extrapolated ρ(T) through the SI circuit model (Eqs. 5–8 / 21–24), redraw Fig. 4c,d against that reference, and discuss how electron heating during the resistive part of the pulse shifts it.

## B. Should address

5. **Time-resolved waveforms across the threshold are not shown, and Jc* is extracted from peak amplitudes in a way that may not give the current at which the transition begins.** Fig. 3c and Fig. S13c show waveforms only far below and far above threshold (between them, 0.2 to 6.5 Jc), and Jc* is read as the local maximum of J_T,peak versus J_IN,peak (Fig. 4b inset, dashed lines in Fig. S14d). In the transition region the sample becomes resistive during the pulse; if depairing occurs on the rising edge, the transmitted waveform is reshaped and its peak is not the current at which the transition began. The "slight leftward tilt" in Fig. 4a is a symptom of this and is a multivalued region when plotted against J_T. The authors should show incident, reflected and transmitted waveforms at drives just below, at and just above Jc* (e.g. 1.8, 2.2 and 2.6 Jc), to show whether the transmitted current is clipped at Jc* within the pulse and when the switch to the resistive state occurs (at the current maximum, or later in the pulse as a thermal instability would); extract the instantaneous current at which the response departs from the inductive one; check whether the "local maximum" in J_T,peak corresponds to it; plot E_T,peak against J_IN,peak alongside Fig. 4a; and state how Jc* is extracted and its uncertainty.

9. **The uniformity of the current distribution is overstated.** SI S8.3 and S10.1 argue that the current is uniform because the Pearl length ≈ 12–13 µm is "larger than the sample's width" (10 µm); these are comparable, not in the w ≪ Λ limit, so the sheet current is still enhanced at the edges. 11. **The adiabatic, equilibrium assumption of the Usadel model is justified circularly.** SI S10.1 assumes the order parameter follows the current instantaneously with an equilibrium (tanh) distribution function, and the final paragraph of S10.3 argues that agreement with the data justifies this ("Since the two values are consistent, we can justify the use of instantaneous equilibrium theory"). In NbN τ_GL at 7 K is sub-ps, but quasiparticle energy relaxation (electron–phonon), inelastic and recombination times are ~10 ps or longer, exceeding the pulse duration, so under 2 ps drive the quasiparticle distribution is likely not thermal, which affects both n_s and the threshold, most of all once the gap collapses within the pulse. The authors should give τ_GL, τ_E and the gap relaxation time at each temperature relative to the pulse, and discuss why a dynamic threshold should coincide with the static depairing current rather than exceed it, as is often found for short pulses.
