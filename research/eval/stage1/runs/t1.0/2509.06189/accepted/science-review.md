A. Must address

1. The data do not separate collective motion of a pre-existing vortex boundary from local tip writing of in-plane polarization. In Figs. 2a–h and 3, each new tip path (PD2, PD3, …; ND1, …) is drawn at, or just beyond, the boundary left by the previous pass, and after PD1 the whole area between the left edge and the boundary becomes uniform (Fig. 2b, 2e; Fig. 3b, 3d–e). The reported "displacement" then grows step by step with the stepped path position, which is what one expects if a biased tip writes a stripe whose width is set by its trailing in-plane field. The claimed novelty, and the Discussion's statement of "long-range modifications across hundreds of nanometers, reflecting the inherently collective and nonlocal nature", depend on showing that the boundary moves as an entity. This is also hard to reconcile with the tip geometry: the tip radius is 25 nm and the paper's own FEM (Supplementary Fig. 4) confines the field to tens of nanometres, yet single passes shift the boundary by 256 nm (Fig. 2b) to 660 nm (Fig. 3b). The phase-field simulations (160 × 80 nm box) reproduce shifts of only tens of nanometres and so do not address the micrometre-scale motion. The authors should: (i) for every pass, give the x-position of the tip path and of the boundary before and after the pass, with a sketch, and state where each labelled displacement arrow is measured from (the arrows in Figs. 2–3 begin at different positions); (ii) run single passes at fixed offsets in front of, on and behind the boundary, and far from any boundary, and plot displacement versus tip–boundary distance, showing whether the boundary moves beyond the swept line or untouched regions reorganize; (iii) provide a simulation with a moving tip over a domain comparable to the experimental displacement, or a physical argument for long-range propagation.

2. There is no direct structural evidence that the region written by the tip is still in the vortex phase, so the moved LPFM boundary may be a conventional ferroelectric domain wall rather than a boundary between two zigzag vortex states. The vortex period is ~14 nm and the tip radius 25 nm, so LPFM cannot resolve individual vortices; it reports only net in-plane polarization along one cantilever axis. A +5 V bias across a ~60 nm trilayer on SrRuO3 gives a nominal vertical field approaching 1 MV/cm, above fields at which PTO/STO vortex phases are known to transform into polar phases, so the uniformly dark band in Fig. 2e and bright band in Fig. 3h are equally consistent with a single-sense zigzag vortex array, an a-type or c-type ferroelectric domain, or a mixed vortex/a-domain state. The evidence offered for vortex survival ("topologically protected motion", "preserving topological state") comes from in-situ STEM on a FIB lamella (Fig. 2j), with a tungsten probe at +17 V, different electrostatic boundary conditions, field direction relative to the tubes, field magnitude and thickness, and a ~100 nm field of view; it does not show the state of an AFM-written region. The authors should cut a cross-sectional lamella across a PFM-written boundary (for example from the Fig. 4 region) and show by polarization mapping that vortices persist on both sides with opposite zigzag sense or opposite net in-plane polarization, compared side by side with an unwritten region. Alternatively or additionally, they should show plane-view STEM, nano-/micro-area diffraction, RSM or SHG of a written area large enough to show that the ~14 nm satellites survive. Without this, "polar vortex boundary motion" in the title and abstract is an interpretation rather than an observation.

3. The identification of dark/bright LPFM phase regions as opposite net in-plane polarization of zigzag vortex arrays (Fig. 1a, Fig. 2l) is asserted rather than demonstrated. No vertical PFM is shown, although the tip applies a strong out-of-plane field that can convert vortex regions toward polar, c-domain or a1/a2 states. Only one cantilever orientation is used; vector PFM, with LPFM at two orthogonal sample orientations, is needed to determine the direction of the in-plane polarization relative to the vortex-tube axis and the tip path. Amplitude images after writing are not shown (Fig. 1a shows amplitude only for the pristine state). The authors should add vertical PFM and vector LPFM of written regions, show amplitude images after writing, state clearly which core configuration corresponds to each LPFM contrast, and quantify the net in-plane polarization from the STEM polar maps (averaged P_x over regions of each zigzag phase), showing that its sign matches the LPFM contrast.

4. Vortex specificity is not tested against any reference sample, and prior work on trailing-field writing is not discussed. Trailing-field, tip-direction-dependent switching and domain-wall motion of in-plane-polarized and ferroelastic domains is known in conventional ferroelectric films such as BiFeO3, and that literature is not cited. The claims that vortex topology underlies the behaviour ("topologically protected motion", Results p. 3; "owing to the topological protection", p. 6) need a comparison sample measured under matched tip, bias, speed and humidity conditions: for example a single PTO layer or thicker PTO film with a/c or a1/a2 domains, or a PTO/STO superlattice in the a1/a2 phase. If the same trailing-field writing appears there, the vortex-specific interpretation needs to be scaled back.

5. "Deterministic" and "controlled" motion are claimed, but the displacement per pass is not reproducible and its dependence on control parameters is not reported; the trailing-field attribution also lacks basic control passes. All data use ±5 V at 50 nm/s. The first positive pass gives 256 nm in Fig. 2b but 660 nm in Fig. 3b under nominally identical conditions, increments are strongly non-uniform (Fig. 3: 660 → 720 nm after PD2, then 1460 nm after PD6), and the text quotes ranges ("~1170–2000 nm", "~928–1400 nm") without saying what sets the spread. At present only the sign symmetry with bias polarity and travel direction supports the mechanism. The authors should report displacement per pass versus bias amplitude (including the threshold below which no motion occurs), tip speed, number of repeated passes along the same line and path length, with error bars from several runs and regions. They should also add control passes: the same path at 0 V and at several contact forces, to exclude mechanical or flexoelectric switching by tip loading, and repeated imaging-only scans at the 0.6–1.25 V drive amplitude, to show that imaging itself does not shift the boundary.

B. Should address

6. The in-situ STEM experiment does not show the same phenomenon as the PFM experiments, and its description is internally inconsistent. Fig. 2j mainly shows a local flattening of the zigzag (red → yellow cores) beneath the tip and a boundary at the edge of the flattened region, a few vortex pairs wide, whose displacement is not quantified; this is not a reversal of zigzag sense propagating laterally. The text describes the change as "CCW/up–CW/down to a near flat-configuration with CCW/down–CW/up", which is both "near flat" and reversed, and the caption says the switching "reverses the zigzag vortex arrangement". A flat core arrangement would have roughly zero net in-plane polarization, whereas the PFM interpretation and Fig. 2l require switching between two zigzag states of opposite net in-plane polarization. Reversal with negative bias is shown only in simulation (Fig. 2k IV–V). The field conditions are not reconciled: STEM uses +17 V on a tip of 10–50 nm with unspecified contact geometry, with the bias essentially along [001] at the lamella edge, while PFM uses ±5 V with an in-plane trailing field along the scan direction; the difference is attributed to a "dielectric gap" (refs. 25, 26) and "differences in the sample environment" without any estimate, and FIB damage, which the text concedes may alter boundary conditions, raises the question of whether the rearrangement is intrinsic. The authors should quantify the vertical core displacement across the boundary before and after biasing, show experimental negative-bias reversal and repeated cycles in both polarities, reconcile the descriptions, give quantitative estimates of the local field in the PTO layer in both geometries, report the number of lamellae and events, and qualify the statement that STEM "confirmed the controlled motion".

8. Electrostatic, charge-injection and electrochemical contributions to the LPFM contrast and to the retention are not excluded. Biased-tip passes at ±5 V in air inject surface charge and can drive electrochemical surface changes, either of which can alter PFM contrast or mimic retention; trapped charge could also be the physical origin of the trailing asymmetry. The authors should show KPFM or surface-potential maps and topography before and after writing, show that the LPFM phase contrast is independent of drive frequency and amplitude, and report the leakage current through the conductive tip during writing (relevant also to Joule heating).

10. "Topological protection" is inferred from retention alone, and the retention data are qualitative. The 120-day retention (Fig. 4) is presented as evidence of topological protection, contrasted with "conventional ferroelectric domains … readily relax back". Tip-written in-plane and out-of-plane domains in epitaxial PTO and BiFeO3 films often persist for months at room temperature, so retention alone does not distinguish the two pictures. Moreover, a zigzag reversal does not change the vortex winding, and no topological invariant or protection test is presented. Fig. 4 is a single region; storage conditions (atmosphere, humidity, temperature) are not given; boundary position is not quantified versus time, and image registration and drift are not addressed; unwritten domains elsewhere in Fig. 4a–f also appear to evolve. The authors should plot boundary position versus time with uncertainty, compare it with the evolution of unwritten domains, repeat in another region or sample, run the same retention test on a written conventional-domain reference, test thermal stability (annealing or thermal cycling), or otherwise remove the "topological protection" and "conventional domains relax" statements.

11. The coercive-voltage evidence (Fig. 1b,c) is internally inconsistent, lacks statistics and is only loosely connected to the claim. The Fig. 1b map is on a −50 to 200 mV colour scale, whereas the loops in Fig. 1c switch at roughly −1.5 to +2.5 V; a quantity on the mV scale looks like an imprint, (V_C+ + V_C−)/2, rather than a coercive voltage, and a low imprint near boundaries does not by itself imply easier switching. Only two loops are shown, each averaged from 10 points, with no spread. The loops are presumably vertical-mode, out-of-plane switching, whereas boundary motion involves in-plane polarization. If boundary coercive biases really were tens to hundreds of mV, the 0.6–1.25 V imaging drive and the 650 mV SS-PFM drive would themselves move the boundaries. The authors should clarify the mode, quantity and units in Fig. 1b, show distributions of V_C+ and V_C− for boundary and interior pixels with standard deviations, address electrostatic contributions to the SS-PFM loops (for example with off-field loops or KPFM), state which loop branch defines the "lower coercive bias", and explain why a lower out-of-plane coercive bias at boundaries governs in-plane boundary motion.

12. The finite-element model does not describe the sample and puts the trailing-field asymmetry in by hand. It treats the film as a 100 nm isotropic dielectric with ε_r = 100 on a grounded substrate, a 20 nm tip radius and 0.1 nm tip height, whereas the actual stack is a ~24–60 nm trilayer on an SrRuO3 electrode with strongly anisotropic and large permittivity in the vortex phase, and the PFM tip radius is 25 nm. The leading/trailing asymmetry comes from "a small perturbation … applied to the tip boundary potential based on the probe's motion direction", so the direction dependence follows from the model by construction and the FEM cannot independently support a trailing field of a given sign or magnitude. The authors should model the real stack and identify a physical origin for the asymmetry, for example a time-dependent simulation of a moving tip given the finite switching time and the 50 nm/s speed, screening or injected surface charge left behind the tip, or water-meniscus effects, and test it (for instance by tip-velocity dependence); otherwise they should present the FEM as qualitative only.

13. The phase-field model has fitted, under-documented parameters, and its comparison with experiment is only qualitative. The DMI-like coefficients D_N = 0.1 and D_B = 0.3 are given without units and were chosen "to provide a quantitatively consistent description" of the experiments, so they are fitted; it is not shown whether the zigzag ground state and the boundary motion survive with D = 0 or across a range of coefficients. The Landau, elastic and electrostrictive coefficients are deferred to the literature. The representation of the moving tip (field magnitude, spatial profile, timescale relative to 50 nm/s) is not given. The "energy barrier" in Supplementary Fig. 9 is the energy along a field-driven TDGL trajectory, not a minimum-energy path, and should not be called a barrier between "nearly degenerate configurations" without a string/NEB calculation. The authors should list all coefficients in the SI, show results with and without the DMI term and for varied D, state which parameters were fitted, give the simulated field amplitude and compare it with the FEM estimate of the experimental trailing field, and compare at least one quantity with experiment, such as the threshold field or the displacement per pass.
