Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper reports that a biased, moving conductive AFM tip (+/-5 V, 50 nm/s) can displace a lateral-PFM contrast boundary in a PbTiO3/SrTiO3 trilayer on SrRuO3/DyScO3 by hundreds of nanometres to about 2 um, reversibly with field polarity and with tip-travel direction, and that the moved boundary is retained for 120 days. In-situ cross-sectional STEM biasing and phase-field simulations attribute this to switching of the zigzag (up-down) arrangement of vortex cores, which carries a net in-plane polarization. Deterministic, reversible repositioning of polar-vortex textures would interest the polar-topology and nanoelectronics communities. As presented, though, the data are hard to tell apart from known trailing-field writing of in-plane ferroelectric domains, and the link between the PFM-scale boundary and a specific vortex-core rearrangement rests mostly on separate experiments in a different geometry.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The vortex phase is established by several methods: RSM satellites (~14 nm period), cross-sectional HAADF/BF-STEM and polarization mapping (Fig. 1d,e), and phase-field simulations (Fig. 1f,g).
- The PFM sequences are systematic: stepwise displacements under repeated passes (Fig. 2a-h, Fig. 3), with reversal both by bias polarity and by tip-travel direction at fixed polarity (Fig. 3f-h, n-p).
- The in-situ STEM biasing (Fig. 2j) gives a unit-cell-scale picture of field-induced rearrangement of vortex cores and of a boundary between two core configurations, and the phase-field simulations reproduce it qualitatively (Fig. 2k).
- The 120-day retention data (Fig. 4) are relevant to memory applications.
- The physical picture, in which zigzag core ordering gives a net in-plane polarization that couples to the in-plane component of a trailing field, is appealing and testable.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. Boundary motion versus tip-written domains. The data in Figs. 2-3 do not separate "motion of a vortex boundary" from conventional writing of in-plane-polarized regions along the tip path. In Fig. 2a-h and Fig. 3, each new path (PD2, PD3, ...) is drawn at or near the boundary left by the previous pass, and after PD1 the whole area between the left edge and the boundary becomes uniform (Fig. 2b; Fig. 3b). This is what one expects when a biased tip writes a stripe whose width is set by the trailing in-plane field, as in trailing-field control of in-plane/ferroelastic switching in BiFeO3 and other films. That earlier work is not cited or discussed. The claimed novelty, controlled motion of a pre-existing topological boundary, depends on showing that the boundary moves as an entity rather than being redrawn at the edge of each written stripe. The authors should:
   - report, for every pass, the tip-path position relative to the boundary before and after the pass;
   - show what happens when the tip runs at fixed offsets (in front of, on, and behind the boundary, and far from any boundary);
   - give displacement per pass as a function of bias amplitude, tip speed and number of repeated passes along the same line, including a threshold voltage;
   - compare the same protocol on a reference film without vortices, for example a PTO film with a/c or in-plane domains, or a superlattice in the ferroelectric a1/a2 phase.

   Separately, displacements of 250-700 nm per pass from a tip of 25 nm radius imply a strongly non-local response, which the Discussion claims ("long-range modifications across hundreds of nanometres"). That claim needs exactly these controls.

2. Vortex topology in the PFM-switched region is not shown. The central interpretation is that the vortex lattice survives in the written region and only the zigzag phase of the cores changes ("topologically protected motion", "preserving topological state"). The evidence for that comes from a FIB lamella biased at +17 V with a tungsten probe (Fig. 2j). That geometry differs from the PFM one in electrostatic boundary conditions, field direction relative to the tubes, field magnitude and lamella thickness. No structural characterization is shown of a region switched by the AFM trailing field. In addition, the in-situ STEM shows zigzag converting to a near-flat configuration (Fig. 2j-II/III, main text p. 3), whereas the PFM interpretation and Fig. 2l require switching between two zigzag states of opposite net in-plane polarization. The Fig. 2 caption says the switching "reverses the zigzag arrangement", which contradicts the "near-flat" description. A flat core arrangement would have roughly zero net in-plane polarization, so it is unclear which state the dark and bright LPFM regions represent. The authors should:
   - cut a cross-sectional lamella across a PFM-written boundary (for example from the Fig. 4 region) and show by polarization mapping that vortices persist on both sides, with opposite zigzag phase or opposite net in-plane polarization;
   - or provide plane-view STEM, or RSM/nano-diffraction, of a written area large enough to show that the satellites survive;
   - state clearly which core configuration corresponds to each LPFM contrast.

3. The origin and interpretation of the LPFM contrast are under-determined. The identification of the dark/bright LPFM phase regions as opposite net in-plane polarization of zigzag vortex arrays (Fig. 1a, Fig. 2l) is asserted, not demonstrated. Four things are missing:
   - No vertical PFM is shown. The tip also applies a strong out-of-plane field, and in a vortex phase this can convert regions toward a polar or a1/a2 state.
   - Only one cantilever orientation is used. Vector PFM, with the sample rotated 90 degrees relative to the cantilever, is needed to determine the in-plane polarization direction and its orientation relative to the vortex tube axis and tip path.
   - Amplitude images after writing are not shown. Fig. 1a shows amplitude only for the pristine state.
   - Electrostatic and charge-injection contributions to the LPFM phase after +/-5 V writing are not excluded. Topography and KPFM after writing, plus PFM at several drive amplitudes, would address this.

   The authors should also quantify the net in-plane polarization from the STEM polar maps (averaged P_x over regions of each zigzag phase) and show that its sign matches the LPFM contrast. Without this, the boundary could equally be a conventional in-plane domain wall, or a boundary between vortex and non-vortex phases.

B. Should address

4. Orientation of the vortex tubes relative to the tip path is not stated. Fig. 2l depicts a field applied along the tubes and Fig. 2m a field perpendicular to them, but for the experiments in Figs. 2-4 the paper does not state the crystallographic direction of the tip path, the tube axis ([100] or [010] on the anisotropic DyScO3) or the cantilever axis. The trailing-field mechanism, and the claim that motion is controlled by tip trajectory, depend on this. Please give these directions for each dataset, and if possible show motion for paths both parallel and perpendicular to the tubes.

5. Reproducibility and statistics are not reported. It is not stated how many samples, regions and repetitions were measured, or what the success rate was. Figs. 2, 3a-h, 3i-p and 4 appear to be single realizations, and the stated displacements vary widely: "~1170-2000 nm" after PD7, "~928-1400 nm" after ND6, ~1930 nm, ~1880 nm, and "exceeding ~1700 nm" in the Discussion. Displacements carry no uncertainty, although the boundaries are rough (Fig. 2e, 3d) and image drift between scans is not addressed. Please report:
   - the number of samples and regions;
   - displacement-per-pass histograms;
   - how boundary position was defined (for example, the mean of the traced contour relative to fixed topographic markers) and its error;
   - whether full restoration (Supplementary Fig. 6) works every time.

6. The coercive-voltage evidence (Fig. 1b,c) is weak and only loosely connected to the claim. SS-PFM loops are averaged from 10 points, with no spread shown. The loops are presumably vertical-mode, out-of-plane switching, whereas boundary motion involves in-plane polarization, so their relevance to the mechanism needs justifying. The Fig. 1b colour bar is in mV (-50 to 200 mV), while the loops in Fig. 1c show coercive voltages of order volts, so the map's units or normalization look inconsistent. The SS-PFM drive (650 mV) is in the same range as the imaging drive (0.6-1.25 V), which could perturb the state. Please clarify the mode and units, show distributions of V_C for boundary and interior pixels with statistics, and explain why a lower out-of-plane coercive bias at boundaries governs in-plane boundary motion.

7. "Topological protection" is inferred from retention alone. The 120-day retention (Fig. 4) is presented as evidence of topological protection "unlike conventional ferroelectric domains". However, tip-written domains in epitaxial PTO and BiFeO3 films often persist for months at room temperature, and Fig. 4 is a single region. Please:
   - provide a control retention measurement on a written conventional-domain reference;
   - test thermal stability (retention after annealing at elevated temperature, or after thermal cycling);
   - examine whether the surrounding unwritten pattern also evolves over 120 days;
   - otherwise soften the claim.

8. The modelling contains built-in assumptions and its parameters are not independently justified.
   - The FEM tip model treats the film as a 100 nm isotropic dielectric with permittivity 100. The actual stack is ~24 nm (60 uc) on a SrRuO3 electrode, with strongly anisotropic permittivity, and the PFM tip radius is 25 nm, not the modelled 20 nm.
   - The trailing asymmetry is introduced through an ad hoc "small perturbation" to the tip boundary potential that depends on motion direction. The direction dependence therefore follows from the model by construction and is not explained by it. A physical model would derive it from the field history in time, given the finite switching time and the tip speed.
   - In the phase-field model, the DMI-like coefficients D_N = 0.1 and D_B = 0.3 have no units and were chosen to match experiment.
   - The phase-field section does not say how the moving tip, its field magnitude and its timescale relative to 50 nm/s were represented.
   - The comparison with experiment is qualitative.

   Please give these details, state which parameters were fitted, and compare at least one quantity: the threshold field, or the displacement per pass.

9. The term "real-time" is overstated. The abstract claims "real-time observation of vortex boundary motion", but PFM images are acquired after each pass, and the in-situ STEM consists of before/after snapshots (I-III) taken with the beam blanked during biasing. Either show time-resolved data or rephrase.

10. In the in-situ STEM, beam effects, the field estimate and the bias/geometry are not controlled. The +17 V bias over a tip of unspecified contact geometry, together with FIB damage, which the text concedes may alter boundary conditions, raises the question of whether the core rearrangement is intrinsic. Please estimate the local field and compare it with the PFM case, quantitatively rather than by citing the dielectric-gap argument. Also report the reversibility of the STEM experiment under negative bias, which is shown only in simulation (Fig. 2k IV-V), and report the number of lamellae and events.

C. Minor

11. The sample is described inconsistently. The main text and Fig. 2j describe a 20 uc STO/20 uc PTO/20 uc STO trilayer, but the Methods state "A 20 uc PbTiO3/20 uc SrTiO3/20 uc PbTiO3 (PTO/STO/PTO) trilayer". Please correct.

12. The displacement numbers are ambiguous. "Cumulative displacement of ~1170-2000 nm observed after the seventh trailing field" and "~928-1400 nm after ND6" give ranges where the figures give single values. Fig. 2h labels 242 nm, which is evidently the remaining offset and not the displacement. Please define consistently what each arrow label measures.

13. The figure captions have problems.
   - The Fig. 3 caption alternates between "PD" and "PU" labels for the path symbols in ways that are hard to follow. For example, panel e "After PD10" shows path PU1.
   - The Fig. 1a caption says the dark contrast "represents the boundary of the vortex", which is not explained.
   - Fig. 4 lacks indication of the tip path for the movement step.

14. The finite-element model would benefit from a figure of the computed in-plane field component versus distance from the tip, compared with the measured displacement per pass.

15. In the Methods, the PFM resonance is given as "~670 kHz" (contact) and 75 kHz (free), and the drive amplitude as both 0.6-1.25 V and 0.75-1.25 V. Please harmonize these. "Piezoresponce" is misspelled throughout the Methods headings.

16. The memory-capacity and LLM/AI motivation in the introduction is not connected to the results, and could be shortened.

17. Data are available only "upon reasonable request". Please deposit the raw PFM image sequences and the phase-field input files.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Run a control matrix on the same sample: tip path in front of, on and behind the boundary, and far from any boundary; bias amplitude and speed series; paths parallel and perpendicular to the tubes. Repeat the key protocol on a reference film without vortices. Together these show whether the effect is vortex-specific boundary motion.
2. Prepare a cross-sectional (or plane-view) STEM lamella across a PFM-written boundary. Map the zigzag phase and the net in-plane polarization on both sides, and tie them to the LPFM contrast.
3. Add vertical and vector LPFM (two sample orientations), together with amplitude, topography and KPFM after writing, to fix the polarization directions and exclude charge-injection artifacts.
4. Replace the ad hoc direction-dependent perturbation in the tip model with a time-dependent simulation of a moving tip on the real stack geometry. Report one quantitative comparison with experiment, such as the threshold bias or the displacement per pass.
