Review outline:

**1. Significance and novelty**

The manuscript reports that boundaries between regions of a PbTiO3/SrTiO3 (20/20/20 uc) trilayer on DyScO3, seen as opposite-phase regions in lateral PFM, can be moved step by step over micrometre distances. The authors move them by scanning a biased AFM tip along chosen paths, and they reverse the motion by flipping either the bias polarity or the scan direction. They support this with in-situ cross-sectional STEM biasing, phase-field simulations and 120-day retention scans. The authors interpret the motion as collective motion of polar-vortex boundaries, driven by a trailing in-plane field that switches the zigzag arrangement of vortex cores. Deterministic, reversible and non-volatile electrical control of polar-topology textures would be of broad interest. The main open question is whether the experiments show vortex-boundary motion, as distinct from local writing of in-plane polarization by the tip.

**2. Potential reasons for acceptance**

- The experiment is clear and simple to reproduce: repeated trailing-field passes give monotonic, reversible boundary displacement of about 1–2 µm (Figs. 2a–h, 3).
- Bias polarity and tip-motion direction both control the direction of motion (Fig. 3a–h versus 3i–p), which points to an in-plane trailing-field component rather than the out-of-plane bias alone.
- The data span length scales from µm-scale LPFM to atomic-scale polarization mapping under bias (Fig. 2j), with phase-field simulations in two geometries (Fig. 2i, k).
- The moved boundary is retained for 120 days (Fig. 4), which matters for any memory application.

**3. Concerns**

**A. Must address**

1. **The data do not separate collective vortex-boundary motion from local tip writing of in-plane polarization.**
   - *What the figures show.* In Figs. 2 and 3 each successive tip path (PD1, PD2, …; ND1, …) appears to be placed at, or just beyond, the boundary left by the previous pass. The reported "displacement" then grows step by step with the stepped path position.
   - *Why this matters.* A biased tip dragged across a ferroelectric film with an in-plane polarization component is known to write in-plane domains through its trailing field. That process needs neither vortices nor any collective, nonlocal response. The Discussion claims "long-range modifications across hundreds of nanometers, reflecting the inherently collective and nonlocal nature", but the figures as presented cannot test this.
   - *What the authors should do:*
     - (i) For every pass, give the x-position of the tip path and the x-position of the boundary afterwards, and state where each displacement is measured from (the labelled arrows in Figs. 2–3 begin at different positions).
     - (ii) Show that the boundary moves beyond the swept line, or that regions not touched by the tip reorganize. A single pass at a fixed distance from the boundary, with the boundary displacement measured as a function of that distance, would test this directly.
     - (iii) Repeat the same protocol in a reference sample without vortices, for example a thicker PTO film with a1/a2 or a/c domains, or a PTO/STO superlattice in the a1/a2 phase. If the same trailing-field writing appears there, the vortex-specific interpretation needs to be scaled back.

2. **There is no direct structural evidence that the region swept by the tip is still in the vortex phase.**
   - *The current basis.* The identification rests on LPFM phase contrast alone, and Fig. 1a was taken on the pristine sample. After PD1–PD7 the swept area is uniformly dark (Figs. 2e, 3d–e). That is equally consistent with:
     - a vortex array with a single zigzag sense; or
     - a ferroelectric in-plane (a-type) domain; or
     - a mixed vortex/a-domain state.
   - *Why the in-situ STEM does not settle it.* The in-situ STEM (Fig. 2j) is done on a FIB lamella with different boundary conditions. It uses a different field geometry (a tungsten tip on the cross-section edge, +17 V) and covers a ~100 nm field of view. It does not show the state of a region written by the AFM.
   - *What the authors should do:*
     - Cross-sectional or plane-view STEM, or polarization mapping, of a region cut out after AFM writing, compared side by side with an unwritten region of the same film.
     - At minimum, vertical PFM and vector (two-orientation) LPFM of the written region, to show the out-of-plane response and the direction of the in-plane component.
     - If possible, micro-area diffraction or SHG showing that the ~14 nm periodicity persists in the switched area.
   - Without this, "vortex boundary motion" in the title and abstract is an interpretation rather than an observation.

3. **The trailing-field attribution lacks basic controls and parameter dependences.** All motion data use ±5 V at 50 nm/s (Methods), and no control passes are shown. The authors should add:
   - **Control passes:**
     - the same path at 0 V, to test mechanical or flexoelectric switching by tip loading; and
     - passes at several contact forces.
   - **Parameter dependences:** displacement per pass as a function of bias magnitude (to find a threshold), tip speed, and number of repeated passes on the same path.
   - **Path orientation:** the effect of path orientation relative to the vortex-tube axis. Fig. 2m claims motion for fields perpendicular to the tubes, but the experimental figures never state the tube direction (set by DSO anisotropy) relative to the tip path.

   These measurements would test the claimed mechanism; at present only the sign symmetry supports it. They would also give the quantitative relation between the trailing field and displacement that "deterministic control" implies.

**B. Should address**

4. **The microscopic mechanism (switching of the zigzag core arrangement) rests on one in-situ STEM sequence and a model with tuned parameters.**
   - *STEM.* Fig. 2j shows a single positive-bias sequence. The flat segment in Fig. 2j(II–III) is a few vortex pairs wide and its displacement is not quantified. No reversal with negative bias is shown.
   - *Phase field.* The zigzag state in the simulations depends on an added DMI-like term with D_N = 0.1 and D_B = 0.3, chosen to give "a quantitatively consistent description". It is not shown whether the zigzag configuration, and the boundary motion, survive without this term or across a range of coefficients.
   - *Applied field.* The simulated field (magnitude, spatial profile, how the "moving tip" is implemented) is not given in the main text.
   - *What the authors should do:*
     - Report the displacements from repeated STEM biasing cycles in both polarities.
     - Show the simulated boundary motion with D = 0 and for varied D.
     - State the simulated field amplitude and compare it with the FEM estimate of the experimental trailing field.

5. **The finite-element model does not describe the sample and puts the trailing-field asymmetry in by hand.**
   - *Geometry and permittivity.* The model uses a 100 nm dielectric with ε_r = 100 on a grounded substrate. The actual film is a ~24 nm trilayer on an SRO bottom electrode, with strongly anisotropic and large permittivity in the vortex phase.
   - *Asymmetry.* The direction asymmetry comes from "a small perturbation … applied to the tip boundary potential based on the probe's motion direction". The FEM therefore cannot independently support a trailing field of a given sign or magnitude.
   - *What the authors should do:* Model the real stack, and give a physical origin and estimate for the trailing component, for example from charge injection and screening dynamics at a given scan speed. Alternatively, present the FEM as qualitative only.

6. **The PFM imaging conditions may themselves perturb the boundary.**
   - *The conflict.* The V_C map in Fig. 1b is plotted on a scale of −50 to 200 mV, and the text claims "significantly lower coercive field" at boundaries. Yet imaging uses drive amplitudes of 0.6–1.25 V and SS-PFM uses 650 mV. If boundary coercive biases really lie in the tens to hundreds of mV range, imaging would move the boundaries.
   - *What the authors should do:*
     - Clarify what V_C in Fig. 1b is. A quantity on the −50 to 200 mV scale looks like an imprint, (V_C+ + V_C−)/2, rather than a coercive voltage; the loops in Fig. 1c have coercive voltages of about −1.5 to +2.5 V.
     - Report the coercive voltages from Fig. 1c with spread and point counts (10 points, no error bars).
     - Show that repeated imaging at 1.25 V does not shift the boundary.
   - SS-PFM probes out-of-plane switching, while the motion concerns the in-plane component. The link between the two should be argued.

7. **Claims of topological protection and contrast with conventional domains are not supported by a control.**
   - *What the claims say.* The text says the moved boundary "persists owing to the topological protection" and that "conventional ferroelectric domains … readily relax back". It also calls the motion "topologically protected".
   - *Why the evidence falls short.* Fig. 4 shows retention in one region only. Tip-written in-plane and out-of-plane domains in PTO films often survive for months, so retention alone does not distinguish the two pictures. The retention is also not quantified: there is no plot of boundary position versus time, and no account of image registration or drift.
   - *What the authors should do:*
     - Quantify the boundary position over time.
     - Run the same retention test on a reference ferroelectric domain sample.
     - Otherwise remove the "topological protection" and "conventional domains relax" statements.

8. **Novelty statements conflict with the cited literature.** The introduction says real-time observation of polar vortex motion under an applied field "has yet to be realized", and the Discussion claims "for the first time". However, ref. 20 (Chen et al., "Electrically driven motion, destruction, and chirality change of polar vortices in oxide superlattices") and ref. 8 report field-driven vortex dynamics by in-situ TEM. The authors should state precisely what is new here, for example µm-scale, reversible, tip-path-controlled boundary motion. The abstract's "real-time observation" should also be corrected: the PFM data are before/after scans, not real-time imaging.

9. **Reproducibility and statistics are not reported.**
   - Only one trilayer sample is described.
   - Figs. 2, 3 and 4 appear to come from three regions.
   - The text gives displacement ranges ("~1170–2000 nm", "~928–1400 nm") without saying how many regions or repetitions they represent.

   The authors should state the number of samples and regions, how often motion succeeded or failed, and the spread in displacement per pass. If possible they should add a second sample, or results after thermal cycling.

10. **The ambient electrostatic and electrochemical contributions to LPFM contrast are not excluded.** Biased-tip passes in air inject surface charge and can drive electrochemical surface changes, and either can alter PFM contrast. The authors should show:
    - KPFM or surface-potential maps after writing;
    - topography before and after writing; and
    - that the LPFM phase contrast is independent of drive frequency and amplitude.

    The long retention argues against transient charge, but a direct check is needed.

11. **The PFM and STEM field conditions are not reconciled.**
    - *The mismatch.* STEM requires +17 V on a ~10–50 nm tip, while PFM requires ±5 V. The authors attribute this to "differences in the sample environment" and the dielectric gap.
    - *Why it matters.* The field geometries also differ: the STEM bias is essentially along [001] at the lamella edge, while the PFM trailing field is in-plane and lies along the scan direction. The STEM therefore shows field-induced reconfiguration, but not necessarily the same process as in PFM.
    - *What the authors should do:* Estimate the local fields in both cases, and qualify the statement that STEM "confirmed the controlled motion".

**C. Minor**

12. **Panel labels in Figs. 2 and 3 are confusing.**
    - The panel titled "After PD1" shows path PD2. This is presumably the next path, but it should be stated in the caption.
    - In Fig. 3, the jump from "After PD2" to "After PD6" to "After PD10" skips several steps. The text cites "PD1–PD10, Fig. 3a–e" but only shows PD1, PD2, PD6 and PD10.
    - Increments are uneven (660 → 720 → 1460 nm) without comment.

13. **Several numbers are inconsistent.**
    - The Discussion gives displacements "exceeding ~1700 nm", while Fig. 3 shows 1930 and 1880 nm.
    - The text gives "~1900 nm" for Fig. 3a–e.
    - The text gives "~1170–2000 nm" after PD7, while Fig. 2e shows 1170 nm.

    Please make these consistent and state what each range represents.

14. **Methods details need correcting.**
    - "Drive amplitude … 0.6 V to 1.25 V" in the PFM section versus "0.75 to 1.25 V" in the trailing-field section.
    - Resonance frequency "~670 kHz" alongside a cantilever nominal frequency of 75 kHz. Please state which resonance (contact versus free) is meant.
    - The "Voltage controlled time evolution" protocol (+5 V for different durations) is not linked to any figure.
    - The phase-field grid is given as both 400 × 200 × 120 and 800 × 200 × 150, without saying which was used where.

15. **Fig. 1 needs clarification.**
    - The caption of Fig. 1a says "dark contrast in the amplitude represents the boundary of the vortex". The authors should explain why LPFM amplitude minima mark vortex boundaries.
    - The relationship between these ~100 nm amplitude features and the 14 nm vortex period should be given.
    - Fig. 1c should state which loop branch defines the "lower coercive bias", and show error bars.

16. **Typography.** "Piezoresponce", "hetrostructures", "Brucker", "tunned", "uc" spacing, and "Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya" interaction included in "gradient energy" all need correcting. The DMI term in Eq. (3) also needs its index convention defined (the ∂P_z/∂x_i term).

**4. Suggestions for improvement**

1. Add a correlative experiment: write a boundary by AFM, then cut a FIB lamella across the written and unwritten regions and map the polarization by STEM. This directly links the LPFM contrast to the vortex/zigzag state.
2. Add a systematic map of displacement per pass against bias, speed, path–boundary distance and path orientation relative to the vortex tubes. This would turn "deterministic control" into a quantitative statement.
3. Run the same trailing-field and retention protocol on a conventional ferroelectric domain reference sample. This would identify what is specific to vortices.
4. Report phase-field results with and without the DMI-like term, using a field profile taken from a realistic FEM model of the actual stack.
