Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports that a "vortex boundary" in a PbTiO3/SrTiO3 trilayer on DyScO3 can be moved back and forth by sweeping a biased conductive AFM tip along a chosen path. The boundary is the boundary between regions of opposite net in-plane polarization set by the zigzag arrangement of vortex cores. The direction of motion is set by bias polarity or by the direction of tip travel (a "trailing field"). The moved state is reported to persist for 120 days. Supporting evidence comes from in-situ cross-sectional STEM biasing and phase-field simulations. Deterministic, reversible, path-programmable motion of a polar-topological texture over micrometre distances would interest the polar-topology and nanoelectronics communities. As presented, however, the central identification is not demonstrated: that the lateral-PFM contrast boundary being moved is a boundary between two vortex (zigzag) states, and that the vortex phase survives in the written region. The mechanism linking a tip field that decays over tens of nanometres to boundary shifts of hundreds of nanometres per pass is also not established.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The basic phenomenology is clear and visually convincing. A single tip pass with a defined bias and travel direction produces a large, monotonic lateral shift of a LPFM phase boundary (Figs. 2a–h, 3a–p).
- The response depends on both bias polarity and tip-travel direction (PD/PU/ND/NU series, Fig. 3). This is a useful experimental discriminator pointing to an in-plane trailing-field component, not only the vertical field.
- The motion is reversible within the same region (Fig. 2f–h; Supplementary Fig. 6), and the written state persists at least 120 days (Fig. 4).
- Multiple techniques are combined: LPFM, SS-PFM, cross-sectional HAADF/BF-STEM with polarization mapping, in-situ STEM biasing, FEM and phase-field modelling.
- The sample-growth and structural characterization (RHEED, XRD, RSM side lobes at ~14 nm period) is standard and appropriate.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. It is not shown that the moved LPFM boundary is a boundary between two zigzag vortex states. The written region could instead be a conventional polar (a/c or in-plane ferroelectric) domain.
   - Where: Results, "Controlled vortex-boundary motion…"; Figs. 1a, 2a–h, 3, 4.
   - The problem: the vortex period is ~14 nm, and the tip radius is 25 nm (Methods). LPFM therefore cannot resolve individual vortices. The phase contrast reports only the net in-plane polarization along one cantilever axis.
   - A competing reading: a +5 V bias across a ~60 nm trilayer on SrRuO3 gives a nominal vertical field approaching 1 MV/cm, above the fields at which PTO/STO vortex phases are known to transform into polar (ferroelectric) phases. The bright/dark regions after writing (e.g., the uniform dark band in Fig. 2e and the uniform bright band in Fig. 3h) could then be field-induced polar domains, not a vortex phase with reversed zigzag sense.
   - The authors should provide direct evidence that vortices exist inside the written region after a PD/ND sequence. Options:
     (i) Correlative cross-sectional STEM polarization mapping of a lamella cut across a PFM-written boundary, showing zigzag vortex arrays of opposite sense on the two sides.
     (ii) Vertical PFM amplitude/phase of the same regions, to rule out switching to c-domains.
     (iii) Vector PFM, with LPFM taken at two orthogonal cantilever orientations, establishing the in-plane polarization direction relative to the vortex-tube axis.
     (iv) Where possible, local nano-diffraction or high-resolution PFM showing the ~14 nm periodicity persists in the written region.
   - Without this, the title claim ("polar vortex boundary") is not supported over the alternative "tip-written ferroelectric domain wall".

2. Vortex specificity: no null or reference control is shown.
   - Where: no control appears anywhere in the manuscript.
   - Trailing-field-driven, tip-direction-dependent motion of in-plane ferroelectric domain walls is a known phenomenon in conventional ferroelectric films. The claim that vortex topology underlies the behaviour (e.g., "topologically protected motion", Results p. 3; "owing to the topological protection", p. 6) needs a comparison sample measured under matched tip, bias, speed and humidity conditions. Examples: a single PTO layer or PTO/STO stack in the a1/a2 or a/c regime, or a thicker PTO film without vortices.
   - Also needed: a zero-bias control, where the tip is swept along the same path at 0 V, and an imaging-only control, where the region is repeatedly scanned at the 0.6–1.25 V drive amplitude. These exclude motion caused by tip pressure (flexoelectric) or by the imaging drive itself.

3. How a highly localized tip field moves the boundary by hundreds of nanometres per pass is not explained.
   - Where: Figs. 2b–e and 3b–e; Discussion ("long-range modifications across hundreds of nanometers").
   - The paper's own FEM (Supplementary Fig. 4) and the 25 nm tip radius imply a field confined to tens of nanometres. Yet single passes shift the boundary by 256 nm (Fig. 2b) to 660 nm (Fig. 3b).
   - Fig. 2 also suggests the tip path is drawn near the current boundary position each time. The authors should state explicitly, with a sketch for every pass, the lateral distance between each tip path and the boundary before the pass.
   - They should also show how displacement per pass depends on that distance.
   - The phase-field simulations (box 160 × 80 nm, Methods) reproduce shifts of only tens of nanometres, so they do not address the micrometre-scale collective motion that is the headline result. A simulation with a moving tip over a domain comparable to the experimental displacement, or a physical argument for the long-range propagation, is needed.

4. "Deterministic" and "controlled" are claimed, but the displacement per pass is not reproducible and its dependence on control parameters is not reported.
   - The numbers: the first positive pass gives 256 nm in Fig. 2b but 660 nm in Fig. 3b under nominally identical conditions (+5 V, 50 nm/s). Increments are strongly non-uniform: Fig. 3 gives 660 → 720 nm after PD2, then 1460 nm after PD6. The text quotes ranges ("~1170–2000 nm", "~928–1400 nm") without saying what sets the spread.
   - Missing measurements: only ±5 V and 50 nm/s are used. There is no dependence of displacement on bias magnitude (threshold field), tip speed, number of passes, or path length. There are no statistics over repeated runs or locations.
   - What to do: report displacement per pass versus bias (including the threshold below which no motion occurs) and versus tip speed, with error bars from several runs and regions. This is needed to support the terms "deterministic" and "controlled", and is central to any device claim.

5. The in-situ STEM experiment does not show the same phenomenon as the PFM experiments, and its description is internally inconsistent.
   - Different geometry and bias: the STEM biasing uses a cross-sectional lamella, a vertical tip-to-SRO field of +17 V, a single polarity, and different electrostatic boundary conditions.
   - What the images show: Fig. 2j mainly shows a local flattening of the zigzag (red → yellow cores) beneath the tip and a boundary at the edge of the flattened region. This is not a reversal of zigzag sense propagating laterally.
   - Inconsistent descriptions: the text describes the change as "CCW/up–CW/down to a near flat-configuration with CCW/down–CW/up", which is both "near flat" and a reversed zigzag. The caption calls the initial state "up-down" and also says the switching "reverses the zigzag vortex arrangement".
   - Reversibility not tested: reversal with negative bias is shown only in simulation (Fig. 2k IV–V), not experimentally.
   - What to do: quantify the vertical core displacement across the boundary before and after biasing, show the experimental negative-bias reversal, and reconcile the descriptions. The 5 V vs 17 V difference is attributed to a "dielectric gap" (refs. 25, 26) without any estimate. A quantitative estimate of the field in the PTO layer in both geometries is needed before the STEM result can "confirm" the PFM motion.

B. Should address

6. The sample stack is described inconsistently.
   - Results (p. 2), Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 captions, and the phase-field Methods describe a 20 uc STO/20 uc PTO/20 uc STO trilayer. The Methods "Growth of the trilayer" section states "20 uc PbTiO3/20 uc SrTiO3/20 uc PbTiO3 (PTO/STO/PTO)".
   - The two stacks would host vortices in one versus two PTO layers, with different electrostatics. This must be corrected, and the stack confirmed from the STEM images (Fig. 1d shows a single PTO layer).

7. The SS-PFM coercive-voltage results are inconsistent with the hysteresis loops and lack statistics.
   - Fig. 1b gives V_C on a −50 to 200 mV colour scale. The loops in Fig. 1c switch at roughly ±1–2 V.
   - The "average coercive bias" (presumably (V_C+ + V_C−)/2) is an imprint measure, not a coercive field. Its low value near boundaries does not by itself imply easier switching.
   - Only two loops are shown, averaged from 10 points. Distributions of V_C+ and V_C− for boundary and interior pixels, with standard deviations, are needed to support "significantly lower coercive field" at boundaries.
   - Electrostatic contributions to SS-PFM phase loops at 5 V should be addressed, for example with off-field loops or a KPFM check.

8. The trailing-field mechanism is put into the FEM by hand.
   - Where: Methods, "Finite element modeling".
   - A "small perturbation" to the tip potential, dependent on motion direction, is imposed to produce the leading/trailing asymmetry, so the model cannot explain the direction dependence.
   - Model parameters also do not match the sample: εr = 100 isotropic, a 100 nm dielectric (vs ~60 nm film), tip radius 20 nm (vs 25 nm), and a 0.1 nm tip height.
   - The physical origin of the trailing asymmetry should be identified and, ideally, tested: screening/injected surface charge left behind the tip, water-meniscus effects, or tip-velocity dependence. KPFM after writing would show whether injected surface charge is present and whether it contributes to LPFM contrast or to the retention.

9. The phase-field model has fitted, undocumented parameters, and the energy analysis does not measure a barrier.
   - The DMI-like coefficients (D_N = 0.1, D_B = 0.3) were chosen "to provide a quantitatively consistent description" of the experiments. They are therefore fitted, and the sensitivity of the zigzag ground state and of the boundary motion to these values should be shown.
   - The Landau, elastic and electrostrictive coefficients are deferred to the literature. They should be listed in the SI.
   - The "energy barrier" in Supplementary Fig. 9 is the energy along a field-driven TDGL trajectory, not a minimum-energy path. It should not be called a barrier between "nearly degenerate configurations" unless a string/NEB calculation is done.
   - The simulated in-plane field geometry for the "moving tip" should be specified.

10. The relation between the tip path, the LPFM cantilever axis and the vortex-tube axis is not defined.
    - Fig. 2m claims motion for fields both along and perpendicular to the vortex tubes. However, the vortex-tube orientation relative to the PFM images, and to the DSO [100]/[010] directions, is not stated for Figs. 2–4.
    - The authors should mark crystallographic axes, the cantilever orientation and the tube direction on each LPFM figure, and state which in-plane component the LPFM phase reports.

11. Retention data are qualitative, and charge-related artifacts are not excluded.
    - Fig. 4 shows one region. Storage conditions (atmosphere, humidity, temperature) over 120 days are not given, and the boundary position is not quantified versus time.
    - Unmoved domains elsewhere in Fig. 4a–f also appear to evolve. The authors should plot boundary position versus time with uncertainty, compare it with the natural evolution of unwritten domains, and repeat in at least one more region or sample.
    - Retention could reflect trapped injected charge rather than topological protection. A thermal anneal or KPFM-based test would help separate the two.

12. Reproducibility across samples and tips is not stated.
    - The manuscript does not say how many samples, regions, or tips were used. Only "a new region" is mentioned for Fig. 4.
    - The number of samples and independent regions in which the effect was seen, any failed or irreversible attempts, and leakage current through the conductive tip at ±5 V (relevant to Joule heating and charge injection) should be reported.

13. The terms "real-time observation" and "topologically protected motion" overstate what was measured.
    - The PFM data are before/after scans, and the STEM data are snapshots. Neither is a real-time observation of motion (Abstract).
    - "Topologically protected" is not tested: a zigzag reversal does not change vortex winding, and no topological invariant or protection test is presented.
    - Statements on sub-THz operation, energy efficiency and memory density (Introduction, Discussion) are not measured here. These should be rephrased.

14. Novelty relative to prior field-driven vortex manipulation is not discussed.
    - The claim of "the first time deterministic control" (Discussion) should be placed against refs. 7, 10 and 20, which report electrically driven motion, destruction and chirality changes of polar vortices and skyrmion bubbles.
    - The authors should say specifically what is new: path-programmable boundary motion and trailing-field directionality.

C. Minor

15. Displacement values are quoted inconsistently: "~1170–2000 nm" after PD7 (p. 3), "~1700 nm" (Discussion), "~1900 nm" (p. 6) versus 1930 nm (Fig. 3e). Give the definition of displacement (from the original boundary or per pass) and quote values from the figures consistently.

16. Fig. 2a–h show 256 nm after PD1 but PD2 drawn in Fig. 2b, so the labelling is shifted by one: "After PD1" shows path PD2. Clarify that dashed cyan lines denote the next path. Similar offsets occur in Fig. 3.

17. Fig. 1c axis/legend ("On vortex boundry") has a typo. The caption says the loops come from "the vortex and near vortex boundary", but the text says averages over 10 points. Clarify.

18. Methods: "Asylum Research MFP-3D origin+", "Budget sensor", and the 75 kHz free resonance versus ~670 kHz DART frequency should be explained (contact/torsional resonance). Units such as "0.9 J/cm2" and "−670 kHz" (minus sign) need correction. The in-situ STEM tip apex is given as "below 50 nm (and in some cases ~10 nm)" and "10–50 nm"; give the value used for Fig. 2j.

19. Phase-field Methods state a 400 × 200 × 120 grid with 30 substrate + 60 film layers + "remaining layers for air", which sums to 120 only if 30 air layers are used. State this explicitly, and give the physical size relative to the experimental displacement.

20. The trailing-field sketch (Supplementary Fig. 4) should be summarized in the main text, since the "trailing field" concept is central.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a correlative experiment. Write a boundary by PFM, then cut a FIB lamella across it and map polarization by STEM on both sides. This one experiment would settle whether a vortex-to-vortex (zigzag-reversed) boundary is being moved (Concern 1).
2. Add a systematic parameter study: displacement per pass versus bias magnitude, tip speed, tip–boundary distance and number of passes, with statistics over several regions and samples. Include a non-vortex reference film measured identically (Concerns 2–4).
3. Add KPFM and vertical PFM of written regions, and leakage-current monitoring during writing. These would separate polarization reorganization from surface-charge injection and c-domain switching, and test the trailing-field origin (Concerns 1, 8, 11).
4. Extend the phase-field simulations to a moving tip over a domain large enough to capture hundreds-of-nanometre displacements. Report parameter sensitivity, including to the DMI coefficients. Replace the trajectory-energy "barrier" with a minimum-energy-path calculation (Concerns 3, 9).
