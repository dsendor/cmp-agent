Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports electric-field manipulation of polar vortex arrays in PbTiO3/SrTiO3 trilayers and [PTO16/STO12]10 superlattices on DyScO3. A biased AFM tip is dragged along predefined paths ("trailing bias", ±5 V). The authors say this moves a "vortex boundary" over about 1.1 µm, that reversing the polarity moves it back, and that the new configuration lasts for up to 120 days. They also report that stationary voltage pulses create and annihilate "vortex rings". In-situ biased STEM and phase-field simulations are offered as support. Deterministic, reversible and non-volatile control of polar topological textures would matter for both physics and devices. However, the paper does not make clear what object is actually being moved, and it does not distinguish vortex motion from ordinary tip-induced domain writing or phase conversion. Prior work on electrically driven vortex motion and toroidal-order switching (refs. 5, 18, 23) also weakens the "first time" claim.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The topic is timely. Controlled, non-volatile manipulation of polar topologies is a recognised open problem.
- Several probes are combined: XRD/RSM, PFM and SS-PFM, ex-situ and in-situ biased STEM, and phase-field simulation. Two heterostructure types (trilayer and superlattice) are studied.
- The retention study is unusually long: PFM over 2 to 120 days (Fig. 4a–f), plus a STEM revisit after 20 days (Fig. 4g).
- The bidirectional sequences in Figs. 2a–i and 3a–f are a sensible protocol, and the reproduction in a second trilayer region (Fig. 4a–b) is a good start on reproducibility.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The object being moved is not identified, and the PFM features cannot be individual vortices. The vortex period is about 10–14 nm (RSM, Fig. 1a). The PFM tip radius is 25 nm (Methods). Yet the PFM "vortices" and "vortex boundaries" in Figs. 1c–d, 2, 3 and 5 are dark meandering features hundreds of nanometres wide, imaged in 2–5 µm scans. At this resolution individual vortex cores cannot be resolved. The dark-contrast regions must therefore be mesoscale regions: arrays of vortices with a given orientation, axial polarization or chirality, or boundaries between vortex and a1/a2/FE phase regions. The text says "the low amplitude of these vortices results in their distinct dark contrast in phase images" and "the dark contrast in the amplitude represents the boundary of the vortex", which mixes these levels. The headline claim, "vortex motion", depends on what the bright and dark LPFM regions actually are. The authors should:
   - state explicitly what the PFM contrast represents;
   - support that assignment with co-located high-resolution PFM (or STEM/DF-TEM) of the same kind of region before and after writing;
   - acquire vertical PFM and LPFM at two sample rotations (0° and 90°) to reconstruct the in-plane and out-of-plane components inside and outside the swept region.

2. Tip-induced domain writing or phase conversion is not ruled out. After each trailing-bias pass, the region swept by the tip becomes uniformly bright in LPFM amplitude, with uniform phase and no dark texture (Fig. 2b–e, Fig. 3b–d, Fig. 4b). This is what one expects if the tip field converts the vortex phase into a poled ferroelectric (c or a/a) state. The "boundary" would then simply be the edge of the written region, as in conventional PFM lithography, rather than a displaced vortex array. Two further observations point the same way:
   - The in-situ STEM sequences end with "all vortex cores in a single orientation" (Fig. 3g IV) or a reoriented array (Fig. 4g). That is reorientation or conversion, not translation.
   - The negative sweeps leave a disrupted texture (Fig. 3f; Fig. 2i differs from Fig. 2a), not the original one.

   The authors should:
   - show what the polarization structure is inside the written region, using vertical PFM, high-resolution STEM or DF-TEM, or local XRD/nano-diffraction;
   - overlay the tip path (channel) on each post-write image, so that boundary position can be compared with channel position;
   - show that vortices displaced from the swept area reappear beyond it. Without this, "motion" cannot be distinguished from "erasure and re-nucleation".

3. Electrostatic and surface-charge artifacts in PFM are not excluded. Writing at ±5 V in ambient conditions can inject surface or bulk charge, cause electrochemical changes or leave water-meniscus residues. All of these produce strong DART amplitude and phase contrast (including lateral signal through cantilever buckling and electrostatic crosstalk). Humidity, KPFM or surface-potential maps, and contact-versus-electrostatic separation are not reported. The authors should provide:
   - KPFM of written areas, including over time;
   - PFM at several drive amplitudes and frequencies, showing that the contrast is piezoelectric;
   - one write experiment in controlled atmosphere or after deliberate surface discharging;
   - topography before and after writing, to exclude surface modification.

4. Reversibility, which is a central claim, is not shown quantitatively, and the numbers do not match.
   - The abstract and conclusion say reversed bias "successfully returned the boundaries to their initial positions". But the trilayer moves about 1170 nm forward and about 890 nm back (Fig. 2). The superlattice moves about 1170 nm forward and about 643 nm back (Fig. 3 caption).
   - Fig. 3f is described as "disrupted" after CHN3.
   - The identical ~1170 nm value for both structures also needs explaining.

   The authors should:
   - plot boundary position against channel index for the forward and reverse sequences, with error bars from boundary-tracing uncertainty and image registration (drift correction against topographic markers);
   - give the number of independent forward/reverse cycles performed and their success rate;
   - test whether the motion survives more than one full cycle.

5. The claim that polarity sets the direction of motion has no mechanism or control. The tip field under a biased PFM tip is mainly out-of-plane and radially symmetric. It is not explained how a vertical field of one sign pushes a lateral boundary to the right and the opposite sign pushes it to the left. The direction may instead come from the order and position of the channels: the authors move the channel to the right (or left) in each step. If so, the polarity dependence is not demonstrated. The authors should:
   - apply the opposite polarity along the same channel sequence (for example, negative bias with channels stepped rightward), and positive bias with channels stepped leftward;
   - report the result, including the scan direction of the tip within each channel.

B. Should address

6. The layer sequence of the trilayer is inconsistent. The main text and the Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 titles give STO/PTO/STO (20/20/20 uc), and Fig. 2j and the phase-field set-up show one PTO layer between STO layers. The Methods (growth) and the Fig. 4 caption give PTO/STO/PTO. These are different structures with different electrostatic boundary conditions and vortex stability. The authors should state which sample was measured and correct the text throughout.

7. The SS-PFM evidence for reduced coercive bias at boundaries is thin.
   - Fig. 1g averages loops from 10 "representative" points per category, with no selection criterion and no statistics.
   - The panels are labelled LPFM, while switching spectroscopy uses a vertical DC field. It is unclear which signal the loops track, and what "nucleation bias" means for a vortex with largely in-plane, rotating polarization.
   - Boundary pixels have low amplitude, which biases phase-based coercive-bias extraction.

   The authors should:
   - give histograms of Vc+, Vc− and imprint over all 2500 pixels, classified by a stated mask;
   - report whether the loops are vertical or lateral, and show amplitude loops;
   - show topography for the same grid, to exclude contact-related variation.

8. The in-situ STEM experiments use a very different field geometry and different boundary conditions. They apply +15 to +20 V through a tungsten tip on FIB lamellae. The authors acknowledge that thinning alters the boundary conditions (a1/a2 superdomains vanish). The electron beam, possible current flow and tip-contact damage could also drive changes. Only a few before/after frames are shown. The authors should:
   - give the estimated field and current;
   - show a no-bias control under identical beam exposure;
   - show more than one event;
   - state how the 20-day stability was measured: was the lamella kept in the holder, and was the same area relocated?

   The text calls the STEM data "consistent with the PFM data", but the STEM displacements are tens of nanometres while the PFM displacements are about 1 µm. That consistency should be argued, not asserted.

9. The phase-field simulations are not specified well enough to support the mechanism.
   - The tip is "modelled by a Lorentz distribution", but its width, potential, field magnitude and electrical boundary conditions are not given. It is also not stated whether the SRO bottom electrode is included as a short-circuit condition.
   - The simulation box for the trilayer (400×200 uc, about 160 nm) is far smaller than the micrometre displacements claimed.
   - The text says the simulated boundary stays displaced after field removal. The Fig. 2 caption says that "upon field removal, the vortex boundary returns to a configuration similar to the initial state (p)", and panel p is labelled "Step 6".
   - The abstract claims that switching of the "zigzag patterns of the vortex core" controls boundary motion, but no figure quantifies this. Core-position or tilt maps, the energy versus step, and a field-threshold scan would be needed.
   - Supplementary Fig. 8's statement about "two energy-equivalent states" needs the total energies of the initial and final states.

10. The claims of stochasticity and "vortex rings" are not substantiated.
    - Fig. 5 is a single voltage ramp at one site. "Stochastic" requires repeated trials at fixed voltage, with the distribution of outcomes, rather than one sequence in which voltage and cumulative history are confounded.
    - Fig. 5m–n fit smooth curves through 8–9 points from 0 to 4 V, implying trends, while the text calls the behaviour random. The data beyond 4 V (up to 7 V) are omitted.
    - The PFM "rings" are hundreds of nanometres across and are features of amplitude contrast. The STEM "rings" (Fig. 5o–q) are closed loops in the stripe contrast. Neither is shown to be a vortex ring in the topological sense (closed vortex-core line, winding, or toroidal structure). Calling them "hidden topological phases" needs 3D polarization mapping or at least atomically resolved displacement maps.
    - It is not stated whether the STEM rings were field-induced or present in the pristine sample.

11. Dependences on writing parameters are missing. All trailing-bias experiments use ±5 V, 1 min and about 60 nm/s. The threshold voltage for boundary motion, and how displacement per pass scales with voltage, tip speed and number of passes, would test the claimed "reduced energy barrier" mechanism. They would also separate it from domain writing, which has its own characteristic voltage and time scaling. The authors should provide these dependences, at least in the trilayer.

12. Sample statistics are not reported. The paper appears to rest on one trilayer and one superlattice sample, with one or two regions each. The authors should report:
    - how many samples, regions and tips were used;
    - how often the experiment succeeded;
    - whether a second growth of each structure behaves the same.

13. The novelty claims need to be reconciled with prior work. The paper says real-time electric-field-driven vortex or boundary motion "has yet to be realized" and that "this work first time demonstrates" it. Yet it cites Chen et al. (ref. 18, electrically driven motion, destruction and chirality change of polar vortices), Behera et al. (ref. 5) and Damodaran et al. (ref. 23, electric-field control of toroidal order). The authors should state precisely what is new relative to these works. Also, "real-time observation" in the abstract is not accurate for the PFM data, which are before/after scans; the STEM data are discrete snapshots.

C. Minor

14. Figure–text mismatches in channel labels and supplementary references:
    - Fig. 2: the text cites CHP1, CHP3, CHP5, CHP6 and CHN1, CHN3, CHN5, CHN6, but the panel labels differ.
    - Fig. 3: the text cites CHP1, CHP5, CHP8 and CHN1, CHN4; the caption says CHP2–CHP7 and CHN1–CHN3; the panels show CHP1, CHP2, CHP4, CHN1 and CHN2.
    - Supplementary figure numbers disagree between text and captions: Supp. Figs. 3/4 versus 4/5 for Fig. 2, and Supp. Fig. 10j–l versus 11 for Fig. 5.

15. Fig. 4 caption: panel f is "after 60 days", but the panel label and the text say 120 days. The Fig. 4a colour bars have no scales.

16. Fig. 1 labels and directions:
    - The caption uses "g" twice (hysteresis loops and "g–i" phase-field), and the text cites "Fig. 1g" for the trilayer simulation, which is panel h.
    - The Fig. 1a caption says [100] and [001]; the text says [100] and [010].
    - In the Fig. 3g caption, directions are written (100), (010) and (001); in plan view, alignment "along (001)" is not possible.

17. The "lithographically defined channel" appears to be a programmed tip trajectory, not a fabricated structure. Please say so, and give the channel length, the spacing between successive channels and the tip-loading force.

18. The phase colour scales vary between panels (for example, Fig. 2e shows an almost uniform phase across the whole frame). Use fixed scales across a sequence so that changes can be compared.

19. The language needs careful editing. Examples include "harnessing" used for almost every action, "underline physical mechanism", "staes" and "revel". The introduction's appeal to large language models and the internet of things is unnecessary. Ref. 16 (YMnO3 films) does not support the sentence it is attached to.

20. XRD: the vortex and FE out-of-plane lattice parameters (4.05 Å and 3.959 Å) are given only for the superlattice. Give the corresponding values and the RSM-derived periods with their uncertainties for both samples.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Tie the mesoscale PFM contrast to the nanoscale structure. Do this with correlative imaging of the same written region: PFM, then a FIB lamella cut across the written edge, then DF-TEM or STEM polarization mapping. This would show directly whether the swept region contains displaced vortices, a converted FE phase or an erased texture.
2. Replace the qualitative image sequences with quantitative boundary-position plots. Show forward and reverse cycles, with error bars and drift correction, and as functions of bias, tip speed and number of passes. Include the opposite-polarity and same-direction controls described in concern 5.
3. Include KPFM and vertical PFM of written areas, and a write experiment under controlled humidity, so that the readout can be shown to be piezoelectric and polarization-based.
4. Fully specify the phase-field tip model and boundary conditions. Use the simulations to predict a measurable dependence, such as a threshold voltage or the dependence on polarity and channel direction, that the experiments can then test.
