Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports electric-field control of polar vortex arrays in PbTiO3/SrTiO3 trilayers and [PTO16/STO12]10 superlattices on DyScO3. A biased PFM tip dragged along line "channels" is said to move a "vortex boundary" by about 1 µm and back again, and in-situ biased STEM is said to show the same thing. The moved boundaries are reported to be stable for up to 120 days. Point pulses are said to create, annihilate and reshape vortices, including "vortex rings". Deterministic, reversible motion of polar topological textures would interest the ferroelectric-topology community and matter for proposed racetrack-type devices. As presented, however, the data do not separate genuine vortex or boundary motion from ordinary tip-induced domain writing or a field-induced phase conversion. The "first time" claim also needs to be set against earlier reports of electrically driven vortex motion and field-controlled toroidal order (refs. 18 and 23).

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- Two sample types (trilayer and superlattice) are studied with complementary probes: XRD/RSM, cross-sectional and plan-view STEM, LPFM, SS-PFM and in-situ biased STEM.
- The PFM sequences (Figs. 2–3) show a sizeable, polarity-dependent change in the written region, built up step by step, and the change is retained.
- A retention series out to 120 days (Fig. 4a–f) and a 20-day STEM revisit (Fig. 4g) are unusual and useful for a memory argument.
- Phase-field simulations of both geometries are included.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. **The PFM "vortex boundary motion" is not separated from tip-written polarization switching.**
   - What the data show. In Figs. 2a–i, 3a–f and 4a–b, the region the tip has passed over becomes uniform in LPFM amplitude and phase: a large single-phase-contrast band. The edge of that band is then called the "vortex boundary".
   - Why this undercuts the motion claim. The channels CHP1…CHP6 sit at successively more rightward positions in Fig. 2a–e. So the rightward "motion" may simply be the written region extending to where the tip was dragged next, the way conventional domain lithography works. It need not be a vortex texture translating. The reverse sweep with −5 V would then write the opposite state.
   - The claim of collective, nonlocal vortex motion (Conclusion: "long-range modifications across hundreds of nanometers") requires showing that the boundary moves **beyond** the tip trajectory, or moves when the tip is not over it. Requested controls:
     - repeated application through the **same** channel position, showing whether the boundary keeps advancing;
     - a channel placed some fixed distance from the boundary, giving boundary displacement against tip–boundary distance;
     - the same writing on a uniform a1/a2 or c-domain region, or on a reference PTO single layer without vortices, to show what ordinary domain writing looks like in this system.
   - The authors should also state the channel coordinates relative to the boundary for every step (Supplementary Figs. 4–7).

2. **What the written region contains is never identified.**
   - The vortex period is about 10–14 nm (RSM; Fig. 1a,b), but the PFM tip radius is 25 nm (Methods). The PFM features labelled "vortices" in Figs. 1c–d, 2, 3 and 5 are dark lines and patches hundreds of nm wide, so individual vortices cannot be resolved.
   - After writing, the region is featureless in amplitude. It could therefore be:
     - a vortex array of different orientation or chirality;
     - an a1/a2 or c-type ferroelectric phase created by the field (field-driven vortex→FE conversion is known in this system, ref. 23);
     - a region whose contrast is dominated by injected surface charge.
   - Without this, "vortex boundary motion" may in fact be "boundary of a field-converted phase". Requested evidence:
     - vertical PFM alongside LPFM for every step;
     - LPFM at two sample rotations (0° and 90°) to assign in-plane polarization components;
     - KPFM, or an electrostatic-force check, to rule out charge contributions;
     - most decisively, cross-sectional or plan-view STEM, or local XRD/nano-diffraction, taken **from inside a PFM-written channel**, showing that vortices are present on both sides of the boundary and what distinguishes the two sides.

3. **The in-situ STEM and the phase-field results describe a different phenomenon from the PFM experiment, at a different scale, and the simulations partly contradict the stability claim.**
   - Scale. In Fig. 2j, the cross-sectional STEM shows a nanometre-scale rearrangement of core heights (zig-zag "up-down-up" → linear alignment) over a few tens of nm. In the simulations (Fig. 2k–p) the "boundary" is a phase shift within a vortex array over about 20 nm. Neither shows the µm-scale displacement measured by PFM, nor that the PFM contrast boundary is a zig-zag/linear core boundary.
   - Field. Fig. 2j uses +17 V, Fig. 3g +20 V and Fig. 4g +15 V on a thinned lamella with a tungsten tip, against an SRO ground. The field distribution and its in-plane component are unknown and not comparable with 5 V through a PFM tip on a 100 nm film. The simulations give no applied-field magnitude, tip/channel parameters or field geometry.
   - Stability contradiction. The text (p. 8) says the simulated boundaries "exhibited stable behavior, maintaining their displaced positions" after field removal. The Fig. 2 caption for panel p says "Upon field removal, the vortex boundary returns to a configuration similar to the initial state".
   - Requested:
     - quantitative field values in the simulations;
     - a simulation at the PFM length scale, or an argument for why the mechanism carries over;
     - field-off simulated states;
     - a reconciliation of the zig-zag-switching mechanism (abstract) with the micron-scale PFM data.
   - Plan-view STEM, Fig. 3g(IV). The caption says all vortices align "along the (001)pc direction" in a plan-view image, which is geometrically inconsistent with a [001]-normal plan view. It should be corrected, and what panel IV shows should be explained.

4. **Reversibility is claimed but only partly shown, and the numbers are internally inconsistent.**
   - What the paper states:
     - Conclusion: "reversing the bias successfully returned the boundaries to their initial positions".
     - Trilayer: ~1170 nm forward vs ~890 nm back (Fig. 2).
     - Superlattice: the Fig. 3 caption gives ~1170 nm forward (again) vs ~643 nm back, and says that "After CHN3, the vortex boundary became disrupted". The text (p. 8) adds "disruptions observed after several field cycles".
   - Why it matters. Full return to the initial position is not demonstrated in either sample. The identical 1170 nm figure for two different samples with different numbers of steps (CHP6 vs CHP7) is suspicious.
   - Requested:
     - boundary position vs step number for every step in both samples, with how position was measured (line profiles) and its uncertainty;
     - more than one forward/back cycle, to establish whether motion is repeatable or degrades;
     - a cycle in at least one further region or sample.

B. Should address

5. **The SS-PFM evidence for reduced coercive bias at vortex boundaries is thin and may be artefactual.**
   - What is given. The Vc maps (Fig. 1e–f) and loops averaged from 10 points (Fig. 1g) are the main support for boundaries being "more susceptible to trailing electric fields".
   - Requested:
     - how "boundary" and "core" pixels were chosen;
     - the full distribution of Vc for each class over the 2500-point grid, with a statistical test;
     - checks for topography and electrostatic or contact-resonance crosstalk at boundaries.
   - Fig. 1e–f is labelled LPFM. SS-PFM switching with a vertical DC field is normally read out in VPFM, so which response the loops track should be stated.
   - The loop "On vortex" in Fig. 1g (superlattice) reaches a 180° phase difference. Coexisting vortex and a1/a2 phases at a sub-tip scale would make the interpretation ambiguous.

6. **The "stochastic" behaviour under pulsed fields rests on a single voltage ramp at one site.**
   - Fig. 5 shows one sequence (0–7 V in 0.5 V steps, cumulative on the same spot). Stochasticity requires repeated identical pulses, or repeated ramps on equivalent sites, with the distribution of outcomes.
   - Because the ramp is cumulative, the voltage dependence cannot be separated from history.
   - Fig. 5m–n draws smooth curves through 5–9 points, with displacements up to ±200 nm, and gives no error bars. The curves imply a functional dependence the data do not establish.
   - Requested: repeated trials, error bars, and removal of the guide-to-eye splines or a statement that they are guides only.

7. **"Vortex rings" and "pair of rings" are identified only from closed contours in PFM amplitude and plan-view STEM contrast.**
   - The topology (a closed vortex line, its polarization winding, its 3D structure) is not established. A closed PFM contrast loop at 200 nm scale could be a bubble domain or an a1/a2 island.
   - The STEM images in Fig. 5o–q are not tied to field application: it is not shown whether these rings were created by a bias or are present in the pristine film.
   - Requested: polarization mapping (e.g. DPC or atomic-displacement mapping) of a ring, and before/after imaging of the same STEM region, or toned-down wording.
   - The proposed origin (p. 11: "charge injection ... strain gradients, field gradient, broken symmetry, or boundary constraints") is a list rather than a tested mechanism.

8. **Charge injection and tip–surface electrochemistry are not excluded.**
   - Writing at ±5 V for one minute (and at up to 7 V for 1–3 min) injects surface and bulk charge. Under ambient conditions it can also cause electrochemical changes, especially for Pb-containing oxides, and both can alter PFM contrast.
   - The 120-day retention argues against purely transient charge, but topography before and after writing is not shown for any channel.
   - Requested: topography before and after writing, humidity, and KPFM decay after writing.

9. **The sample structure is described inconsistently, which affects comparison with the simulations.**
   - Main text (p. 4): "20 uc STO/20 uc PTO/20 uc STO trilayer".
   - Methods (p. 14): "20 uc PbTiO3/20 uc SrTiO3/20 uc PbTiO3 (PTO/STO/PTO) trilayer".
   - Figure 1 and 2 titles: STO/PTO/STO. Fig. 4 caption: "PTO/STO/PTO trilayer".
   - Phase field (p. 16): STO/PTO/STO.
   - Superlattice: [PTO16/STO12]10 is ~28 uc × 10 ≈ 110 nm, not "total 100nm".
   - Why it matters. A single 20 uc PTO layer clad by STO and two PTO layers separated by STO have different electrostatics and vortex structure. The PFM response and the phase-field comparison depend on which was grown. The authors should state the actual structure and confirm it from the cross-sectional STEM (Fig. 1b, Fig. 2j shows STO/PTO/STO).

10. **There are few regions and samples, with no statistics.**
    - What is stated. Fig. 4 repeats the trailing-field experiment on "a new region" of the trilayer. Otherwise each experiment appears to be one region on one sample.
    - Requested:
      - how many regions and samples were tried;
      - how often the boundary moved as reported;
      - whether any channel failed to move it;
      - the spread of displacement per step.
    - This matters for the device-oriented conclusions ("deterministic control", "reliable memory devices").

11. **The comparison with prior work is incomplete.**
    - Ref. 18 (Chen et al., "Electrically driven motion, destruction, and chirality change of polar vortices") and ref. 23 (field control of toroidal order) report electric-field manipulation of vortices in the same system.
    - The Introduction (p. 3) nonetheless says real-time observation of vortex or boundary motion "has yet to be realized", and the Conclusion says "This work first time demonstrates".
    - The authors should state explicitly what is new relative to refs. 18 and 23, e.g. the µm displacement, the reversibility and the retention.

C. Minor

12. **Several figure and text references disagree.**
    - The step labels in the text and captions differ:
      - text (p. 6): CHP1, CHP3, CHP5, CHP6; caption Fig. 2b: CHP2, CHP3;
      - text: CHN1, 3, 5, 6, with displacement "after CHN5"; caption: CHN1–CHN5;
      - text (p. 8): CHP5, CHP8 and CHN1, CHN4; Fig. 3 caption: CHP2–CHP7 and CHN1–CHN3.
    - Supplementary figure numbers disagree: the text gives Supplementary Figs. 4–5 for the trilayer steps, the captions Supplementary Figs. 3–4. Page 10 cites "Supplementary Fig. 10j-k" and "10l" where Fig. 11 is meant.
    - Fig. 4 caption (f) reads "after 60 days", while the panel and text say 120 days.
    - Fig. 1 caption labels both the loops and the simulations as "g" (the simulations should be h–i). The text (p. 6) also refers to simulation panels as "Fig. 1g".
    - Fig. 1a caption says vortices run along "[100]pc and [001]pc" in plan view; the text says [100]/[010].

13. **Colour scales are missing.** Several PFM panels (Figs. 2, 4a–e) have colour bars without numerical scales, so amplitude changes cannot be judged. Scale values should be added, and a consistent scale used across a sequence.

14. **The Methods give two drive amplitudes.** They state both 0.6–1.25 V and 0.75–1.25 V for imaging. With a drive of 1.25 V, it should be checked that imaging does not itself perturb the boundaries (e.g. repeated imaging with no writing).

15. **The energy argument is not quantified.** The energy profile (Supplementary Fig. 8, p. 8) describes switching "between two energy equivalent states". The authors should say what the two states are and give energies with units. The maximum "when the electric field direction switches from negative to positive" also needs a clearer link to the protocol.

16. **The device-motivation claims are not supported here.** These include sub-THz processing, 60 Tb/inch², and the IoT and large-language-model applications. They should be shortened or clearly separated from what is shown.

17. **The PFM tip velocity is not reconciled with timing.** A tip velocity of ~60 nm/s and "one minute" per channel imply ~3.6 µm per pass. The channel lengths and the number of passes should be given.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a correlative experiment: write a channel by PFM, then cut a FIB lamella across the written boundary and image it in STEM. This would directly show what lies on each side of the boundary seen in PFM, and would settle concerns 1–2.
2. Plot boundary position vs tip position and vs step for every step, with several forward/back cycles in at least two regions and both samples. This would separate motion beyond the tip from domain writing under the tip and quantify reversibility.
3. Run phase-field simulations with the actual tip bias and channel geometry, with field-off relaxed states, and compare boundary displacement per pass quantitatively with the PFM data.
4. Reword the claims to match the evidence: "tip-written boundaries" rather than "vortex motion" unless motion beyond the tip path is shown; "closed-loop textures" rather than "vortex rings" unless their topology is established; and "partially reversible" where return is incomplete.
