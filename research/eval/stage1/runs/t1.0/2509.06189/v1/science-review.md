A. Must address

1. **What the PFM "vortex boundary" is, and what the written region contains, is never established.** The vortex period is about 10–14 nm (RSM, Fig. 1a,b), and the PFM tip radius is 25 nm (Methods). Yet the PFM "vortices" and "vortex boundaries" in Figs. 1c–d, 2, 3 and 5 are dark meandering lines and patches hundreds of nanometres wide, imaged in 2–5 µm scans. Individual vortex cores cannot be resolved at this resolution, so the contrast must come from mesoscale regions: arrays of vortices with a given orientation, axial polarization or chirality (the net in-plane polarization of the array, as in refs. 1 and 23), or boundaries between vortex and a1/a2/FE regions. The text mixes these levels ("the low amplitude of these vortices results in their distinct dark contrast in phase images"; "the dark contrast in the amplitude represents the boundary of the vortex"). After writing, the swept region is featureless in LPFM amplitude with uniform phase (Figs. 2b–e, 3b–d, 4b). It could therefore be a vortex array of different orientation or chirality, an a1/a2 or c-type ferroelectric phase created by the field (field-driven vortex→FE conversion is known in this system, ref. 23), or a region whose contrast is dominated by injected charge. No data show that the swept region still contains vortices with the original ~10–14 nm period. The headline claim, "vortex motion", depends on this assignment. The authors should state explicitly what polarization component the PFM contrast reports; acquire vertical PFM alongside LPFM for every step, and LPFM at two sample rotations (0° and 90°) to reconstruct in-plane and out-of-plane components inside and outside the swept region; and, most decisively, cut a FIB lamella from inside a PFM-written region (across the written edge) and from a pristine region and image both by cross-sectional or plan-view STEM with polarization mapping (or DF-TEM, high-resolution PFM, or local XRD/nano-diffraction satellite peaks), showing whether vortices are present on both sides of the boundary and what distinguishes the two sides.

2. **Boundary "motion" is not separated from conventional tip-induced domain writing or phase conversion.** The channels (CHP1…CHP6 in Fig. 2a–e; CHP1, CHP2, CHP4 in Fig. 3b–d) are placed at successively more rightward positions, and the new boundary appears close to the most recently written channel. The rightward "motion" may therefore simply be the written region extending to where the tip was dragged next, as in PFM lithography, with the reverse sweep at −5 V writing the opposite state or erasing it. Further observations point the same way: the in-situ STEM sequences end with "all vortex cores in a single orientation" (Fig. 3g IV) or a reoriented array (Fig. 4g), which is reorientation or conversion rather than translation; and the negative sweeps leave a disrupted texture (Fig. 3f; Fig. 2i differs from Fig. 2a). The text and Methods say channels were used "one at a time" but give no channel coordinates. The Conclusion's "long-range modifications across hundreds of nanometers, reflecting the inherently collective and nonlocal nature" requires showing that the boundary moves beyond the tip trajectory, or moves when the tip is not over it. The authors should: overlay the tip path on each post-write image and report, for every step (including Supplementary Figs. 4–7), the channel position and the boundary position afterwards; apply bias repeatedly through the same channel position to see whether the boundary keeps advancing; place a channel at a fixed distance (several hundred nm) from, or parallel to, an existing boundary and report boundary displacement versus tip–boundary distance; report displacement per step versus channel spacing; show that vortices displaced from the swept area reappear beyond it (otherwise "motion" cannot be distinguished from "erasure and re-nucleation"); and perform the same writing on a uniform a1/a2 or c-domain region, or a vortex-free PTO reference layer, to show what ordinary domain writing looks like in this system.
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

3. **The claim that bias polarity sets the direction of motion has no mechanism and no control.** The field under a biased PFM tip is mainly out-of-plane and radially symmetric. The paper does not explain how a vertical field of one sign pushes a lateral boundary to the right and the opposite sign pushes it to the left. The direction may instead come from the order and position of the channels, since the channel is stepped rightward in the positive sequence and leftward in the negative one; if so, polarity dependence is not demonstrated. The authors should apply negative bias with channels stepped rightward and positive bias with channels stepped leftward, report the result, and state the scan direction of the tip within each channel.
Raised by: r1

4. **Reversibility, a central claim, is overstated and the numbers are internally inconsistent.** The abstract and Conclusion say reversed bias "successfully returned the boundaries to their initial positions", and p. 7 says it "effectively move[s] the boundary to its original position". But the trilayer moves ~1170 nm forward and ~890 nm back (Fig. 2e vs 2i), and the superlattice ~1170 nm forward and ~643 nm back (Fig. 3 caption), with the boundary "disrupted" after CHN3 (Fig. 3f) and "disruptions observed after several field cycles" (p. 8). Full return is shown in neither sample. The identical ~1170 nm forward value in two different samples with different numbers of steps (CHP6 vs CHP7) needs explaining; if it reflects channel layout, it bears on concern 2. The authors should plot boundary position against channel index for every forward and reverse step in both samples, stating how position was measured (line profiles) and with error bars from boundary-tracing uncertainty and image registration (drift correction against topographic markers); overlay pristine and final boundary positions and quantify the residual offset; compare the restored texture with the pristine one (e.g. 2D FFT period and orientation of the PFM amplitude); and take at least one region, and preferably a second region or sample, through several full forward/back cycles, reporting the number of cycles and their success rate, to judge reversibility against degradation.

B. Should address

5. **Charge injection, electrostatic and electrochemical artefacts in the PFM readout are not excluded.** Writing at ±5 V for one minute (and up to 7 V for 1–3 min in Fig. 5) in ambient conditions can inject surface or bulk charge, cause electrochemical changes (especially in Pb-containing oxides) or leave water-meniscus residues. All of these produce strong DART amplitude and phase contrast, including lateral signal through cantilever buckling and electrostatic crosstalk. The 120-day retention argues against purely transient charge but does not show that the contrast is vortex-related. Humidity, surface-potential maps and topography before and after writing are not reported for any channel. In addition, the Methods give two imaging drive ranges (0.6–1.25 V and 0.75–1.25 V); a 1.25 V drive may itself perturb a low-coercivity vortex state. The authors should provide KPFM of written areas and its decay over time; PFM at several drive amplitudes and frequencies showing the contrast is piezoelectric; topography before and after writing; humidity, and one write experiment in controlled atmosphere or after deliberate surface discharging; repeated imaging with no writing at 0.6 V and 1.25 V on the same area to show imaging does not move boundaries; and whether the written state survives surface cleaning or a mild anneal.
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

6. **The in-situ STEM experiments describe a different phenomenon, at a different scale and in a different field geometry, from the PFM experiments.** STEM uses +15 to +20 V (Fig. 2j +17 V, Fig. 3g +20 V, Fig. 4g +15 V) through a tungsten tip on FIB lamellae against an SRO ground; the field distribution, its in-plane component and the contact geometry are unknown and not comparable with 5 V through a PFM tip on a film. The authors acknowledge that thinning changes the boundary conditions (a1/a2 superdomains vanish, p. 5). What the images show is a rearrangement of core heights from zig-zag ("up-down-up") to linear within ~10–50 nm (Fig. 2j), or a change of vortex orientation ([100] vs [010]) over tens of nm (Figs. 3g, 4g), while PFM displacements are ~1 µm. Calling STEM "consistent with the PFM data" should be argued, not asserted, and it is not shown that the PFM contrast boundary is a zig-zag/linear core boundary. Electron-beam effects, current flow and tip-contact damage could also drive the changes, and only a few selected frames are shown. The authors should estimate the field and current in the lamella; report beam dose rates and charging, with a no-bias control at identical beam exposure; show more than one event, as time-resolved series or videos; and state how the 20-day stability (Fig. 4g IV) was measured — how the lamella was stored, whether it stayed in the holder, and whether exactly the same area was relocated.
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

7. **The phase-field simulations are under-specified, at the wrong scale, partly contradict the stability claim, and do not substantiate the stated mechanism.** The tip is "modelled by a Lorentz distribution", but the width, potential, applied field magnitude, channel geometry, domain-wall mobility L and electrical boundary conditions are not given, nor whether the SRO bottom electrode (as a short-circuit condition) and the actual stack (see concern 14) are modelled, nor how "Step 2 … Step 6" map onto experimental steps. The simulation boxes (e.g. 400×200 uc ≈ 160 nm for the trilayer; ~100 nm in Figs. 2k–p and 3h) are far smaller than the micrometre displacements claimed, and the simulated "boundary" is a phase shift within a vortex array over ~20 nm. The text (p. 8) says simulated boundaries "maintain[ed] their displaced positions" after field removal, but the Fig. 2 caption says that "upon field removal, the vortex boundary returns to a configuration similar to the initial state (p)", while panel p is labelled "Step 6" and described in the text as continued negative-field motion. The abstract's mechanism — that switching of the "zigzag patterns of the vortex core" controls boundary motion — rests on these simulations and one STEM sequence; no figure quantifies it and it is not tested against the PFM data. The energy profile (Supplementary Fig. 8) describes switching "between two energy equivalent states" with a maximum "when the electric field direction switches from negative to positive", without identifying the two states, giving energies with units, showing they are degenerate, or saying what sets the barrier. The authors should specify the model fully; provide core-position or tilt maps, energy versus step and a field-threshold scan; give field-off relaxed states; run a simulation at the PFM length scale with the actual tip bias and channel geometry, or argue why the mechanism carries over; and use the model to predict a measurable dependence (threshold bias, its thickness dependence, displacement per pass, or polarity/channel-direction dependence) that experiment then tests.

8. **Sample statistics and reproducibility are not reported.** Each main protocol appears to be shown once per sample type: one trilayer and one superlattice boundary-motion sequence, one retention region (Fig. 4, "a new region" of the trilayer), and one pulsed-field site (Fig. 5). The paper's selling points — "deterministic control", "reproducibility and reliability" (p. 7) and "reliable memory devices" — require statistics. The authors should report how many samples, growths, regions and tips were used; how often the boundary moved as reported, failed to move or moved irregularly; the spread of displacement per step; and whether a second growth of each structure behaves the same, ideally as a table of all attempted experiments including failures.

9. **Dependences on writing parameters are missing.** All trailing-bias experiments use ±5 V, 1 min and ~60 nm/s. The threshold voltage for boundary motion and how displacement per pass scales with voltage, tip speed, number of passes and channel offset would test the claimed "reduced energy barrier" mechanism and separate it from domain writing, which has its own characteristic voltage and time scaling. The authors should provide these dependences, at least in the trilayer.

10. **"Real-time observation" is not demonstrated.** The abstract claims "real time observation of vortex motion", but all PFM data are before/after snapshots taken after the field is removed, and the STEM data are presented as discrete frames. No velocity, time constant, or dependence on pulse duration or tip speed is reported. The authors should provide genuinely time-resolved data (e.g. timestamped STEM movies, or displacement versus write time and tip velocity) or rephrase the claim.
Raised by: r1, r3

11. **The SS-PFM evidence for reduced coercive bias at vortex boundaries is thin and possibly artefactual.** The Vc maps (Fig. 1e–f) look noisy at the pixel level, and the loops in Fig. 1g are averages over 10 "representative" points per category with no selection criterion or statistics; yet they are the main support for boundaries being "more susceptible to trailing electric fields". The panels are labelled LPFM, while switching spectroscopy uses a vertical DC field and is normally read in VPFM; which signal the loops track, and what "nucleation bias" means for a vortex with largely in-plane rotating polarization, is unclear. Boundary pixels have low amplitude, which biases phase-based coercive-bias extraction. The superlattice "On vortex" loop reaches a 180° phase difference, and coexisting vortex and a1/a2 phases at sub-tip scale would make it ambiguous. It is also unclear how a "vortex boundary" at the 40 nm grid pitch relates to the µm-scale boundary later moved. The authors should classify all 2500 grid points with a stated objective mask (e.g. from the simultaneously acquired amplitude) and show histograms of Vc+, Vc− and imprint for boundary and interior pixels with means, standard errors and a statistical test; state whether the loops are vertical or lateral and show amplitude loops; address imprint and electrostatic or contact-resonance contributions; and show topography for the same grid.

12. **The "stochastic" behaviour under pulsed fields rests on a single cumulative voltage ramp at one site.** Fig. 5 shows one sequence (0–7 V in 0.5 V steps on the same spot), so voltage dependence and cumulative history are confounded. Fig. 5m–n draws smooth curves through 5–9 points from 0 to 4 V, with displacements up to ±200 nm and no error bars; a smooth fit implies a deterministic trend, contradicting the stochastic interpretation, and the data beyond 4 V (to 7 V) are omitted. The authors should repeat identical pulses at fixed voltage, at several equivalent sites, and report the distribution of outcomes; give displacement uncertainties (pixel size, drift correction); and remove the curves or state that they are guides to the eye only.
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

13. **"Vortex rings", "pairs of rings" and "hidden topological phases" lack structural evidence.** The PFM "rings" (Fig. 5) are closed contours of amplitude hundreds of nanometres across, which tip-written bubble domains or a1/a2 islands also produce. The STEM "rings" (Fig. 5o–q) are closed loops in stripe contrast, or white boxes around bright point features, with no polarization mapping, and it is not stated whether they were field-induced or present in the pristine film. Neither is shown to be a vortex ring in the topological sense (closed vortex-core line, polarization winding, 3D structure). The proposed origin (p. 11: "charge injection ... strain gradients, field gradient, broken symmetry, or boundary constraints") is a list rather than a tested mechanism. The authors should provide polarization-resolved imaging of a ring (atom-resolved displacement maps, DPC or 4D-STEM, ideally 3D) and before/after imaging of the same STEM region under bias, or rephrase as "closed-loop textures" and drop "hidden topological phases".
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

14. **The trilayer stack is described inconsistently.** The main text (p. 4: "20 uc STO/20 uc PTO/20 uc STO"), the Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 titles, the phase-field set-up (p. 16) and the cross-sectional STEM in Fig. 2j give STO/PTO/STO, whereas the Methods (p. 14: "20 uc PbTiO3/20 uc SrTiO3/20 uc PbTiO3") and the Fig. 4 caption give PTO/STO/PTO. A single 20 uc PTO layer clad by STO and two PTO layers separated by STO have different electrostatic boundary conditions, vortex structure and stability, so the PFM response and the phase-field comparison depend on which was grown. The superlattice [PTO16/STO12]10 is ~28 uc × 10 ≈ 110 nm, not "total 100nm". The authors should state the actual structure, confirm it from cross-sectional STEM (Fig. 1b, 2j), correct the text throughout, and state whether the SRO electrode is present in the trilayer and in the simulations.

15. **The novelty claims need to be reconciled with prior work.** The Introduction (p. 3) says real-time electric-field-driven vortex or boundary motion "has yet to be realized" and the Conclusion says "this work first time demonstrates" it, yet the paper cites Chen et al.
