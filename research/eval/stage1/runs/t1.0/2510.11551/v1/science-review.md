A. Must address

1. The slow relaxation component has not been shown to come from a random-singlet state rather than from simpler origins (Fig. 3a, 3c, 3d; "Slow T1 component" section, pp. 2–4). The RS claim rests entirely on the slow rate 1/T1s and its weight w2, measured on peak P5. The paper assigns P5 to "either the H(3) site in water molecules or to protons in the benzoate groups" (p. 2), the field is admittedly off the b-axis, and Fig. 2a shows the lines are not fully resolved at 0.2 T. P5 is therefore probably a superposition of inequivalent protons with different hyperfine couplings and form factors. Because 1/T1 scales with the square of the hyperfine coupling, such a superposition gives a two-component recovery with rates two orders of magnitude apart. In a Heisenberg chain, a site whose form factor filters out the q = π fluctuations sees only the q ≈ 0 contribution, which itself gives 1/T1 ∝ T at a much smaller rate: exactly the phenomenology attributed here to the RS state. Other explanations that fit the data equally well are not excluded: (a) nuclei relaxing through a channel that becomes visible only once the fast channel saturates; (b) paramagnetic impurities, chain ends or orphan spins acting as local relaxation centres, including Zeeman polarization of weakly coupled spins that suppresses their fluctuations and leaves nearby protons relaxing slowly; (c) a temperature-dependent w2 produced by signal wipeout of the fast-relaxing nuclei through a shortening T2, or by changing pulse-excitation conditions; (d) a field-induced effect (see concern 3). The authors should: (i) measure the recovery at low temperature on well-resolved single-site lines (P1–P4, H(1)/H(2)) and show whether the same slow component with the same w2(T) appears there, or explain why that is impossible; (ii) quote the slow fraction implied by site multiplicities if P5 contains several sites; (iii) show the integrated spectral intensity × T versus T at 0.2 T to test for wipeout, and T2 of both components; (iv) show w2(T) at a second field and in a second orientation. Without these, the "two spatially distinct contributions" picture (p. 4) is an assumption, and the slow channel cannot be attributed to an RS state.

2. The link between the ILTA weight w2 and a correlation length, and the claimed −ln T law, are not derived and conflict with RS theory (Fig. 3d, 3e; p. 4, "Within the 1D limit, the increase of w2 directly reflects the growth of the thermal correlation length"). No model connects the fraction of nuclei in the slow channel to ξ, yet the headline claim "ξ ∝ −ln T" rests entirely on this identification. At the Dasgupta–Ma/Fisher infinite-randomness fixed point (Ref. 16), length and energy are related by ln(Ω0/Ω) ∝ L^(1/2), so ξ ∝ ln²(Ω0/T), not ln(1/T); the fraction of undecimated spins falls as 1/ln²(Ω0/T). Neither gives w2 linear in ln T. The statement that ξ ∼ T^(−1/z) with z → ∞ "signifies" an infinite-randomness fixed point does not fix the power of the logarithm, so the conclusion does not follow as written. Experimentally, the fit in Fig. 3d spans about one decade (0.03–0.3 K, roughly six to eight points) with no error bars, the w2 = 0 points above 0.4 K are excluded by construction, and the extrapolation that the RS state "percolate[s] throughout the entire system… near 8 mK" (w2 → 1) is an artefact of the chosen form. The authors should give an explicit model, analytical or numerical, in which w2 is a known function of ξ or of the density of unpaired spins (for example the fraction of protons within some distance of a decimated strong bond), and that also predicts 1/T1s(T); or else drop the correlation-length language and present w2(T) as an empirical result. They should compare ln, ln² and weak power-law or saturating-crossover fits on the same data with fit quality and uncertainties, and remove or label as speculative the 8 mK extrapolation.

3. At 0.2 T the system is not in the zero-field gapless regime, and the onset of the slow channel coincides with field-generated energy scales (Fig. 3b–d, 4d; Discussion, p. 5). Scaling the measured gap with the authors' own H^(2/3) law (about 0.2 meV at 5 T, or about 0.18 meV at 4 T, in Fig. 4d) gives Δ(0.2 T) ≈ 0.023–0.024 meV ≈ 0.27–0.3 K. The Zeeman energy gμBH/kB at 0.2 T is about 0.27 K. Both are essentially equal to T0 ≈ 0.4 K, where the slow component appears, and lie well above the 0.03 K base temperature. In this regime singlets with couplings below the Zeeman energy are polarized and the staggered field opens a gap, so the low-energy scaling of a random chain differs from the zero-field RS fixed point. The argument on p. 5 that field-polarized singlets with Δ ≈ gμBH give "effective gapless excitations" is qualitative and does not address the staggered-field gap at all. The decisive test is the field dependence of the slow channel. The authors should analyse the 0.5 T data (Supplementary Fig. S3) exactly as in Fig. 3d, add 1 T (and ideally 0.1 and 2 T) if possible, and plot T0, a, the onset temperature and 1/T1s against H together with Δ(H). A T0 that tracks H or the H^(2/3) gap, or a slow channel that vanishes as the gap grows, would point to a field-induced origin; a field-independent T0 would support disorder. Alternatively, a calculation (QMC or tanTRG with random bonds, uniform field and staggered field) should show that w2 ∝ −ln T survives at h = 0.2 T and extrapolates to zero field.

B. Should address

5. The quenched disorder is not identified or characterized (Materials and methods; p. 4, "weak quenched disorder unavoidable in real materials"). The paper attributes the RS state to bond disorder of strength ΔJ/J ≈ 0.2 but does not say what it is (water loss from the readily dehydrating trihydrate, Cu vacancies, chemical impurities, stacking or bond defects, surface degradation during measurement), and gives no independent estimate of its concentration, even while stressing "high-quality" crystals. Describing the disorder as "weak" while it controls about 60% of the relaxation weight at 0.04 K also needs discussion. The authors should provide independent characterization (a low-temperature Curie tail in the susceptibility, ESR/EPR count of free spins, impurity concentration, water-of-crystallization stoichiometry, the magnitude of the Kn and linewidth tails), relate it to ΔJ/J ≈ 0.2, and check that it is consistent with the amplitude a ≈ 25%.

7. The robustness of the ILTA decomposition and the uncertainties on w2 and 1/T1s are not shown (Fig. 3a, 3c, 3d; Methods). The peak positions, widths and weights from Tikhonov-regularized inversion depend on the regularization parameter, noise, the time window (the slow channel at 30 mK has T1 of order 3–10 s, near the end of the recovery window in Fig. 3a) and imperfect inversion (M(0) ≠ −M(∞)). The breadth of the slow-component distribution, cited on p. 4 as evidence for RS physics, is partly set by the regularization. The text also says single-exponential fits are used above 0.4 K and biexponential fits "below 0.1 K" (p. 2; Fig. 3a caption, "0.1 K and below"), yet w2 is nonzero from about 0.3 K, so the procedure between 0.1 and 0.4 K is unstated. The authors should give error bars on w2 and 1/T1s, state the regularization parameter and show w2(T) is stable over a range of it, cross-check against a_s/(a_f + a_s) from direct biexponential fits and against stretched-exponential fits, show whether the intrinsic width of the slow distribution is resolution-limited, state the fitting procedure between 0.1 and 0.4 K, and ensure the longest recovery delay exceeds about 5 T1s at every temperature.

8. The QMC comparison (Fig. 3e; Supplementary Sec. S4) is not independent or quantitative. ΔJ/J ≈ 0.2 appears chosen by eye rather than from any measurement. 9. The linear-in-T slow rate, 1/T1s ∝ T (Fig. 3c), is presented as the RS signature but is not connected to any RS prediction. 11. The spinon assignment is only qualitative (Fig. 3b). For the S = 1/2 Heisenberg chain, 1/T1 from the q ≈ π channel carries a multiplicative ln^(1/2)(J/T) correction (Sachdev, Ref. 33; Sandvik; Starykh, Singh and Sandvik), which over T/J ≈ 0.002–0.08 changes 1/T1 by tens of percent, plus a q ≈ 0 diffusive term ∝ T. A constant 1/T1 is necessary but not unique. Whether P5's hyperfine form factor filters the staggered fluctuations decides which term is seen at all; if it does, 1/T1 is not a direct measure of the η = 1 spinon correlations. The authors should state the form factor of P5 at q = π versus q = 0, and compare the magnitude and temperature dependence of 1/T1f with the field-theory prediction including the log correction, using J ≈ 18 K and the P5 hyperfine coupling from a Kn–χ plot.

12. The 1 T and 2 T relaxation data contradict a simple gapped soliton picture and are not discussed (Fig. 3b, 4c; Fig. 1). 13. The extraction of the soliton gap from Kn and 1/T1 is not justified (Fig. 4b–d, p. 5). The low-temperature upturn of |Kn| is attributed (Fig. 4e) to growth of the field-induced staggered magnetization, an order-parameter-like quantity, yet Δ is extracted with Kn = a·e^(−Δ/kBT) + b, the form of a thermally activated uniform susceptibility, which would make the shift decrease toward zero rather than grow and saturate. In the sine-Gordon description (Essler 1999) the spectrum also contains breathers, and the lightest breather B1 can lie at or below the soliton mass; Kn should be set by the lightest excitation, while 1/T1 at the NMR frequency requires two-particle (Raman) processes whose activation energy need not equal the single-particle mass. The "excellent agreement" with INS is overstated: in Fig. 4d the INS points near 7–8 T lie well below the NMR points. The authors should state which excitation each probe measures, justify the fit forms or use sine-Gordon expressions for the susceptibility and 1/T1, test the procedure by fitting the tanTRG Hhf(T) curve (with known gap) using the same form, and give uncertainties on Δ at each field.
