Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper reports ¹H NMR on single-crystal copper benzoate down to 0.03 K and 0.2 T. From it the authors identify three regimes: spinons (constant 1/T1, 1/T2G ∝ T^-1/2), field-induced sine-Gordon solitons (gap Δ ∝ H^~2/3 from Knight shift and 1/T1), and a low-temperature, low-field "random-singlet-like" regime. The evidence for that regime is a second, slow relaxation channel with 1/T1s ∝ T, a spectral weight w2 that grows as −ln(T/T0), and a Knight-shift upturn fitted to T^-1 ln^-2(Ω/T). The spinon and soliton parts largely confirm known physics of this material with a new local probe. The new and most interesting claim is that a very weakly disordered (ΔJ/J ≈ 0.03) Heisenberg chain crosses over to random-singlet-like behaviour at T ≈ 0.02J. If it holds, this matters for the interplay of integrability and disorder. At present, however, that claim rests on a phenomenological reading of a two-component recovery from a single crystal, and several obvious alternative explanations are not excluded.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- Measurements reach an unusually wide range (0.03–100 K, 0.2–10 T) on a well-characterised quasi-1D S = 1/2 chain. The magnetic order temperature of ≲0.8 mK makes the chain effectively one-dimensional throughout.
- The constant 1/T1f over more than a decade (0.03–1.4 K) at 0.2 T is clean, direct evidence for the T-independent q = π spinon relaxation of the HAFC.
- The field-induced gap is extracted with two probes (Kn and 1/T1). It agrees quantitatively with specific heat and INS (Fig. 4e), and LTRG reproduces the uniform magnetisation from the DM-induced spin canting (Fig. 4c), which is a nice quantitative check.
- The heating checks (randomised recovery delays, pulse power varied by ±6 dB, repetition time varied by a factor of 4) are explicitly described.
- ILTA of the recovery curves gives a direct picture of the distribution of relaxation rates (Fig. 3c).

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The growth of w2 may be an artefact of spin-echo T2 weighting. The headline RS evidence is w2(T) = −c ln(T/T0), rising from ~0 at 0.4 K to 65% at 0.03 K (Fig. 3d). The same figure (Fig. 3b inset; p. 2) shows that 1/T2G of the spinon (fast) component diverges on cooling. T2G "becomes too short to be reliably measured" below 0.4 K, which is exactly where w2 starts to grow. In a spin-echo measurement, the echo intensity of a component is reduced by roughly exp(−2τ/T2) (Gaussian: exp[−(2τ/T2G)²]). If the fast-T1 component also has the fast-T2 decay, its contribution to M(t) is progressively suppressed on cooling. The apparent weight of the slow component, whose T2 is presumably longer, then grows even if the physical volume fraction is constant. The text says only that the w2 are "calculated from the P(1/T1) distribution"; no T2 correction is mentioned.
   The authors should:
   - measure (or bound) T2 separately for the fast and slow T1 components, for example with a T1–T2 two-dimensional correlation (ref. 45, which they cite);
   - correct w2 for echo-delay weighting, or show w2 at several echo delays τ extrapolated to τ → 0;
   - check the total echo intensity × T against the Curie expectation at 0.2 T to see whether signal is being lost.
   Without this, the −ln T law and the extrapolation "w2 growing towards 100%" (p. 6) are not established.

2. The slow component may come from a second, distinct proton site rather than from disorder. The P5 line on which the two-component recovery is measured is "attributed either to the H(3) site in water molecules or to protons in the benzoate groups" (p. 2). Peak P5 may therefore contain inequivalent protons with very different hyperfine form factors. A proton whose transferred hyperfine field cancels at q = π (for example, one located symmetrically with respect to two Cu ions) is relaxed only by q ≈ 0 fluctuations. For the HAFC those give 1/T1 ∝ T, which is precisely the T-linear, two-orders-slower behaviour attributed to the RS component. The authors themselves note that a 1/T1 ∝ T term from q = 0 fluctuations appears above 4 K. Supplementary Note 5 reportedly rules out "spectral overlap" through the frequency dependence of M(t), but the reader cannot see that test, and the main text does not engage with the form-factor scenario.
   The authors should:
   - compute the q = 0 and q = π hyperfine form factors for every proton plausibly contributing to P5, using the hyperfine tensors of Supplementary Note 2;
   - show the two-component recovery (and its −ln T weight) on a second, well-resolved line, ideally H(1), whose large coupling the authors use for Kn;
   - show that the slow component is absent above 0.4 K for a physical reason, not merely because it is buried under the fast one.

3. The disorder scale is inconsistent with theory for weak disorder. The paper concludes that ΔJ/J ≈ 0.02–0.05 drives an RS-like crossover at T0 ≈ 0.4 K ≈ 0.02J (pp. 4, 6). For weak bond disorder in the S = 1/2 Heisenberg chain, disorder is marginally relevant. The crossover scale to the infinite-randomness regime is expected to be exponentially small in the inverse disorder strength (Doty–Fisher, Fisher 1994, ref. 16). For 3% disorder on only 10% of the bonds, it would naively lie far below 30 mK.
   The QMC inset of Fig. 3d (ξ/a ∝ −ln(T/T0) for T/J ≈ 0.01–0.1) is offered as support. However:
   - ξ is not compared with the clean chain under the same QMC conditions. Clean-chain ξ ∝ 1/T with logarithmic corrections, so how different the two curves really are in this window is not shown.
   - The link between ξ(T) and the NMR volume fraction w2 is not derived. The text calls it "phenomenological" (p. 4).
   - Only 32 realisations of L = 256 chains are used, with no finite-size check.
   - The bimodal distribution (10% of bonds, ΔJ = +0.03) is not motivated by any independent measurement.
   The authors should:
   - show QMC for ΔJ = 0 alongside, and for several ΔJ and L;
   - compute a quantity directly comparable to NMR, such as the distribution of local 1/T1 or the local susceptibility, rather than ξ;
   - discuss explicitly why a crossover at 0.02J is expected for such weak disorder, or else reconsider the identification.

4. Only one crystal is measured, and there is no control where the disorder is varied. The RS-like interpretation rests entirely on "weak quenched disorder" whose origin is speculative ("domain-type…stress or unbalanced benzoate group rotation freezing", p. 5). The NMR was done on one sample ("Three samples were used for the NMR, specific heat and susceptibility measurements, respectively", p. 7).
   - If the slow channel is disorder-driven, w2(T), T0 and c should vary between crystals, and between differently grown or annealed or deliberately disordered samples (for example a lightly substituted or partially deuterated crystal).
   - If it is intrinsic, they should not vary.
   The authors should show the 0.2 T two-component recovery and w2(T) on at least one other crystal, preferably one with a different disorder level, and include a thermal-cycling check. Structural characterisation of the claimed disorder (X-ray mosaic/diffuse scattering, low-T linewidth versus crystal) would also help.

5. The Knight-shift evidence is fitted over a narrow window and the lowest-T data are discarded. The divergence Kn ∝ T^-1 ln^-2(Ω/T) (Fig. 3e) is fitted only between roughly 0.2 and 0.8 K, and the authors note that "the above fit is only applied within a narrow temperature window". The downturn below 0.2 K, exactly where the RS regime should dominate (w2 > 50%), is attributed to a signal-to-noise artefact (p. 5). Over this window a free Curie term (a + C/T), a Curie–Weiss form, or a boundary-induced (Eggert–Affleck) chain-end contribution would likely fit equally well.
   The authors should:
   - compare these alternative forms quantitatively (χ², number of free parameters);
   - show the full spectra below 0.2 K with the low-frequency tail and the spectrometer bandwidth;
   - either recover the missing weight (frequency-stepped sweeps covering the tail) or exclude those points from all conclusions consistently.
   They also report w2 from the spectrum (Supp. Fig. 5c) with a different T0 than from T1. Both T0 values should be quoted in the main text, because they bear directly on whether the two probes see the same component.

B. Should address

6. There is no calculation of 1/T1 in the RS state. The claim that "1/T1s ∝ T signals a type of gapless excitation… a direct consequence of quenched disorder" is supported only by analogy to a thermal-conductivity result (ref. 49, quadratic T dependence). The paper gives no prediction of the local 1/T1 for an RS-like state at T ≈ 0.02J and H = 0.2 T. Earlier work on random chains (ref. 50, Shiroka et al.) shows a broad, stretched-exponential distribution of 1/T1 rather than a second discrete component two orders of magnitude slower. The bimodal P(1/T1) in Fig. 3c is not obviously what an RS state produces. The authors should derive or cite a quantitative expectation for the magnitude, T dependence and distribution width of 1/T1 in the RS-like regions, and compare with the measured 1/T1s and the width of the slow lobe in Fig. 3c.

7. The field-induced gap competes with the RS interpretation at 0.2 T. From Fig. 4e, Δ ∝ H^2/3 extrapolates to roughly 0.1–0.15 K at 0.2 T (and ~0.25 K at 0.5 T). The downturn of 1/T1f below 0.3 K at 0.2 T is itself attributed to solitons (p. 2). The RS-like signals therefore appear in the same temperature range as the onset of the soliton gap and the Zeeman scale gμBH ≈ 0.27 K.
   The authors should:
   - show w2(T), T0 and the 1/T1s slope at 0.5 and 1.0 T in the main text (currently only Supp. Fig. 10);
   - extend the measurement to lower field if possible;
   - demonstrate how the slow channel evolves with field.
   The phase-separation argument on p. 6 (Supplementary Note 8) needs quantitative support. The regime boundaries in Fig. 1 should also be marked as schematic, with the data points used to construct them shown.

8. The field misalignment is not quantified, which affects the soliton gap. The authors infer from the line count that the field was "slightly away from the crystallographic b-axis" (p. 2). In copper benzoate, the staggered field h = c(θ)H and hence Δ depend strongly on field direction. The comparison with Cp and INS (Fig. 4e), measured at possibly different orientations, and the exponent α = 0.8(1) both depend on this. The authors should estimate the misalignment angle from the proton line positions and hyperfine tensors, and propagate it into the expected staggered-field coefficient and the Fig. 4e comparison.

9. The gap-fitting forms are too simple. The authors should address three points:
   - Kn = a e^−Δ/kBT + b ignores the power-law prefactors expected for the thermally activated soliton susceptibility.
   - 1/T1 = a e^−Δ/kBT assumes single-soliton activation. In the sine-Gordon model the relevant NMR processes (intra-band soliton scattering versus soliton–antisoliton creation, or breather contributions) can give different activation energies, for example Δ versus 2Δ, and prefactors.
   - The "+b" offset is attributed to DM-induced canting, but in the fits it is a free constant.
   The fitted α = 0.8(1) is only marginally consistent with 2/3. The authors should state which process dominates 1/T1, fit with theory-motivated prefactors, constrain b with the LTRG magnetisation, and report how Δ changes with the fit window.

10. Thermalisation of the insulating crystal at 30 mK is not demonstrated. The heating checks confirm that RF pulses do not heat the sample on the timescale of M(t). They do not show that the crystal (physically separated from the coil, with only lattice phonon conduction) reaches the mixing-chamber temperature. The authors should show a thermometric NMR quantity that keeps evolving down to 30 mK (for example the Curie-like line shift or w2 itself at various waiting times), or a cooling-time test showing it does. If the crystal saturates near 60–80 mK, the "w2 = 65% at 0.03 K" point and the −ln T extension change.

11. The ILTA results depend on the regularisation. w2 and the separation into two lobes come from Tikhonov ILTA with a single α = 0.001, chosen "to provide stability without inducing excessive smoothing" (p. 7). The authors should show how w2(T) depends on α over at least a decade, and compare with a direct two-exponential (or stretched-plus-single) fit of M(t). They should also state how the slow-component weight is constrained when 1/T1s is so small that its recovery is incomplete at the longest delay, given that the M(t) data in Fig. 3a extend to ~10 s.

12. The spinon evidence from 1/T2G is weak. The fit 1/T2G ∝ T^-1/2 (Fig. 3b inset) spans 0.4–2 K, less than a decade, and the expected logarithmic corrections to 1/T1 are not observed (p. 2). The authors should quantify the range and uncertainty of the T^-1/2 exponent. They should also discuss why the logarithmic correction is absent, given that it was seen in Sr2CuO3, rather than attributing its absence to the onset of solitons. If disorder is already acting below 0.4 K, it could also mask the log term.

13. The thermal-conductivity link is asserted, not shown. The statement that RS-like behaviour "readily accounts" for the thermal-conductivity drop below 0.3 K (ref. 29, p. 6) is qualitative. The authors should compare the temperature scales and sample batches, since the samples come from the same batch as ref. 29, or soften the statement.

C. Minor

14. Bulk susceptibility is reported only above 1.8 K (Methods). A low-temperature (dilution) magnetisation or AC susceptibility measurement would independently test the claimed T^-1 ln^-2 enhancement. If it is unavailable, say so.

15. Fig. 1: state the field values at which the data were taken and draw the boundaries as dashed lines, since they are interpolated from six fields.

16. Fig. 3d: the main-panel fit gives T0 = 0.40(2) K and c = 25(1)%. Please quote the fit range and how many points are below 0.1 K. The inset should state L, the number of realisations and the error-bar meaning.

17. Fig. 3e: the legend labels "RS-like" (circles) and "Spinon" (squares), but the text describes a single H(1) Knight shift. Clarify how the two sets are separated spectrally, since this is central to concern 5.

18. p. 2: "1/T1 ~ T^-0.25" from an early NMR study (ref. 30) is mentioned but never revisited. Briefly explain how the present two-component picture reconciles with it.

19. Text and figures give different disorder estimates: ΔJ/J ≈ 0.03 (Fig. 3d inset) versus ≈ 0.02–0.05 (Discussion). Please explain how the range was derived.

20. Methods, NMR: give the echo delay τ used for M(t) and the spectra at low T, which is needed to evaluate concern 1.

21. The phrase "nearly doubling of the Knight shift… and threefold increase of the FWHM" (p. 6) should cite the specific figure panels.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Settle the origin of the slow channel by measuring T1 on a second, spectrally isolated line (H(1)) and by T1–T2 correlation spectroscopy. This simultaneously addresses the echo-weighting and site-overlap alternatives (concerns 1–2).
2. Measure a second crystal, ideally with deliberately enhanced disorder, and show that T0 and c track the disorder level. This is the most direct test that the slow channel is disorder-driven (concern 4).
3. Replace the ξ(T) comparison with QMC or strong-disorder RG predictions of quantities NMR measures (the local susceptibility distribution, P(1/T1)), with a clean-chain reference (concerns 3, 6).
4. Present the field dependence of w2(T) at 0.2, 0.5 and 1 T in the main text, so the RS-like and soliton/Zeeman regimes are separated by data rather than by argument (concern 7).
