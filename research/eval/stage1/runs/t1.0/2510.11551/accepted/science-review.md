## A. Must address

1. **The staggered-field (soliton) physics is not excluded at 0.2 T, even though it sets in at the temperatures where the "RS-like" behaviour appears.** The authors' own gap data (Fig. 4e: Δ ≈ 0.3 meV ≈ 3.5 K at 10 T, with Δ ∝ H^0.8 or H^2/3) extrapolate to Δ ≈ 0.1–0.25 K at 0.2 T (and ~0.25 K at 0.5 T). The Zeeman scale gμBH is ≈ 0.27 K. These are the same scales as the 0.4 K crossover, the growth of w2 and the downturn of the fast 1/T1f below 0.2–0.3 K. The text itself attributes that downturn to a "field-induced origin ... soliton behaviours" (p. 2). At 2 T and 5 T, the DM-induced canting produces a low-temperature upturn of Kn (Fig. 4b,c), which LTRG reproduces (Fig. 4c). This looks qualitatively like the 0.2 T upturn in Fig. 3e that is fitted to the RS form, shifted to lower temperature. The Discussion's argument that there is "no Zeeman gap" at 0.2 T therefore has to be demonstrated, not asserted. If the clean staggered-field model already gives an upturn of the observed size, the Kn evidence for an RS-like state disappears. The authors should: (i) run the same LTRG calculation (staggered field plus DM canting) at 0.2 T, 0.5 T and 1 T and overlay it on Fig. 3e and on 1/T1f in Fig. 3b; (ii) show quantitatively that neither the Kn upturn nor the onset of the slow channel at 0.2 T can come from the field-induced staggered magnetization of the clean chain; (iii) present w2(T), T0 and the 1/T1s slope at 0.5 T and 1.0 T quantitatively in the main text (currently only ILTA maps in Supplementary Fig. 10), showing how the slow channel evolves with field and that this differs from what a soliton gap ∝ H^2/3 would produce; (iv) ideally, add data at lower field (e.g. 0.1 T) or with the field along a direction that minimizes the staggered-field coefficient. That coefficient depends strongly on field direction in copper benzoate, so this is a natural null control: an RS-like signal that survives where the staggered field is suppressed would be strong evidence.

2. **The growth of the slow-component weight w2 may be an artefact of spin-echo T2 weighting, not a growing RS-like volume fraction.** The headline RS evidence is w2(T) = −c ln(T/T0), rising from ~0 at 0.4 K to ~65% at 0.03 K (Fig. 3d). Yet 1/T2G of the spinon (fast) component diverges on cooling and "becomes too short to be reliably measured" below 0.4 K (Fig. 3b inset, p. 2), exactly where w2 starts to grow. In a spin-echo measurement the echo intensity of each component is reduced by roughly exp(−2τ/T2), or exp[−(2τ/T2G)²] for a Gaussian decay. Nuclei coupled strongly to the critical spinon fluctuations (fast T1, short T2) therefore lose amplitude at fixed τ, while slow-T1 nuclei, which plausibly also have long T2, keep theirs. The apparent fraction of the slow component would then grow on cooling even if the true population were fixed. The text says only that w2 is "calculated from the P(1/T1) distribution"; no T2 correction is mentioned, and the echo delay τ used for M(t) and for the low-temperature spectra is not given (Methods). The spectral w2 (Supplementary Fig. 5c) is subject to the same weighting, so it is not an independent check. The authors should: state τ and measure or bound T2 separately for the fast and slow components at each temperature, for example by T1–T2 two-dimensional correlation (ref. 45); measure M(t) at several echo delays and extrapolate the component amplitudes to τ → 0; and check that the total echo intensity × T follows the Curie expectation through 0.4 K, i.e. that no fast-relaxing intensity goes missing. Without this, the −ln T law, the parameters T0 and c, the "effective volume fraction" picture and the extrapolation "w2 growing towards 100%" (p. 6) are not established.

3. **The slow relaxation channel may come from a second proton species, from defects, or from molecular motion, rather than from RS-like regions.** The two-component recovery is measured on line P5, which is attributed "either to the H(3) site in water molecules or to protons in the benzoate groups" (p. 2). P5 may therefore contain inequivalent protons with very different hyperfine couplings and form factors. A proton whose transferred hyperfine field cancels at q = π (e.g. one located symmetrically with respect to two Cu ions) is relaxed only by q ≈ 0 fluctuations, which for the HAFC give 1/T1 ∝ T: precisely the T-linear, two-orders-slower behaviour attributed to the RS component. The authors themselves note a 1/T1 ∝ T term from q = 0 fluctuations above 4 K. Benzoate protons, with weak and mostly dipolar coupling, are another candidate. A temperature-dependent shift (Kn changes strongly below 0.8 K) can also move spectral weight into or out of the excitation bandwidth, making w2 change with temperature. Other non-RS origins are not excluded either: relaxation near dilute paramagnetic defects (chain ends, broken bonds, free Cu spins), spread by nuclear spin diffusion or limited by a spin-diffusion bottleneck, which also gives a distinct fraction whose weight grows as defect moments polarize; and freezing of water or benzoate-group motion below ~0.4 K, which the authors themselves raise as a source of disorder and which would give a temperature-dependent weight unrelated to the Cu spins. Supplementary Note 5 reportedly rules out spectral overlap via the frequency dependence of M(t), but the reader cannot see this test in the main text, and it does not address the form-factor scenario. The authors should: compute the q = 0 and q = π hyperfine form factors for every proton plausibly contributing to P5 from the tensors in Supplementary Note 2; show recoveries measured on different parts of the P5 line; repeat the two-component and ILTA analysis on the well-resolved H(1) line (P1/P2), whose large coupling they use for Kn; and report the ratio 1/T1s / 1/T1f across sites. Disorder-induced RS physics in the Cu chain should affect all Cu-coupled sites, scaled by their hyperfine couplings, whereas contamination by a second proton species would not. They should also state explicitly what distinguishes their data from an impurity-relaxation scenario (field dependence of 1/T1s, its dependence on hyperfine coupling across P1–P5, its absolute value), and show that the slow component is absent above 0.4 K for a physical reason, not merely because it is buried under the fast one.

4. **The link between the NMR observables and a random-singlet state is qualitative, and some features do not match what RS theory predicts.** The claim that "1/T1s ∝ T signals a type of gapless excitation ... a direct consequence of quenched disorder" is supported only by analogy to a thermal-conductivity result (ref. 49, quadratic T dependence); no prediction of the local 1/T1 in an RS-like state at T ≈ 0.02J and 0.2 T is given. In RS scenarios the local susceptibility and local relaxation rates have broad, continuous distributions, and earlier NMR on random Heisenberg chains (refs. 50, 58, e.g. Shiroka et al.) found stretched-exponential relaxation, not two discrete components two orders of magnitude apart. The bimodal P(1/T1) with a gap between the modes (Fig. 3c) is therefore not obviously what an RS state produces. The QMC (Fig. 3d inset) computes only a correlation length ξ, which is linked to w2 by a heuristic ("increasing effective correlation length"; the text calls it "phenomenological", p. 4); ξ is not w2. The spatial picture is also inconsistent: the QMC uses a dilute bimodal random-bond model (10% of bonds with ΔJ/J = 0.03, randomly placed), in which every ~10-site segment contains a modified bond, so there are no spatially separate clean and dirty regions. The Discussion instead invokes "domain-type" disorder (dirtier regions isolated by vast cleaner ones), and the reading of w2 as a volume fraction of RS-like regions next to "pure HAFC" regions (p. 4) makes sense only for that. The authors should compute, with the disorder model, quantities NMR actually measures, such as the distribution of local susceptibilities (bearing on Kn and the line broadening) or of local 1/T1 (via QMC with analytic continuation, strong-disorder RG, or the real-time tensor-network methods of ref. 52). They should show whether this produces a bimodal distribution, a linear-in-T slow mode and a −ln T weight, compare its magnitude and width with the measured 1/T1s and slow lobe in Fig. 3c, explain physically why a nucleus in an "RS-like region" relaxes ~100× slower and linearly in T while one in a "clean region" keeps the spinon rate, and use a disorder model consistent with the one invoked in the Discussion. Absent this, "random-singlet-like" should be presented as a hypothesis, and the title and abstract softened.
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

5. **The disorder scale is inconsistent with theory for weak disorder, and the QMC support lacks a clean-chain reference and controls.** The paper concludes that ΔJ/J ≈ 0.02–0.05 drives an RS-like crossover at T0 ≈ 0.4 K ≈ 0.02J (pp. 4, 6). For weak bond disorder in the S = 1/2 Heisenberg chain, disorder is marginally relevant and the crossover scale to the infinite-randomness regime is expected to be exponentially small in the inverse disorder strength (Doty–Fisher; Fisher 1994, ref. 16). For 3% disorder on only 10% of bonds it would naively lie far below 30 mK. The QMC inset of Fig. 3d (ξ/a ∝ −ln(T/T0) for T/J ≈ 0.003–0.1) is offered as support, but: the clean-chain ξ also grows on cooling (∝ 1/T with log corrections) and can look logarithmic over a limited window when plotted this way, yet ΔJ = 0 is not shown on the same axes; only 32 realisations of L = 256 chains with open boundaries are used, and at T/J ≈ 0.003 the clean-limit ξ is of order hundreds of sites, so finite-size saturation could mimic slower-than-power-law growth; and the 10% fraction and ΔJ = 0.03 appear chosen rather than derived or independently motivated. The authors should show the ΔJ = 0 result analysed identically, results for at least two L and several ΔJ and bond fractions, state how the parameters were fixed and how sensitive ξ(T) is to them, and discuss explicitly why a crossover at 0.02J is expected for such weak disorder, or else reconsider the identification.

## B. Should address

6. **The Knight-shift upturn at 0.2 T is fitted over a narrow window, and the lowest-temperature data are discarded.** The fit Kn = aT^−1 ln^−2(Ω/T) (Fig. 3e) spans less than a decade (roughly 0.2–0.8 K), has no constant offset, and the authors note it is "only applied within a narrow temperature window". The downturn below 0.2 K, exactly where the RS regime should dominate (w2 > 50%), is attributed to a signal-to-noise artefact from signal on the low-frequency tail beyond the spectrometer window (p. 5). Over this window a Curie term C/T + K0 from dilute free spins at chain breaks, a Curie–Weiss form, or a boundary-induced (Eggert–Affleck) chain-end contribution would likely fit equally well. The authors should fit these forms with an offset, report χ² or an information criterion with the number of free parameters, the stability of Ω and the implied impurity concentration, and show the excluded points. The artefact explanation should be tested: show full spectra below 0.2 K with the low-frequency tail and the spectrometer bandwidth, integrate the full line using frequency-stepped sweeps, measure at a second frequency or field, or show whether integrated intensity × T drops at the same temperature. Otherwise the points should be excluded from all conclusions consistently. The downturn could alternatively reflect the soliton gap at 0.2 T (concern 1) or imperfect thermalisation (concern 9).

7. **Only one crystal was measured by NMR, and the disorder is not characterised independently.** The RS-like interpretation rests on unintentional quenched disorder of speculative origin ("domain-type ... stress or unbalanced benzoate group rotation freezing", p. 5), and the NMR used a single crystal ("Three samples were used for the NMR, specific heat and susceptibility measurements, respectively", p. 7). If the slow channel is disorder-driven, w2(T), T0 and c should vary between crystals and growth batches; if intrinsic, they should not. The disorder strength ΔJ/J ≈ 0.02–0.05 and the 10% bimodal-bond model are inferred from the same NMR energy scales they are meant to explain. The authors should show the 0.2 T two-component recovery and w2(T) on at least one other crystal, preferably from a different batch or with deliberately different disorder (faster growth, light substitution, partial deuteration, ageing), include a check before and after thermal cycling, and provide an independent bound on the disorder: the low-temperature Curie tail or the claimed T^−1 ln^−2 enhancement in ac susceptibility or magnetisation in the dilution refrigerator (bulk susceptibility here stops at 1.8 K; if unavailable, say so), a Schottky or low-T anomaly in specific heat (Supplementary Note 3), ESR linewidths, or structural characterisation (X-ray rocking curves, mosaicity, diffuse scattering, water content/stoichiometry). A systematic change of T0 and c with disorder level would be the most convincing evidence.

8. **The robustness of the ILTA results and of w2 (Fig. 3c,d) needs to be shown.** The Tikhonov parameter α = 0.001 was chosen by inspection, "to provide stability without inducing excessive smoothing" (p. 7), and the text notes that α "directly influences the distribution's width". Extracted weights and peak positions also depend on the time window and on how M(∞) and the inversion factor are fixed. At 0.03 K the slow rate is ~0.1–1 s^−1 (Fig. 3c), so the recovery must extend to several T1s (tens of seconds), but Fig. 3a ends near 10–30 s. The authors should: show w2(T) for α spanning at least two decades; state the maximum recovery delay, the number of recovery points and the S/N at each temperature, and how the slow-component weight is constrained when its recovery is incomplete; show that w2 from ILTA (via double-Lorentzian fits) agrees with the amplitude a_s from direct double-exponential fits, reporting whether a_f and a_s were constrained and whether they share a common M(∞); compare with a stretched-exponential (or stretched-plus-single) fit, the standard description for random chains; state how the error bars in Fig. 3d were propagated; and, since the −ln(T/T0) form is fitted to roughly six points over one decade, show that a power law w2 ∝ T^−x or a smooth crossover fits measurably worse before giving the logarithm physical meaning.

9. **Thermalisation of the insulating crystal at 30 mK is not demonstrated.** The heating checks (randomised recovery delays, ±6 dB pulse power, a factor-of-4 change in repetition time) show that RF pulses do not heat the sample on the timescale of M(t). They do not show that the crystal, with only lattice phonon conduction and a thermal conductivity the authors say is strongly suppressed below 0.3 K (ref. 29), reaches the mixing-chamber temperature; the thermometer is on the holder. The authors should give the thermal anchoring geometry, the thermometer position and the waiting times after temperature changes, and show that a thermometric NMR quantity keeps evolving down to 30 mK, e.g. the equilibrium NMR intensity × T (nuclear Curie law), the line shift, or w2 at various waiting times. If the crystal saturates near 60–80 mK, the "w2 = 65% at 0.03 K" point and the −ln T fits change.

10. **The spinon evidence at 0.2 T is narrower than stated.** The abstract and p. 2 describe 1/T1f as "nearly constant between 1.4 and 0.03 K", while the text also says it "shows a weak downturn below 0.3 K" (p. 2) and "remains broadly temperature-independent before showing field-induced suppression below 0.2 K" (p. 3); Fig. 3b shows the downturn. These should be reconciled and the range supporting the spinon claim stated. The predicted logarithmic correction, 1/T1 ∝ ln^1/2(J/T) (refs. 35, 41), should raise 1/T1 by roughly 1.5× between 1.4 K and 0.03 K; it is not seen, and its absence is not explained, although it was observed in Sr2CuO3. Possible explanations should be examined quantitatively: cut-off by the field or staggered field (concern 1), masking by disorder below 0.4 K, or filtering of the q = π fluctuations by the P5 hyperfine form factor (give the form factor at q = 0 and q = π from Supplementary Note 2, or compare with 1/T1 at H(1)). The 1/T2G ∝ T^−1/2 fit spans only 0.4–2 K (Fig. 3b inset), so the exponent needs an error bar and the fit window should be stated; the introduction's claim of a "diverging 1/T2G ∝ T^−1/2" below 1.4 K and the abstract's "divergence-like" 1/T2G should be revised, since T2G is measurable only down to 0.4 K. Otherwise the claim of a textbook HAFC realisation should be softened.

11. **The absolute Knight shift at 0.2 T needs a field reference.** In the linear-response regime Kn should not depend on field, yet the 0.2 T "spinon" Kn at 1–4 K is ≈ −0.7 to −0.8% (Fig. 3e), while at 2 T and 5 T the same H(1) site gives ≈ −0.3% at 2–4 K (Figs. 2c, 4b). At 0.2 T (γH ≈ 8.5 MHz), a remanent or trapped field of a few mT in a superconducting magnet, or an error in the reference frequency, shifts Kn by about 1%; Kn = (f/γH − 1) × 100% (Methods) requires H to better than 10^−3. The authors should state how the field was calibrated at 0.2 T (e.g. a reference sample, or measurements at both field polarities) and reconcile the 0.2 T and 2 T values.

12. **The field misalignment is not quantified, although the staggered field depends strongly on it.** The resolved peak count is taken to show that the field is "slightly away from the crystallographic b-axis" (p. 2), but no angle is given. In copper benzoate the staggered field h = c(θ)H, and hence the soliton gap and the DM-induced canting, vary strongly with field direction. The 0.2 T analysis (concern 1), the exponent α = 0.8(1), and the comparison with specific-heat and INS gaps measured in specified orientations (Fig. 4e) all depend on this. The authors should estimate the misalignment angle from the site splittings and the hyperfine tensors of Supplementary Note 2 and propagate it into the staggered-field coefficient used in LTRG and in Fig. 4e.

13. **The extraction of the high-field gap is too heuristic.** (i) Kn = a e^{−Δ/kBT} + b has three free parameters, ignores the power-law prefactors expected for the thermally activated soliton susceptibility, and leaves b free although it is attributed to zero-temperature canting. The authors should state the fit range, constrain b with the LTRG canting value, and fit the LTRG magnetisation (Fig. 4c) with the same form to show the procedure returns the input gap. (ii) 1/T1 = a e^{−Δ/kBT} (Fig. 4d) is fitted at P5, whose assignment is uncertain, and assumes single-soliton activation; in sine-Gordon theory intra-branch soliton scattering, soliton–antisoliton creation or breather contributions give different activation energies (Δ versus 2Δ) and prefactors. The authors should state which process dominates and why the activation energy equals Δ, and use theory-motivated prefactors. (iii) α = 0.8(1) differs from 2/3; they should say whether the logarithmic correction Δ ∝ H^2/3 ln^1/6(J/H) or the field range accounts for this, and report how Δ changes with the fit window. (iv) Since the gap is already known from specific heat and INS, they should state what the NMR determination adds.
