A. Must address

1. The inverse Thiele model has no pinning restoring force, but the paper's own data show that pinning acts during the relaxation, so recoil from skyrmion–skyrmion scattering is not established. The Methods (p. 18) and p. 10 say that post-pulse motion of the L and R skyrmions is "governed solely by skyrmion–skyrmion interactions and dissipation" (Eq. 1). Yet p. 9 attributes the restoring forces to "skyrmion–skyrmion repulsion and the surrounding pinning landscape". Three features of the data point to pinning:
   (i) The "immobile" class I skyrmions move about 20–25 nm during the negative pulse and then recoil with large amplitude (Fig. 2c; Table 1: 17.5 and 20.1 nm, against 12.8 and 9.1 nm for class M; the Fig. 2 caption says I shows "the strongest recoil"). This is the signature of skyrmions bound elastically to pinning sites. It also means the I skyrmions cannot be fixed scattering centres or fixed boundary conditions.
   (ii) In Fig. 3a, R1 relaxes to different final x positions after +Jx and after −Jx (about 1.085 and 1.11 µm), so its rest position depends on pulse history.
   (iii) The predicted Thiele trajectories settle about 10–20 nm away from the measured ones in both x and y (Fig. 3a: −Jx branch predicted near 1.125 µm against data near 1.11 µm; y near 0.21 µm against data near 0.19 µm). This is a persistent force imbalance or a mis-set initial condition.
   The L and R skyrmions at the edge of the pinned region presumably sit in similar local wells. Fitting their relaxation with a pairwise repulsion alone folds any pinning force into a* and b*, which undermines the headline claim that the scattering potential was measured. Oersted-field or field-like-torque transients at the pulse edges should also be excluded. The authors should:
   - add a pinning term to the Thiele model (for example a harmonic or Gaussian well about each skyrmion's pre-pulse position, with fitted stiffness) and report how a*, b* and the loss change, with residuals of the final positions with and without it;
   - use the class I recoil, where the pairwise force should be small, to constrain the pinning stiffness independently, and test whether the I-class relaxation is consistent with the same V(r) given its own neighbours;
   - add a control without a scattering partner: measure the recoil of an isolated skyrmion or a low-density region under identical pulses. A scattering origin predicts much weaker recoil there; pinning or thermal origins do not.

2. Transient Joule heating and cooling are not estimated, and could produce both the low-drive recoil and the high-drive depinning. The device is a 25-repeat Pt/Ir-based stack (about 30 nm) on a 100 nm SiN membrane, driven with 5 ns pulses at 2.8 × 10^11 A/m² and 2.5 ns pulses at 1.2 × 10^12 A/m². Heating during the pulse changes Ms, anisotropy, DMI and interlayer coupling, and with them skyrmion size and equilibrium spacing. The membrane cools on nanosecond to tens-of-nanosecond scales, overlapping the 3–20 ns relaxation times. A recoil driven by changing equilibrium sizes and positions during cooling would also be deterministic, opposite to the drive and exponential, and would not probe the room-temperature interaction potential. At high drive, heating lowers pinning and could cause the depinning attributed to SOT (p. 13). Heating is also relevant to the linear v(J) and to the claim that results are "free from stochastic thermal activation" (p. 10); the same group reports enhanced thermal diffusion in similar SyAFMs (ref. 20). The only mitigation described, the 1 µs delay and 10 mbar He (Methods), addresses cumulative rather than transient heating. The authors should estimate the peak temperature rise during and after each pulse at both current densities, from device resistance measured during the pulse (transmitted-pulse amplitude) or from a thermal model of the stack on the membrane, and give the expected cooling time; report skyrmion diameter and contrast through the relaxation window and show that the size is the same before and after the pulse; and vary pulse length and amplitude at fixed J and fixed lattice configuration to show whether the recoil amplitude and τ_relax scale with pulse energy as a thermal process would.

3. Pump–probe averaging of stochastic events could mimic the exponential "recoil", and determinism is assumed, not checked. The analysis assumes each frame shows one deterministic trajectory repeated identically over billions of cycles (p. 4: "any non-reproducible skyrmion motion will average out"; p. 6: "highly reproducible across all cycles, confirming their non-stochastic origin"; p. 10: "free from stochastic thermal activation"). Stochastic behaviour does not vanish in a stroboscopic average. If a skyrmion depins, or relaxes from a metastable position, at a random time after the pulse in each cycle, the averaged contrast shifts its centroid smoothly and roughly exponentially while the texture blurs. That is what Fig. 2c,d and Table 1 show, and it is what the Thiele inversion is fitted to; a trajectory fitted by a deterministic model could be an average over several pathways. Repeatability of the average does not prove determinism of individual events, especially at room temperature in a SyAFM. The authors should quantify skyrmion contrast amplitude and apparent diameter or edge width frame by frame through the pulse and the relaxation window for the M, L, R and I classes, and compare them with static images and with skyrmions far from any motion; compare static images taken before and after the pump–probe run; report any skyrmions whose contrast fades during the cycle; compare runs with different accumulation times or beamtimes if available; and show that any blurring stays within what the 20 nm resolution and tracking noise allow. This is needed to support "dynamics that are reproducible over billions of cycles", "deterministic control ... even in the incoherent flow regime", and the reading of τ_relax and V(r) as single-particle deterministic quantities.

4. The rigid-particle Thiele model is applied to skyrmions that the authors say are deforming. On p. 9 the authors describe R1 as showing "a breakdown of the rigid-particle approximation, as asymmetric constraints induce spin structure deformation", yet R1 is the example used to show the agreement of the rigid-particle fit (Fig. 3a). Shape relaxation, such as breathing or elongation relaxing back, shifts the contrast centroid without any centre-of-mass interaction, and internal-mode relaxation in a SyAFM could fall within the 3–20 ns window. The authors should measure skyrmion shape (aspect ratio, area) against time during relaxation for the L and R skyrmions used in the inversion, and show that centroid motion is not dominated by shape recovery, or restrict the fit to skyrmions with negligible deformation.

5. The agreement between experiment and simulation is overstated, the reported numbers are inconsistent, and the simulation is not shown to be independent. The Methods (p. 18) give an experimental a* = (38 ± 5) nm and b* = (236 ± 9) nm; the simulation gives a* = (28 ± 4) nm and b* = (238 ± 9) nm (p. 10). The two a* values differ by about 1.6 combined standard deviations. The abstract nonetheless quotes "a range of 30 nm, in full agreement with micromagnetic simulations", and p. 15 says "excellent agreement"; the 30 nm is the simulated value, not the measured one. Fig. 3b shows the bands overlapping only near r ≈ 200–250 nm and diverging below about 180 nm. The paper also says only that the simulated system "closely match[es]" the experiment. It does not say which parameters (A, D_DMI, K_u, interlayer exchange, Ms per sublattice, α) were measured independently and which were tuned, or whether the simulated potential was extracted with the same inverse procedure. If D_DMI or the interlayer exchange were tuned to the observed lattice spacing or skyrmion size, the agreement in b* (about 236–238 nm, close to the lattice spacing) is largely built in. The authors should quote the experimental a* in the abstract, state whether the 68% intervals overlap and describe the agreement accordingly, list all micromagnetic parameters with their sources, and preferably fix them from measurements (SQUID, Brillouin light scattering or domain-spacing DMI, spin-flop field) before simulating.

6. The extracted parameters are not shown to be well constrained, and the exponential form and the length scale are assumed rather than tested. Eq. (1) imposes F/D = (1 m/s) exp(−(r − b)/a). In this form b only sets the prefactor, as exp(b/a), so a and b are strongly covariant. The separations sampled (Fig. 3b, lower panel) run from about 130 to 400 nm, with most weight near 200 and 350 nm, so a ≈ 30–40 nm is extracted over 4–10 decay lengths, and the steep part of V(r) below about 150 nm is an extrapolation. The paper does not say which trajectories and how many skyrmions and time points enter the loss, how long the "early-time relaxation window" is, what the goodness of fit or noise model behind the "68% confidence intervals" is, or whether the result is stable across skyrmions; only R1 is shown. The authors should show the full two-dimensional loss landscape L(a, b) with the a–b correlation and the dependence on the fit window; state the number of data points against the number of free parameters; show fits for skyrmions other than R1 with a leave-one-out or bootstrap estimate of the spread; compare losses for alternative forms (the modified-Bessel K1(r/λ) form expected for exchange-mediated overlap, an exponential plus a constant, a power law), since without this "reveals an exponential decay" is an assumption; restrict the plotted potential and confidence band to the sampled r interval; and validate the whole pipeline on simulated trajectories with a known potential, including pinning and deformation.

7. Magnetization compensation is stated, not demonstrated, and the stated parameters contradict it. The Methods (p. 16) give Ms = 0.85 × 10^6 A/m "for each ferromagnetic sublattice", while sublattice A is FCB(0.4)/CFB(0.2) = 0.6 nm and sublattice B is CB(1.1) (Fig. 1a). Taken literally, the moments differ by nearly a factor of two. The suppressed skyrmion Hall angle, the angular-momentum compensation argument (p. 15) and the neglect of the gyrotropic term in the inversion (p. 10) all rest on compensation, and angular-momentum compensation is asserted, not measured. The authors should report Ms·t of each sublattice and the net moment at remanence from the SQUID data (Fig. S1), correct the statement, and estimate the residual gyrotropic term G_eff/D, either including it in the inversion or showing it is negligible for the y-motion seen in Fig. 3a.

8. The Co L3 edge does not isolate sublattice B, and the timing between the Fe and Co movies is not described. Sublattice A (FeCoB/CoFeB) contains Co, so the Co-edge XMCD signal is the Co moment of B minus that of A; only the Fe edge isolates one sublattice. A relative displacement of the two sublattices would partly cancel in the Co channel rather than appear as a shift. This weakens the "sublattice-resolved" framing (p. 4), the claims that both sublattices "move in synchrony" with "robust sublattice locking" (pp. 6–7, Fig. S3), and the statement that "no delay or phase shift between the sublattices is detected" (p. 13). The Fe- and Co-edge movies were recorded sequentially (p. 6). The authors should give the Co content and Co moment fraction of each sublattice (thickness × concentration, XAS) or use a difference analysis, estimate the cross-contamination, state what sublattice separation would be detectable given it and the 20 nm resolution, and explain how the two datasets were registered in time and space (common t₀, jitter) and with what accuracy.

9. The "no inertia", "no sublattice delay" and "GHz operation" claims are not compared with what is resolvable or predicted. The velocities in Fig. 4d are finite differences at about 1 ns spacing during a 2.5 ns pulse sampled by a few frames (Fig. 4c,d), so an inertial lag or transient interlayer separation below about 0.5–1 ns or about 20 nm would not be seen. "Tracking the voltage" compares to the applied voltage rather than the current in the strip, which reflections or RC filtering can shift. "Defying theoretical predictions" (p. 13) requires the delay and separation that ref. 43 predicts for this stack's interlayer exchange; if these are sub-nanosecond or a few nanometres, there is no contradiction. "Robust GHz operation" (abstract, p. 15) is inferred from 2.5–5 ns pulses every 1 µs (duty cycle about 10^-3) at about 50 m/s. The authors should fit the displacement or v(t) against the drive with a damped-mass or first-order response model and quote an upper bound on the effective mass or inertial time; measure the transmitted current waveform; state the predicted delay and separation using the 650 mT spin-flop and 1.8 T saturation fields (p. 16); and either show repetition at higher rates or present GHz capability as a projection.
