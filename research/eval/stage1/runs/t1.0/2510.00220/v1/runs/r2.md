Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors report that, in marginally twisted double-bilayer graphene that has relaxed into mixed Bernal (ABAB) and rhombohedral (ABCA) tetralayer domains, gate voltages drive a hysteretic, bistable switching between a "Bernal-dominant" (B-dominant) state and a "rhombohedral–Bernal mixed" (RB-mixed) state. They map the switching boundary in the (n, D) plane, compare it with a tight-binding energy difference, and show that at fixed gate voltages the RB-mixed state shows a hysteretic anomalous Hall effect (AHE) while the B-dominant state does not. They also find that the sign of the AHE reverses with the sign of D, which they attribute to rhombohedral/Bernal domain boundaries. If the structural assignment holds up, nonvolatile electrostatic switching between a magnetic and a non-magnetic ground state through sliding is a new and interesting result for the slidetronics and rhombohedral-graphene communities. In its current form, however, the structural assignment and the magnetic claims rest on indirect transport evidence, and much of the supporting material (Extended Data and Supplementary Information) is cited but not included in the submission.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- A clear, reproducible bistable resistance switching driven only by gates (Fig. 1c,d), with a well-defined protocol for mapping the switching boundary in the full (n, D) plane (Fig. 2).
- A phase diagram (Fig. 3a) with several resolvable jump contours that are mirror symmetric about D = 0, plus a calculation (Fig. 3b) that captures the qualitative trends: hole doping and large |D| near neutrality both favour rhombohedral stacking.
- A direct comparison at identical gate voltages showing hysteretic AHE in one history-prepared state and only an ordinary Hall response in the other (Fig. 4c,d and 4g,h). This is an attractive demonstration of history-dependent control of a magnetic state.
- The D-odd AHE is an unexpected observation that, if confirmed, points to new physics at stacking domain boundaries.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The structural phase transition is inferred, not observed, in the measured device. The central claim, a "sliding-induced first-order structural phase transition between Bernal and rhombohedral tetralayer graphene", rests on resistance jumps (Fig. 1c, Fig. 2) and on the presence or absence of quantum oscillations (Extended Data Fig. 3, not included). The real-space evidence comes from other samples. Per the Methods, Fig. 1b was taken on a separate twisted double-bilayer/hBN/graphite stack on SiO2, and Extended Data Fig. 2a on a stack still on the stamp. No structural probe of device A itself is shown, in either state. The main text calls Fig. 1b "a typical real-space image of the domain configuration", which a reader will take to mean the device. Because the switching is said to be domain-wall motion within a fixed mixed-domain landscape, the claim needs evidence that the rhombohedral area fraction in the device actually changes between the two states. Several kinds of evidence would do this, roughly in order of strength:
   - (i) Spatially resolved Raman (2D-band line shape) or KPFM/near-field imaging of a device after setting it into each state. Devices with a thin top hBN and no top graphite, or with a graphite gate, imaged at room temperature after a low-temperature switching cycle, would still be informative.
   - (ii) Landau-fan or quantum-oscillation data in both states at the same (Vt, Vb), showing the rhombohedral-specific sequence (or the Bernal band-overlap sequence) appearing or disappearing.
   - (iii) A quantitative estimate of how much rhombohedral area must change to produce the observed ΔRxx.

   Without one of these, the data are equally consistent with purely electronic bistability in a fixed domain landscape, for example hysteresis between competing correlated states, which is well documented in rhombohedral multilayers.

2. Gate-voltage history artifacts are not ruled out. The switching is triggered by excursions to the highest hole densities, at or near the end of the gate range: in Fig. 1c the backward-sweep jump sits at n = −15.5 × 10^12 cm^-2, essentially the end of the scan. Large gate excursions are a classic cause of charge trapping or detrapping in hBN, at graphite/hBN interfaces, or at contacts. That shifts n0 and D0 and produces hysteretic, history-dependent transport and Hall signals that look nonvolatile. The paper calibrates n0, D0, Ct and Cb only "from the Landau levels in the B-dominant state" (Methods), so it is not shown that the two states compared at "exactly the same gate voltages" (Fig. 4c vs 4d, 4g vs 4h) have the same carrier density and displacement field. The authors should:
   - (a) extract the Hall density and the charge-neutrality-point position in both states at the same gate voltages, and show they agree;
   - (b) show that the threshold at −15.5 × 10^12 cm^-2 is an intrinsic switching density, not the scan limit, for example by sweeping further or showing the threshold's dependence on D, sweep rate and dwell time;
   - (c) report gate leakage currents at the extreme voltages;
   - (d) explain why trapped charge would not produce the mirror-symmetric multi-contour structure in Fig. 3a. If the contours track constant ΔF, as claimed, that argument should be made explicitly as a discriminator.

3. The evidence that the RB-mixed state is a ferromagnet and the B-dominant state a paramagnet needs strengthening.
   - (a) The AHE is reported from a single Hall pair. Rxx on the other side of the Hall bar differs in magnitude and even sign (Extended Data Fig. 7), which the authors attribute to current redistribution across a domain patchwork. In such a sample, a hysteretic Ryx can arise from a hysteretic longitudinal (magneto)resistance of a switching domain mixed in through inhomogeneous current paths. Antisymmetrization does not remove this for hysteretic data unless it is done by pairing the forward sweep at +B with the backward sweep at −B. The authors should state exactly how the antisymmetrization was applied to the hysteretic loops, show raw Ryx and Rxx loops together, and show the AHE loop from the second Hall pair. Ideally they should also check that the loop scales as expected with current direction or contact permutation (Onsager reciprocity).
   - (b) The field range (±0.15 T) only just exceeds the apparent coercive field (~0.1 T, Fig. 4c,g). Wider-field loops would separate the anomalous contribution from the ordinary Hall slope and show that the high-field branches merge.
   - (c) The maps in Fig. 4a,b,e,f were taken at a single field, B = 0.03 T, below the coercive field. Their sign therefore depends on the prior field and magnetization history. The field-training protocol for each map should be stated; otherwise the sign contrast between Figs. 4a and 4e cannot be read off the maps.
   - (d) The "paramagnet" label for the B-dominant state rests on the absence of AHE at one temperature (1.5 K) and a few gate points. It would be more accurate to say "no detectable AHE/hysteresis down to 1.5 K", or else to add supporting evidence such as temperature-dependent Hall slopes.

B. Should address

4. The D-odd AHE and its domain-boundary interpretation (abstract and p. 7) need stronger tests and alternatives. Four points:
   - (a) Comparable points. The positive-D and negative-D loops are taken at non-mirrored points: the diamond is at about (n, D/ε0) ≈ (1.3, +1.0) and the star at about (1.4, −1.13). The maps in Fig. 4e show both AHE signs within the negative-D region. Because the AHE sign evidently varies with (n, D), the inversion-symmetry argument needs loops taken at exactly mirrored (n, ±D) points, and preferably several such pairs.
   - (b) Preparation protocol. The RB-mixed state at positive and at negative D is prepared by different gate paths (Fig. 2a vs 2e; Fig. 4 caption: "going along the edge of the gate boundary... for each line"). This can leave different domain configurations, so the two signs may belong to two different microscopic states rather than to one state under D → −D. The authors should show that the D-odd sign persists when both are reached from the same prepared state, for example by sweeping D continuously through zero at fixed n within the bistable window.
   - (c) An unconsidered alternative. Tetralayer graphene also has the ABCB (equivalently ABAC) polytype, which lacks inversion symmetry and is spontaneously polar; ref. [37], cited for the work function, discusses exactly this. Sliding in a cut-and-stack double bilayer, especially the nearly-180° device A, could create ABCB regions. Such regions would give a D-odd response without invoking domain walls. Domain identification here rests on KPFM contrast alone. The authors should explain why ABCB domains are excluded (Raman, KPFM sign and magnitude, or stacking geometry).
   - (d) The domain-wall picture itself. No symmetry analysis shows that an ABCA/ABAB boundary (a partial dislocation at the 2–3 interface) actually breaks inversion in the relevant sense. Nor is there an estimate of how quasi-1D boundary regions could produce a Hall signal larger than that of pure rhombohedral tetralayer (ref. [23]). Both should be supplied, or the claim stated more tentatively.

   The device-B reproduction (Extended Data Figs. 10–11) is not available to evaluate. Its key data, a mirrored-point loop pair, should appear in the main text.

5. The theory–experiment agreement is qualitative, and the comparison is not quantified. Fig. 3b overlays the experimental contours on a calculated ΔU map. "Agree well" is asserted, but nothing shows that ΔU is in fact constant along each experimental contour. The model has two free parameters:
   - ΔΦ, taken from KPFM (22 mV) measured at room temperature, in ambient conditions, on a different sample, partly while still on the stamp;
   - ΔU(0,0), whose determination is deferred to the Supplementary Information, which is not included.

   The authors should plot the calculated ΔU along each experimental contour, with its spread, and state which parameters were fitted. They should also show the sensitivity to ΔΦ, to the tight-binding parameter set and to screening or self-consistent layer potentials. The calculation is non-interacting, yet the region of interest at large |D| and small n hosts spin-valley-polarized correlated phases. The paper should discuss how interaction energies (of order meV per carrier) would modify ΔU, since they could shift the contours near the black boxes of Fig. 3a. Finally, n and D in the RB-mixed state are computed from capacitances calibrated in the B-dominant state; the possible error from different quantum capacitance or layer polarization should be estimated.

6. Reproducibility and sample statistics are thin. All data except Extended Data Figs. 10–11 come from one device (A). The text notes that the hysteresis "appears only in part of the device" (Extended Data Fig. 4). The paper should state:
   - how many devices or regions were measured and how many showed switching;
   - whether device B (0.03° rather than ~180° construction) shows the full bistable switching and phase diagram, or only the AHE sign reversal;
   - whether the switching boundary and AHE survive thermal cycling to room temperature.

   Since the domain landscape is set by fabrication and relaxation, device-to-device variation of the switching densities is central to the "on-demand toggling" claim.

7. The temperature, rate and retention dependence of the switching is missing. A first-order transition governed by domain-wall pinning (pp. 3, 5) predicts a temperature-dependent hysteresis width and thresholds, and possibly sweep-rate and dwell-time dependence. All switching data are at 1.5 K. The authors should show:
   - the switching thresholds versus temperature, at least up to and above the ~8 K Curie temperature, and ideally to tens of kelvin, to establish that the structure and not the magnetism drives the bistability;
   - the retention of each state over hours at the AHE operating point;
   - the dependence on sweep rate.

   This is also needed to support the "nonvolatile memory" framing. Fig. 1d shows about seven cycles over 300 s.

8. The nonlinear Hall response at the pentagon (Fig. 4i) is read as a "weak but finite AHE" without hysteresis. In a mixed-domain sample with Bernal and rhombohedral regions of different carrier types and mobilities, multi-carrier ordinary Hall transport naturally produces a nonlinear Ryx(B) over ±0.15 T. The authors should exclude this with a two-carrier fit over a wider field range, or with the temperature dependence they mention in Extended Data Fig. 8 (not included). That figure should appear in the main text or a provided supplement if the "two-component AHE" summary (p. 7) is retained.

9. The priority claim on p. 7 ("first realization of a magnet-to-non-magnet nonvolatile phase transition controllable entirely by electrostatic means") should be qualified or removed. Gate-hysteretic switching between correlated magnetic and non-magnetic states, including AHE states, has been reported in graphene moiré systems and in graphene/hBN ferroelectric-like hysteretic devices. The distinction claimed here is that the switching is structural, which returns to Concern 1.

10. The derivation of the magnetic susceptibility difference from the field dependence of the switching boundary (p. 3) appears only in the main text, with all data in Extended Data Fig. 5 and the Supplementary Information, which are not included. Either the result (the value, its sign and its comparison with the expected orbital susceptibilities of ABAB and ABCA tetralayers) should be summarized in the main text, or the statement should be removed. It cannot be assessed as submitted.

C. Minor

11. Missing material. The submission refers repeatedly to Extended Data Figs. 1–11 and a Supplementary Information file that are not provided: the quantum-oscillation evidence, the work-function measurement, the device-B data, the Curie temperature, the cycling endurance and the calculation details. Several key steps of the argument therefore cannot be checked. Please include them.

12. Fig. 1b. The caption and main text should state explicitly that this image is from a different heterostructure than device A (as the Methods say). They should also state whether that heterostructure has the same ~180° construction as device A.

13. Terminology. "First-order quantum phase transition" (p. 2) is unusual for a hysteretic structural transition driven at finite temperature through domain-wall depinning. "Gate-driven first-order structural transition" would be more accurate.

14. Methods, definitions of n and D. As written, D = (−CtVt + CbVb)/2 − D0 has units of charge per area, while the figures plot D/ε0 in V/nm. Please make the definition consistent, give the values of Ct, Cb, n0 and D0, and state the hBN thicknesses.

15. The Curie temperature "around 8 K" is quoted without the criterion used (onset of hysteresis, or extrapolated remanent Ryx) or an uncertainty.

16. Fig. 2d/2h and Fig. 3a. Please state how the "black dashed boundary" was drawn (by eye, or by a threshold on ΔRxx/R̄xx), and how the blue equipotential contours from the other contact configuration were extracted.

17. Typographical errors: "The Bernal regions is larger" (Fig. 1 caption); "Free energy" and "Helmholtz Free energy" (pp. 3, 5); "L.M.." (Contributions); "Keithly" should be "Keithley" (Methods).

4. Suggestions for improvement

- Add a direct structural measurement on the transport device (or a sister device switched in the cryostat) in both states, such as Raman 2D-band mapping or KPFM after warming, so that the "sliding transition" is observed rather than inferred.
- Measure the AHE loops and Hall densities at precisely mirrored (n, ±D) points, reached from a single prepared RB-mixed state, using both Hall pairs. This would settle whether the D-odd AHE is intrinsic and rule out gate-trapping and contact-mixing artifacts in one set of measurements.
- Present the theory comparison quantitatively, as ΔU evaluated along each experimental contour, and discuss the role of interactions in the correlated regions where the AHE appears.
- Provide the temperature dependence of the switching thresholds and the retention of each state, to separate structural bistability from electronic or magnetic hysteresis and to substantiate the memory application.
