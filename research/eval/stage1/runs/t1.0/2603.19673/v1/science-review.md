A. Must address

1. The torque switching angle θc is not shown to measure canting, and the model is never compared with the full torque curves. θc is defined operationally as half the separation of two extrema of d(τ/H)/dθH near θH = 90° (p. 4; Fig. 2b–d), and each τ(θH) curve is reduced to this one number, which is then read throughout as the canting angle of the equilibrium magnetization (Fig. 3d plots it in degrees). Two problems follow. First, the signature is not unique to a canted state. The statement on p. 3 that "in a strictly collinear ferrimagnet with a single uniaxial anisotropy, these extrema would remain pinned to the crystallographic axes" is not generally true at finite field: for a single-domain uniaxial (Stoner–Wohlfarth-type) magnet below the anisotropy field, M lags H, τ(θH) becomes sawtooth-like (as Fig. 1b itself shows at 0.8 T), and the extrema of dτ/dθH sit at field-dependent angles away from the principal axes. Shape anisotropy, multi-domain reorientation or a small misalignment of the rotation plane could do the same. Second, at high field (9 T, against |K1/M| ≈ 2.4 T) M nearly follows H and dτ/dθH → −d²F_anis/dθ² at θ ≈ θH, so the positions of its extrema are set only by the shape of the anisotropy function (the ratios K1:K2:K3), not by any tilt of the magnetization. That "switching markers persist up to 9 T" is therefore the expected behaviour of any anisotropy whose second derivative has extrema off θ = 90°, and does not show that canting "survives" or is "sustained" at high field, as the abstract states. The authors should compute τ(θH) and d(τ/H)/dθH directly from the free energy at the measured fields and temperatures (with the measured M(T)) for (i) K1 only, (ii) K1–K2 and (iii) K1–K2–K3, overlay them on the traces of Figs. 1b, 1c and 2b–d, show where the extrema fall in each case, and state which θM tilt (if any) θc corresponds to at each field. Ideally the full τ(θH) or d(τ/H)/dθH curves should be fitted jointly with the ESR dispersions, with goodness of fit reported with and without K3. Calibrating the cantilever (plus sample mass) to give absolute torque would independently constrain K1 and M and test consistency across samples.

The claimed agreement with the non-monotonic temperature trend is also weak: the 9 T data dip at 10 K and peak at 30 K at 16–19°, while the simulation peaks near 30 K at roughly 20° on a different scale, so the statement on p. 6 that the model "supports the non-monotonic temperature dependence" is not demonstrated. 3. "Canted ferrimagnet" (sublattice non-collinearity) is not distinguished from a tilted collinear macrospin (easy-cone state). The free energy on p. 4 describes a single macrospin. With K1/M = −2.40 T and K2/M = 1.40 T its zero-field minimum lies at sin²θM = −K1/(2K2) ≈ 0.86 (θM ≈ 68°), an easy-cone state with the net moment about 22° out of the plane, which is the 22° ESR-derived θc at 5 K in Fig. 3d. That is a statement about the orientation of the net moment. It says nothing about non-collinearity between the Mn1 and Mn2 sublattices, which is what "canted ferrimagnetism" usually means and what the neutron study (Ref. 21) addresses; a ferrimagnet with canted sublattices and a collinear ferrimagnet with an easy-cone anisotropy give the same macrospin F. The title, abstract and conclusions slide between the two meanings and cite Ref. 21 as support ("consistent with a non-collinear canted state reported by neutron scattering", p. 3). The authors should state explicitly which canting is meant and either describe the state as an effective easy-cone / tilted-magnetization state, or show that the easy-cone angle agrees quantitatively with the neutron-determined moment direction and its temperature dependence, or adopt a two-sublattice model (intersublattice exchange, sublattice-specific anisotropy) that produces sublattice canting, relate its parameters to K1–K3, and show that the data require sublattice non-collinearity.

4. The ESR evidence rests on an H//c fit that returns an unphysical g-factor. The concave-down H//c dispersion (Fig. 3c) is presented as the key ESR evidence for canting (pp. 4–5), but the global fit gives g_a = 2.01 and g_c = 0.68. For Mn²⁺/Mn-based moments with quenched orbital moment g ≈ 2 is expected, and Ref. 25 reports a value close to that. A factor-of-three error means the single-mode macrospin model does not describe the H//c mode; g_c is not a harmless "effective parameter" (p. 5). Because H//c is the only orientation far from the plane, the remaining constraints on K1/M and K2/M come only from the 90° and 80° geometries, which undermines the claim that ESR "pins down K1/M and K2/M reliably", and K1/M and K2/M are taken from this same fit. The ESR window (≤ 1.5 T) is also well below the c-axis instability field implied by the paper's own K1 (curvature at θM = 0 requires H > −2K1/M ≈ 4.8 T), so for H//c the sample is in a reoriented, probably multi-domain state in which domain modes, demagnetization and the ferrimagnetic exchange branch matter; the concave-down shape need not imply canting. Whether the observed branch is the quasi-ferromagnetic mode, and whether an exchange mode falls in the 1–60 GHz window or mixes with it, is not discussed. The authors should refit with g fixed near 2 for all orientations and report how K1, K2 (and K3) and the residuals change; model the H//c data with a two-sublattice ferrimagnetic resonance treatment including demagnetization for the measured sample shape and misalignment, or show that a collinear easy-plane model in the multi-domain regime cannot produce the concave-down branch at g ≈ 2. If g_c cannot be brought near 2, they should state the limitation and not use the H//c branch as support for canting.

5. The fitted ESR dispersions are inconsistent with the stated free energy at zero field. F contains no azimuthal (φ) dependence. In the Smit–Beljers formula (Ref. 24), ω = (γ/M sinθ)[FθθFφφ − Fθφ²]^½, and for a uniaxial F at zero field with the moment off the c axis Fφφ = 0, so the uniform mode should be gapless. The fitted dashed lines in Fig. 3a–c nevertheless reach finite frequencies at H = 0 (about 10 GHz for H//ab, about 5 GHz for H//c). The zero-field frequency must also be independent of field orientation, since the state at H = 0 is the same, yet the fitted curves differ between panels. This is impossible with the stated F unless in-plane anisotropy, a demagnetizing term or an exchange (two-sublattice) mode is included. The zero-field gap is itself a strong constraint on the cone state and should be compared explicitly with the model's prediction. The authors should write in the main text the resonance condition actually used, with every term in F and the γ used, show residuals and the K1–K2 covariance, and include the terms needed to reproduce the gap (or adopt a two-sublattice or φ-dependent model).

6. The K1–K2–K3 parameters imply a large zero-field tilt that is not tested against magnetization or neutron data. Minimising F = K1 sin²θ + K2 sin⁴θ + K3 sin⁶θ with the Fig. 3e values gives sin²θM ≈ 0.56 at 5 K (K1/M ≈ −2.4 T, K2/M ≈ 1.4 T, K3/M ≈ 0.9 T), i.e. a moment about 42° out of the ab plane, and about 46° at 40 K (K1/M ≈ −1.4, K2/M ≈ 0.7, K3/M ≈ 1.1 T); at 20–40 K K1 + K2 + K3 ≥ 0, so the in-plane direction is no lower in energy than H//c. Adding K3 thus roughly doubles the K1–K2 tilt of about 22°, yet K1 and K2 are held fixed at their K3 = 0 ESR values (p. 5). Such a state should show a large c-axis component in M(H), a low-field c-axis susceptibility comparable to the in-plane one, and a cone angle that the neutron study [21] would have detected; Mn3Si2Te6 is usually described as easy-plane with a hard c axis, and Fig. 1d shows a much larger in-plane response. SQUID data were taken along both directions (Methods), but only dχ/dT is shown. M(H) for H//c and H//ab is the most direct, model-independent check of the anisotropy constants: it gives the hard-axis saturation field (predicted ≈ 4.8 T at 5 K from −2K1/M, which also bears on Fig. 4b,c, where the 5 T data reach only cos θM ≈ 0.6 at θH = 0) and its temperature dependence, and M(T) is needed to convert K/M into energies. The authors should show M(H) at 5, 20 and 40 K along both axes with the model's predictions, state the zero-field θM implied at each temperature and compare it quantitatively with the canting angle in [21], and refit the ESR with K3 included at the torque-derived value so that the parameter set is self-consistent rather than built in stages.

8. "Coherent" (single-domain) behaviour is assumed, not demonstrated. An easy-cone state in a trigonal crystal is degenerate under ±θM (cos θM → −cos θM) and in azimuth, so domains are expected at low field. This matters most for Sample #1 at 0.8 T (Fig. 1b,c), where the "purely torque-based indication" of canting is extracted; domain averaging, domain-wall pinning and hysteresis can all shift extrema in d(τ/H)/dθH. The hysteresis below 0.5 T (p. 4, Fig. S1), the "weak hysteretic effect" in θM(θH) (pp. 6–7), the jump at θH = 180° in Fig. 4b and the abrupt torque changes near the hard axis in Fig. 1b all suggest domain or switching processes. The authors should define "coherent" operationally and support it, for example by showing forward- and reverse-rotation data in the main text at low and high field, showing that extrema positions are independent of sweep direction and field history, showing reversibility above some field or that the ESR line is a single uniform mode, or comparing the torque amplitude with the expected single-domain value; otherwise drop the term.

9. K1, K2 and K3 at 20 and 40 K are weakly constrained, which undercuts the temperature trend. ESR at 20 and 40 K was measured only for H//ab (symbols in Fig. 3a), with no tilted or H//c data, so K1 and K2 are strongly covariant: K1/M at 40 K has error bars of roughly ±1.2 T, K2/M at 40 K is consistent with zero (Fig. 3e), and the ESR θc at 40 K is 4.7° with an error bar of about ±6° (Fig. 3d). The 20 and 40 K H//ab points in Fig. 3a also nearly overlap the 5 K dispersion, while K1/M is claimed to change from −2.4 to −1.4 T. K3/M is then fitted separately at only three temperatures (5, 20, 40 K) to a few θc(H) values per temperature, with K1 and K2 held fixed from a different sample (ESR on Sample #3, torque on Sample #2). The claim that K1–K2 "collapses too rapidly with temperature" and the crossing of K2 and K3 depend on these points. The authors should measure ESR at θH = 80° and 0° at 20 and 40 K, or propagate the full covariance into K3 and the θc prediction; report how error bars on K1–K3 were obtained, the covariance matrix and number of data points at each temperature; explain how K1 can change by ~40% while the H//ab resonance hardly moves; and test whether K3 > 0 and its improvement survive when K1 and K2 vary within their uncertainties.

10. Torque-specific artifacts and the θc extraction are not quantified. The analysis relies on angular positions of derivative extrema accurate to a degree or so, but the paper does not report: the zero-angle calibration (how θH = 90° is located), angular and field resolution, and misalignment of the rotation plane relative to c for the mechanical rotators (Samples #2, #4) and the vector magnet (Sample #1); the cantilever deflection, and hence angle error, at 9 T for these strongly anisotropic crystals; piezoresistor nonlinearity near the hard axis, where Fig. 1b shows abrupt torque jumps; the empty-cantilever background and piezoresistor magnetoresistance; or how extrema are located in the numerically differentiated signal (smoothing, peak fitting) and with what uncertainty (Fig. 3d has no error bars on torque θc). Out-of-plane misalignment in particular changes the torque near the hard axis. The model also holds M fixed, although the ferrimagnet is not saturated and M depends on H and θH; a paramagnetic or field-induced component, and the T- and H-dependent M entering K/M, could also produce extrema persisting near the plane at 9 T (the "field-induced contribution" of Fig. 3d is alluded to but not developed). The authors should give these numbers, describe calibration, background subtraction and deflection corrections, add error bars on θc, include M(H, θH) from SQUID in the model, and show that θc is insensitive to these effects, for example by comparing θc for Samples #2 and #4 (different cantilever orientation or sample size) at matched T and H in the main text.

11. The anisotropy expansion ignores trigonal symmetry and the in-plane azimuth. Mn3Si2Te6 is trigonal (P-31c). Besides uniaxial sin²ⁿθ terms, symmetry allows in-plane terms such as sin⁶θ cos 6φ and sin³θ cosθ cos 3φ. The latter is antisymmetric about the plane for a given φ and can shift torque extrema away from θH = 90° and 270°, which is the very signature used for canting (Fig. 1c); a cos 6φ term could mimic or contribute to the "K3" stiffness and affects the H//ab zero-field ESR gap (~10 GHz, Fig. 3a). Calling K3 sin⁶θ "the simplest symmetry-allowed extension" is therefore incomplete. The rotation plane is described only as "an in-plane direction", and Fig. 1a labels the axes [100], [001] and [110] with [100] apparently along c, which conflicts with "θH measured from the c-axis". The authors should specify the in-plane azimuth ([100] versus [110]) of the rotation plane for each sample, check whether θc depends on azimuth (one additional rotation plane, or an in-plane torque rotation, would suffice), estimate the in-plane anisotropy, and justify neglecting the trigonal and φ-dependent K2/K3 terms.

12. Demagnetization (shape anisotropy) is not treated. For a plate-like crystal, shape anisotropy adds an effective uniaxial term of order μ0Ms that enters K1 directly and is not negligible relative to anisotropy fields of a few tesla; it also affects the ESR resonance conditions, particularly for H//c. The authors should state whether K1 includes demagnetization, give the dimensions of the ESR and torque crystals, and estimate the correction.
