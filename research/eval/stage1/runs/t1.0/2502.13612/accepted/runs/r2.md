Review outline:

**1. Significance and novelty**

The authors measure in-plane resistivity and high-field (up to 33–35 T) magnetoresistance on two combinatorial La2−xCexCuO4 films covering 0.116 ≤ x ≤ 0.188 in fine steps. They model the data with a Shockley–Chambers Boltzmann treatment that has an anisotropic T-linear scattering channel, hotspots and (for the Hall effect) current vertex corrections. From this they extract a single doping-dependent amplitude g(x) of the anisotropic T-linear scattering rate. It falls about tenfold as Tc goes from 25 K to 0, and the authors argue that commensurate low-energy (π, π) spin fluctuations are the pairing glue in electron-doped cuprates. If it held, a quantitative link in a cuprate between normal-state inelastic scattering and Tc, with the scatterer identified, would be an important result. The fine doping resolution and the high-field MR dataset are strong on their own. However, the step from "a model parameter tracks Tc" to "spin fluctuations mediate pairing" depends on several modelling choices and on a spin-versus-other discrimination that is still only semiquantitative.

**2. Potential reasons for acceptance**

- The dataset is unusually dense and uniform: 13 + 7 channels on one substrate, with matched conditions and fields up to 33 T over 2–190 K. Extrinsic sample-to-sample variation, which plagues doping series, is largely removed.
- The experimental observation is clear and model-independent: for x > x_FSR the MR falls with decreasing T below 50–75 K even though ρ also falls, a strong violation of Kohler's rule (Fig. 2a–g, Extended Data Fig. 7). There is also no H/T quadrature scaling, in contrast with p-type cuprates (Fig. 2h–l).
- The modelling is unusually transparent about what fails. Extended Data Fig. 3 shows alternative forms of Γ(φ) that do not reproduce the data, and Extended Data Fig. 6 shows how different anisotropies affect R_H.
- One fitted form at x = 0.159 reproduces ρ(H,T), Δρ/ρ(0) and dρ/dH over 20–50 K, including absolute magnitudes (Fig. 4).
- The comparison with hole-doped cuprates is thought-provoking.

**3. Concerns**

**A. Must address**

1. **A correlation between g(x) and Tc does not by itself establish that the scatterer is the pairing glue, and the paper gives no quantitative link.**
   - *Where:* main text after Fig. 5 ("This clear correlation between Tc and the strength of the anisotropic T-linear scattering rate is the second key result… we conclude that SC pairing in electron-doped cuprates is mediated by the exchange of low-energy spin fluctuations"); title and abstract.
   - *The problem:* Over 0.116 ≤ x ≤ 0.188 almost every quantity changes monotonically with x: carrier density, the AFM correlation length, the proximity to Fermi-surface reconstruction, the hotspot positions and t′/t. Any of these will correlate with Tc on the overdoped side of a dome. The paper's analogy with λ_ph, λ_tr and the McMillan formula (introduction; Supplementary Fig. 2) is not followed through.
   - *What is missing:*
     - g(x) is never converted into a dimensionless coupling λ.
     - No estimate of Tc from such a λ is attempted, for example with a spin-fluctuation Eliashberg or McMillan-type expression.
     - Tc → 0 at x ≈ 0.175–0.188 while g stays finite at about 0.55 (Extended Data Table 3; Fig. 5c). On the authors' own data, superconductivity therefore disappears at finite coupling. That needs a mechanism, such as a threshold, pair breaking or competition with disorder, and the paper does not discuss it.
   - *Rough estimate:* with α1 = 0.75 ps⁻¹ K⁻¹ (Extended Data Table 2), the peak value of λ ≈ ħα1g/(2πk_B) is of order 1 at x = 0.159 and about 6 at x = 0.116. These numbers deserve discussion.
   - *What to do:* (i) Give λ(x), both peak and FS-averaged, from g(x)α1. (ii) Discuss why Tc vanishes at finite g. (iii) Show at least an order-of-magnitude Tc estimate. (iv) Otherwise, reword the title and conclusions from "evidence for spin-fluctuation-mediated superconductivity" to "a correlation between Tc and the strength of anisotropic inelastic scattering at the AFM hotspots".

2. **The extraction of g(x), and above all the "order-of-magnitude" drop, rests on untested assumptions, and most of the drop comes from dopings where the model's Fermi surface does not apply.**
   - *Where:* Fig. 5, Extended Data Table 3, Methods "Simulations".
   - *(a) Wrong Fermi surface below x_FSR.* The order-of-magnitude change (6.5 → 0.55) comes mainly from S1 channels at x < x_FSR ≈ 0.14, where g goes from 6.5 to about 2. There the authors acknowledge AFM order and FS reconstruction: a negative MR component, mixed-state curves in Fig. 2h–j, and T* above 40 K in Fig. 1. Yet the simulations use the unreconstructed large FS and tight-binding parameters for every x. The statement that at 40 K "signatures of AFM order are absent or weak across the doping range" (Fig. 5 caption) conflicts with Fig. 1 and with Extended Data Fig. 7, where γ1 for x = 0.116–0.131 is already strongly suppressed below about 40 K.
   - *(b) Everything else fixed.* For S1, g(x) comes from fitting just two numbers per channel, ρ(0, 40 K) and Δρ/ρ(0) at 20 T, with τ_imp⁻¹, α2 and the hotspot height and width held at their x = 0.159 values. A residual scattering rate that stays the same across a combinatorial film with a composition gradient is a strong assumption.
   - *(c) Two methods spliced together.* For S2, g(x) is instead taken from the T-linear coefficient A1 of ρ(0,T) and normalised to S1 at x = 0.159. Yet the two films' absolute resistivities at x = 0.159 differ by a factor of 1.5 (Methods; Extended Data Fig. 1a). This points to a sizeable geometric-factor uncertainty, and that uncertainty feeds straight into a fit to absolute ρ.
   - *What to do:*
     - Show full ρ(H,T) fits of the Fig. 4 type at no fewer than two other dopings, one near x = 0.12 and one near x = 0.18, with only g varying, to show that the one-parameter description really holds across the series.
     - Give uncertainties on g(x) that include the geometric-factor uncertainty and the effect of letting τ_imp vary.
     - State explicitly how much of the g–Tc correlation survives when the analysis is restricted to x > x_FSR. In that range g drops only about 3.5-fold, from 2.1 to 0.55, while Tc goes from roughly 15 K to 0.

3. **The identification of the scatterer as (π, π) spin fluctuations rests on a semiquantitative Hall analysis that is not compared with Hall data from these films and does not exclude a nodal-peaked phonon-type anisotropy.**
   - *Where:* main text after Fig. 4; Methods "Incorporation of CVCs"; Extended Data Figs. 4–6.
   - *What the paper concedes:* the MR alone cannot tell spin from charge scattering (Fig. 4b,c), so the case rests entirely on the sign and trend of R_H.
   - *(a) The Hall calculation is limited.*
     - The CVC model uses a Jones–Zener low-field expansion, whereas the R_H data were taken at 14 T (Extended Data Fig. 4).
     - It replaces ε with a fixed Gaussian (σ = 0.07, prefactor 1/(1 − ε²) ignored).
     - It is evaluated only at single parameter sets (Extended Data Fig. 6), not as R_H(T,x) curves to compare with the data.
     - The Hall data themselves come from other films (ref. 27, and the combinatorial films of ref. 10), not from S1 and S2.
   - *(b) The decisive ingredient need not be spin fluctuations.* By the authors' own account (Extended Data Fig. 6d and the text around it), what lowers R_H is a broad anisotropic component peaked toward the nodes (Γ_N) together with the CVCs. Nothing in the analysis shows that a T-linear Γ_N must come from spin fluctuations rather than, say, anisotropic electron–phonon scattering above about Θ_D/5. The Methods themselves cite the −45 meV antinodal kink and the possibility of phonon coupling. The charge-fluctuation alternative is rejected using the antinodal form Γ_AN plus charge hotspots (Extended Data Fig. 6e). A nodal-peaked non-magnetic component combined with the AFM-hotspot CVCs is not tested.
   - *What to do:*
     - Compute R_H(T) for representative x with the same Γ(φ,T,x) used for the MR, at the field of the measurement, and compare it quantitatively with Hall data. Ideally measure Hall on S1 and S2 themselves.
     - Show that a nodal-peaked, non-magnetic T-linear component cannot reproduce MR and R_H together.
     - Alternatively, describe the spin-fluctuation assignment as the most natural interpretation rather than as established.

**B. Should address**

4. **Where the anisotropic scattering peaks is described inconsistently between the MR fit and the Hall argument.**
   - *The conflict:*
     - The main text and Fig. 4b(i) describe the T-dependent component as peaked at the hotspots on the AFM zone boundary, with β(φ) = sin¹²[2(φ ± (π/4 − HS))].
     - Methods describe it as "eight broad peaks, each located at the intersection of the underlying FS and the AFM BZ boundary".
     - The Hall argument requires Γ_N, "peaked along the zone diagonals" (Extended Data Fig. 6d caption), and the Methods summary says "the inclusion of an associated Γ_N with a magnitude that leads to a doubling of the MR (Fig. 4) also causes R_H to halve".
   - *Why it matters:* A reader cannot tell whether the same Γ(φ) explains both MR and R_H, and the paper's central argument for spin fluctuations depends on exactly that.
   - *What to do:* State the angular function used in each calculation explicitly, with HS in defined units. Plot the MR-fit Γ(φ) and the Γ_N of Extended Data Fig. 6d on the same axes.

5. **Other explanations for the fall in MR at low T are not ruled out at the dopings and temperatures that matter.**
   - *The claim:* Anisotropy in the inelastic channel is inferred from the MR shrinking as T falls (main text points 1–2).
   - *Alternatives that could also reduce the MR:*
     - A negative, isotropic spin-scattering MR, which the authors invoke below x_FSR and which could persist in weaker form above it.
     - Superconducting fluctuations near Tc. Δρ/ρ curves are fitted down to 20 K, while Tc reaches about 25 K at x = 0.116 and is still finite at x = 0.159.
   - *The evidence offered:* The orbital assignment is supported by longitudinal versus transverse MR only at 75 K and only for x ≥ 0.159 (Extended Data Fig. 2). The Methods themselves note that a longitudinal MR appears at lower T.
   - *What to do:* Show longitudinal MR at 20–40 K and at x < 0.15, and subtract it or bound it in the analysis. Estimate the paraconductivity contribution, for example from the field scale at which dρ/dH becomes linear, at the lowest temperatures used in Fig. 4 and Fig. 5.

6. **Doping calibration, inhomogeneity within a channel, and Tc per channel are not documented.**
   - *What is missing:*
     - The nominal Ce content per channel, its uncertainty (Δx ≲ 0.003 is stated without justification) and the oxygen reduction state across a combinatorial film are not described; the paper refers to ref. 10. In electron-doped cuprates the effective doping depends strongly on oxygen content and annealing.
     - The Tc values on the top axis of Fig. 5c are not shown as data. The resistive transitions and the Tc criterion for each channel are needed to judge the correlation.
     - Within one microbridge the composition gradient may itself produce H-linear MR (Methods, last section). The authors argue against this using non-combinatorial films (ref. 24), but give no estimate of the gradient across a single channel.
   - *What to do:* Provide ρ(T) through Tc for all channels, the Tc criterion, the x calibration with uncertainties, and the composition spread per channel.

7. **The fits are not unique, and parameter correlations are not quantified.**
   - *The problem:* The authors say the parameterisations of τ_HS⁻¹(φ) and β(φ) "are somewhat arbitrary" (the sin¹² exponent, HS = 0.48, a hotspot height of 50 ps⁻¹ and width of 0.05). The hotspots are taken as T-independent without physical justification. The Methods even invoke a √T hotspot dependence as one possibility.
   - *What to do:*
     - Show how g(x) and its trend change under reasonable changes to the β exponent, the hotspot width and height, and a T-dependent hotspot term.
     - Give a goodness-of-fit measure for Fig. 4 and Fig. 5a,b.
     - In Fig. 5b the simulated Δρ/ρ(0) falls systematically below the data at x ≈ 0.12–0.14; comment on this.

8. **The CVC parameters are held fixed in x and T, although the physical quantity they stand for, ξ_AFM, changes strongly across the series.**
   - *Where:* Methods eq. (12).
   - *The problem:* ε(φ) is taken with fixed breadth and height for every x, yet ε ≈ (1 − cξ²_AFM⁻²) and ξ_AFM changes strongly across this doping range and with T.
   - *What to do:* Explain this choice. Estimate how a realistic ξ_AFM(x,T) would change the predicted R_H trends, especially the claimed agreement with the drop in R_H(T) and the sign change.

9. **The conclusions about hole-doped cuprates go beyond the evidence.**
   - *Where:* abstract and final section ("implies that the higher Tc values in the latter cannot be simply due to an enhanced coupling to spin fluctuations").
   - *The problem:* This rests on the absence of a successful Boltzmann parameterisation for p-type MR, which is an argument from failure to model, not a measurement.
   - *What to do:* Rephrase as a suggestion, or support it with a direct comparison, for example the inferred λ at the hotspots in LCCO against what the same analysis yields for a p-type compound.

**C. Minor**

10. **"Threefold" does not match Fig. 5a.** The text says "Figure 5a,b show the threefold drop in ρ(0, 40 K) and the twofold drop in Δρ/ρ(0)", but Fig. 5a shows ρ(0, 40 K) falling from about 44 to about 24 μΩ cm, roughly 1.8-fold. Please correct this, or explain which quantity is threefold.

11. **The size of the drop in g(x) is stated three ways.** The Extended Data Table 3 caption says g(x) "is found to drop by roughly a factor of 2 across this doping range" (for S2), the main text says "order-of-magnitude drop", and the abstract says "diminishes markedly". Make clear which range each statement refers to.

12. **Citation errors.** The Extended Data Table 1 title cites "Tang et al.⁵⁷", but ref. 57 is Chambers. The tight-binding source is cited as ref. 26 in the Methods, and Tang et al. also appear as ref. 58. Please check these numbers.

13. **Possible scale inconsistency in Extended Data Fig. 3e.** The caption gives a stronger hotspot of "10³ ps⁻¹", but panel e1 is cut off at 250 ps⁻¹. State the hotspot height and width used there.

14. **Plot g against Tc directly.** Fig. 5c places Tc on a nonlinear top axis. A direct plot of g (or λ) against Tc would make the central correlation easier to judge, and so would A1(x) from both films on the same plot.

15. **Extended Data Fig. 2 legend.** The legend "H||i||ab" should be defined, presumably as field parallel to the current.

16. **Unify the notation.** τ⁻¹, Γ, g(x)α1 and α1 are used in overlapping ways between the main text and the Methods.

**4. Suggestions for improvement**

1. Add a figure or table that converts g(x)α1 into a dimensionless λ(x), both at the hotspots and averaged over the FS, plotted against Tc and compared with the Planckian value. Then confront it with even a simple spin-fluctuation Tc estimate.
2. Measure the Hall effect, and longitudinal MR at low T, on the same combinatorial films. Simulate R_H(T,x) with the same Γ(φ,T,x) used for the MR, so that the spin-versus-other discrimination becomes quantitative.
3. Repeat the full Fig. 4 analysis at one strongly underdoped-side composition and one heavily overdoped composition, and give uncertainty bands on g(x) from a sensitivity analysis over the hotspot and β parameters.
4. Separate clearly the conclusions that hold for x > x_FSR, where the large-FS model applies, from those that depend on channels with AFM order, and adjust the strength of the title and abstract to match.
