**A. Must address**

1. **A correlation between g(x) and Tc does not establish that the scatterer is the pairing glue, and no quantitative link is given.** The main text after Fig. 5 ("This clear correlation between Tc and the strength of the anisotropic T-linear scattering rate is the second key result… we conclude that SC pairing in electron-doped cuprates is mediated by the exchange of low-energy spin fluctuations"), the title and the abstract rest on g(x) and Tc both falling across 0.116 ≤ x ≤ 0.188. Over this range almost every quantity changes monotonically with x: carrier density, the AFM correlation length, proximity to Fermi-surface reconstruction (x_FSR), hotspot positions, t′/t, ρ0 and A1 (Fig. 1). Any of these will correlate with Tc on the overdoped side of a dome. The data also contradict a simple proportionality: in Extended Data Table 3, g stays finite (0.66 at x = 0.174, about 0.55 at x = 0.184–0.188) where Tc is already zero on the Fig. 5c top axis, which places Tc → 0 near x ≈ 0.175 at g ≈ 0.6; between x = 0.141 and 0.188, g falls only about 3.4-fold. The abstract and introduction frame the result through the McMillan/λ_tr analogy (Supplementary Fig. 2), but no λ is ever extracted. Using 1/τ = 2πλk_BT/ħ with α1 = 0.75 ps⁻¹ K⁻¹ (Extended Data Table 2), λ ≈ ħα1g/(2πk_B) is about 0.9–1 at the hotspot maximum for x = 0.159, about 6 at x = 0.116 and about 0.5 where Tc = 0; the angle average is smaller. Moreover, the near-equality λ_ph ≈ λ_tr in Supplementary Fig. 2 is an s-wave phonon result: for d-wave pairing the relevant coupling is the d-channel projection of the interaction, which weights (π,π) scattering differently from λ_tr, and Tc in spin-fluctuation theories also depends on the spin-fluctuation energy scale, not only the coupling. The authors should (i) give λ_tr(x), both peak and Fermi-surface averaged (from α1·g(x)·⟨β(φ)⟩), and discuss whether these magnitudes are plausible for Tc ≈ 8–25 K; (ii) give at least an order-of-magnitude Tc estimate from a weak-coupling, spin-fluctuation Eliashberg or McMillan-type expression using the extracted kernel and literature spin-fluctuation energies; (iii) explain why Tc vanishes at finite g (a threshold set by μ*, pair breaking by disorder in the films, a falling spin-fluctuation energy scale, competition with disorder); (iv) rule out that g and Tc merely co-vary with another quantity that falls with x, and show the correlation is specific to the anisotropic channel, for example by showing that the isotropic T² coefficient α2, held fixed here, would not correlate equally well if freed. Failing this, the title and conclusions should be reworded from "evidence for spin-fluctuation-mediated superconductivity" to "a correlation between Tc and the strength of anisotropic inelastic scattering at the AFM hotspots".

2. **g(x) largely restates the zero-field resistivity, because every other parameter is fixed and S2 values come from A1; the extra constraint from the MR is not demonstrated.** For S1, g(x) is the only free parameter (Fig. 5a,b; Extended Data Table 3; Methods "Simulations"). It is set per channel by matching just two numbers, ρ(0, 40 K) and Δρ/ρ(0) at 20 T and 40 K, with τ_imp⁻¹ (Γ0), α2, the hotspot height and width, the β(φ) exponent and the hotspot position all held at their x = 0.159 values (Extended Data Table 2). ρ(0, 40 K) falls from about 44 to 24 μΩ cm across S1; with Γ0 fixed, most of that drop, and any change in residual resistivity from oxygen content or disorder across a composition-gradient film, must be absorbed into g(x). A constant residual scattering rate across a combinatorial film is a strong assumption. For S2, g(x) is not obtained from the MR at all: it is the normalized T-linear coefficient A1 of ρ(0,T) (Extended Data Fig. 1c; Extended Data Table 3 caption), normalized to S1 at x = 0.159, although the two films' absolute resistivities differ there by a factor of 1.5. The g(x) curve in Fig. 5c is thus stitched from two methods, and its high-x flattening comes from resistivity alone. The headline quantity is therefore essentially A1(x), whose correlation with Tc is already established (refs. 8, 10; Fig. 1 inset). The paper's claimed advance, that the MR isolates the anisotropic inelastic part, is not shown. The authors should: fit the full ρ(H,T) or Δρ/ρ(H,T) data, as in Fig. 4, for further channels, at least one low-x (≈ 0.116–0.125, and ideally 0.131, 0.146) and one high-x (≈ 0.18) channel, to show that the one-parameter description holds across the series; fit with Γ0 (or ρ0), and preferably the hotspot strength, free per channel alongside g(x); show that the MR by itself, without absolute ρ(0, 40 K), fixes g(x) and gives the same trend (absolute resistivity in combinatorial microbridges is the least reliable input; see concern 9); model the S2 MR curves (Fig. 2k,l) in the same way rather than substituting A1; and give uncertainties on g(x) in Fig. 5c and Extended Data Table 3, including the geometric-factor uncertainty and the effect of letting τ_imp vary, preferably from the fit covariance.

3. **Most of the order-of-magnitude drop in g(x) comes from channels below x_FSR, where the model's unreconstructed Fermi surface does not apply.** In Extended Data Table 3, g falls from 6.5 to about 2.1 between x = 0.116 and 0.138 and to 1.46 at x = 0.146; above x_FSR ≈ 0.14 it falls only from about 2.1 (or 1.46) to 0.55, a factor of roughly 2.7–3.5, while Tc goes from about 15 K to 0. The S2 caption itself says "a factor of 2". For x < x_FSR the authors' own data show departures from the model's assumptions: a negative MR component attributed to spin scattering (ref. 21), mixed-state curves in Fig. 2h–j, a Hall coefficient that changes sign at low T (Extended Data Fig. 4b, x = 0.117), a negative high-field slope below 20 K and γ1 already strongly suppressed below about 40 K for x = 0.116–0.131 (Extended Data Fig. 7), the MR maximum at 50–75 K (Fig. 2), and the reconstruction shown in Fig. 1, where T* near x ≈ 0.12 appears to lie above 40 K. Yet g(x) at these dopings is obtained from a single temperature and field (40 K, 20 T) with the unreconstructed large Fermi surface and tight-binding parameters of Fig. 3 and Extended Data Table 1, and with no isotropic negative MR term. The statements that 40 K is "above T_FSR" and that at 40 K "signatures of AFM order are absent or weak across the doping range" (Fig. 5 caption) are asserted, not shown, and conflict with Fig. 1 and Extended Data Fig. 7. The authors should state T_FSR (or T*) for each channel and show that 40 K is above it; show that the 40 K data at x < 0.14 are free of the negative MR component (for example from the low-field curvature or the longitudinal MR at 40 K); justify the unreconstructed Fermi surface there; and state explicitly how much of the g–Tc correlation survives when the analysis is restricted to x > x_FSR. If the quantitative claim holds only above x_FSR, the "order of magnitude" statement in the abstract, main text and Fig. 5 should be revised to the smaller dynamic range, and conclusions that hold for x > x_FSR separated from those that depend on channels with AFM order.

4. **The identification of the scatterer as (π,π) spin fluctuations rests on a semiquantitative Hall analysis that is not tied to these samples and does not exclude non-magnetic alternatives.** The authors concede that the MR alone cannot separate spin hotspots from antinodal charge scattering (Fig. 4b,c), so the discrimination rests entirely on the sign and trend of R_H from the Jones–Zener plus current-vertex-correction (CVC) calculation (main text after Fig. 4; Methods "Incorporation of CVCs" and "Anisotropic scattering term"; Extended Data Figs. 4–6). That calculation has several weaknesses. R_H is given as single numbers (8.9, 5.6, 10.3, 4.3 and 11.8 × 10⁻¹⁰ m³ C⁻¹) for fixed Γ(φ) shapes, not as R_H(T, x); the main text says the calculation "captures the key features of R_H(T,x) beyond x > x_FSR, most notably the reduction in R_H with increasing T", but no computed R_H(T) or R_H(x) curve is compared with data. The CVC strength ε(φ) is an ad hoc Gaussian (σ = 0.07, fixed height, eq. 12) whose prefactor 1/(1 − ε²) is dropped and whose amplitude and T dependence are not tied to the spin-fluctuation parameters used for the MR. The low-field Jones–Zener expansion is used although the R_H data were taken at 14 T. The Hall data come from other films (non-combinatorial films of ref. 27 and combinatorial films of ref. 10), not from S1 or S2, and the two data sets disagree near x ≈ 0.15–0.16 by a factor of about 1.6–1.7 (about 8–9 × 10⁻¹⁰ in the non-combinatorial films versus about 13–15 × 10⁻¹⁰ m³ C⁻¹ in the combinatorial ones; the computed isotropic 8.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ agrees "excellently" only with the former), a discrepancy as large as the differences between the scenarios being distinguished. Further, by the authors' own account (Extended Data Fig. 6d), what lowers R_H is a broad nodal-peaked component Γ_N together with the CVCs. Nothing shows that a T-linear Γ_N must come from spin fluctuations rather than, for example, anisotropic electron–phonon scattering above about Θ_D/5 (the Methods cite the −45 meV antinodal kink and possible phonon coupling). The charge alternative is rejected only in the form Γ_AN plus charge hotspots (Extended Data Fig. 6e), without CVCs tied to the (δ,0) charge-ordering wavevector or an anisotropy peaked where the curvature changes, so it is not clear it received a comparably favourable treatment. The authors should compute R_H(T) for at least two or three dopings above x_FSR with exactly the Γ(φ, T, x) used for the MR, including g(x) and its T dependence, at the measurement field, and compare quantitatively with Hall data, ideally measured on the S1/S2 channels or films from the same growth; show how sensitive the sign and trend of R_H are to σ, the CVC amplitude, the omitted prefactor and the hotspot width; explain the factor ~1.7 discrepancy between the Hall data sets; show that a nodal-peaked non-magnetic T-linear component combined with the CVCs cannot reproduce MR and R_H together; and give the charge scenario an equivalent best effort. Otherwise the spin-fluctuation assignment should be described as the most natural interpretation rather than as established.

**B. Should address**

5. **The model assumes, rather than tests, that the whole T-linear term is anisotropic.** Eq. (1) multiplies both g(x)α1T and α2T² by the same β(φ) "for simplicity", so there is no isotropic T-linear component; the choice is motivated by A1(x) falling with x, but it builds in the outcome that the T-linear coefficient is entirely anisotropic and tracks A1. The claim of "anisotropic T-linear scattering" therefore follows from the parameterization. The authors should fit isotropic T-linear plus anisotropic T² (and the reverse), and also an isotropic T-linear term added to the anisotropic one, at x = 0.159 and at least one other doping, and show what the MR data constrain about the ratio of the two as a function of x. This is what would make the MR-based claim go beyond the existing A1–Tc correlation.
Raised by: r1, r3

6. **The fits are not unique, and the sensitivity of g(x) to the fixed hotspot and β(φ) choices is not shown.** The authors call the parameterizations of τ_HS⁻¹(φ) and β(φ) "somewhat arbitrary": the sin¹² exponent, HS = 0.48, a hotspot height of 50 ps⁻¹ and width 0.05 (rad), all fixed across x (Extended Data Table 2), with Fig. 4 shown only for x = 0.159 over 20–50 K. The hotspots are taken as T-independent without physical justification, although the Methods mention a √T dependence as one possibility, and the spin correlation length, and hence hotspot strength, should change with doping, which would feed into g(x). In Fig. 5b the simulated Δρ/ρ(0) falls systematically below the data at x ≈ 0.12–0.14. The authors should report how the β exponent and HS were chosen; show how g(x) and its trend, and the fit quality, change under reasonable variations of the exponent, hotspot width, height and position that still fit x = 0.159, with the hotspot term held fixed and also allowed to vary with x, and with a T-dependent hotspot term; give a goodness-of-fit measure for Fig. 4 and Fig. 5a,b; and comment on the Fig. 5b mismatch at x ≈ 0.12–0.14.
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

7. **The angular form of the anisotropic scattering is described inconsistently between the MR fit and the Hall argument.** The main text and Fig. 4b(i) put the T-dependent component at the hotspots on the AFM zone boundary, with β(φ) = sin¹²[2(φ ± (π/4 − HS))]. The Methods describe "eight broad peaks, each located at the intersection of the underlying FS and the AFM BZ boundary", and say the anisotropy "most likely" broadens toward the nodes. The Hall argument instead uses Γ_N, "peaked along the zone diagonals" (Extended Data Fig. 6d caption), described as an additional component, and the Methods summary says "the inclusion of an associated Γ_N with a magnitude that leads to a doubling of the MR (Fig. 4) also causes R_H to halve". A reader cannot tell whether one Γ(φ) explains both MR and R_H, and the central argument for spin fluctuations depends on exactly that. The authors should state the angular function used in each calculation explicitly, with HS in defined units, plot the MR-fit Γ(φ) and the Γ_N of Extended Data Fig. 6d on the same axes, and reconcile them into one form.

8. **Non-orbital and superconducting-fluctuation contributions at the fitting temperatures are not quantified, so other explanations for the low-T fall of the MR are not excluded.** Anisotropy in the inelastic channel is inferred from the MR shrinking as T falls (main text points 1–2), but the longitudinal-MR control (Extended Data Fig. 2) is at 75 K only, for three channels with x ≥ 0.159, and is already slightly negative; the Methods state that "at lower temperatures a non-zero MR appears that we attribute to the emergence of SC fluctuations". g(x) is fixed at 40 K and Δρ/ρ is fitted down to 20 K, while Tc is 20–25 K for x = 0.116–0.13 and still finite at x = 0.159, and an isotropic negative spin-scattering MR is invoked below x_FSR and could persist in weaker form above it. The authors should show H || I MR at 20–40 K across x, including x < 0.15; estimate the paraconductivity contribution and its field suppression at 40 K and at the lowest temperatures used in Figs. 4 and 5 (e.g. 30 K and 20 K), for example from the field scale at which dρ/dH becomes linear; and either subtract these contributions or show they are negligible compared with the orbital signal used to fix g(x).
Raised by: r1, r2

9. **The absolute resistivity calibration (thickness and geometric factor) of the combinatorial films is uncertain, and this enters g(x) directly.** The S2 resistivity had to be divided by 1.5 to match S1 at the shared composition x = 0.159 below 70 K (Methods "Zero-field resistivity"; Extended Data Fig. 1a), and the combinatorial-film R_H values are about 1.6 times those of non-combinatorial films (Extended Data Fig. 4). Both point to a geometric-factor or thickness uncertainty of that size. Because absolute ρ(0, 40 K) is one of the two targets that set g(x) for S1 (Fig. 5a; concern 2), channel-to-channel thickness variation along the gradient maps straight into g(x). The authors should give the film thickness profile and its uncertainty across S1 and S2, state how geometric factors were determined, and show how a channel-to-channel geometric error of, say, 10–20% would propagate into g(x).
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

10. **Doping calibration and the Tc of each channel are not documented.** The x of each channel comes from its position on a composition-spread film, and the paper does not show how it was verified (for example c-axis lattice parameter or EDX) or whether the quoted Δx ≲ 0.003 is a step size or an accuracy; the paper refers to ref. 10. In electron-doped cuprates the effective doping depends strongly on oxygen content and annealing, yet oxygen/annealing uniformity across the film is not described. The Tc values on the Fig. 5c top axis are not shown as data and are not obviously derived from these channels; zero-field ρ(T) through Tc is shown only for S2 (Extended Data Fig. 1b), and S1's zero-field ρ(T) is known only at discrete temperatures, so A1 for the S1 channels (where most of the g variation lies) is not measured directly and the comparison with A1 in Fig. 1 relies on ref. 10. The authors should provide ρ(T) through Tc for all channels with the Tc criterion, tabulate measured Tc alongside g(x), and give the x calibration with uncertainties and a check of oxygen uniformity.

11. **The quantitative result rests on one film per doping range, analysed differently, with no cross-check between films or against conventional films.** S1 provides all MR-based g(x) values and S2 only A1-based values. At the single shared composition (x = 0.159) the resistivities differ in scale, and no MR comparison between the films is shown. The authors should overlay the S1 and S2 MR at x = 0.159 at the same T; if possible, show the anomalous T dependence and the g(x) trend in a second film covering 0.12–0.16; and, since ref. 21 found similar MR in conventional films, confirm g and MR(H) at one or two dopings in an ordinary single-composition film.
Raised by: r1, r3

12. **A composition gradient within each channel could produce part of the H-linear MR, biasing the fixed hotspot parameters and hence g.** The Methods (last section) note that compositional variation along a microbridge could produce H-linear MR through Hall-voltage inhomogeneity, and respond only that H-linear MR also appears in non-combinatorial films (ref. 24). The hotspot term τ_HS⁻¹, introduced specifically to keep the MR H-linear, has no intrinsic T dependence and no microscopic justification, so any extrinsic H-linear component would be absorbed into it and into g. The authors should estimate the composition spread Δx along a single channel and the expected size of the inhomogeneity-induced linear MR, and compare it with the measured slope; a comparison of MR(H) with a single-composition film at one or two compositions would also address this.
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

13. **The CVC parameters are held fixed in x and T, although the quantity they stand for, ξ_AFM, changes strongly across the series.** In Methods eq. (12), ε(φ) has fixed breadth and height for every x, yet ε ≈ (1 − cξ_AFM⁻²) and ξ_AFM changes strongly with doping and temperature in this range. The authors should explain this choice and estimate how a realistic ξ_AFM(x, T) would change the predicted R_H trends, especially the claimed agreement with the drop in R_H(T) and its sign change.
Raised by: r2

14. **The conclusion about hole-doped cuprates goes beyond the evidence.** The abstract and final section state that the higher Tc of p-type cuprates "cannot be attributed solely to an increase in the coupling strength to the AFM fluctuations". This rests on the absence of a successful Boltzmann parameterization of hole-doped MR, which is a failure to model, not a measurement or any analysis carried out here. The final paragraphs themselves invoke larger U/t, a different Fermi-surface geometry and a dual coherent/incoherent response, any of which could break the analogy, and Tc in spin-fluctuation theories depends on the characteristic spin-fluctuation energy as well as the coupling, so even within LCCO g(x) alone does not fix Tc. The authors should present this as a hypothesis in an explicitly speculative outlook, discuss the role of the fluctuation energy scale (refs. 46, 61, 62), or support it with a direct test, for example applying the same model to Tl2201 or Bi2201 MR data and showing quantitatively where it fails, or comparing the inferred hotspot λ in LCCO with what the same analysis yields for a p-type compound.
