**A. Must address**

1. **The g(x)–Tc correlation largely restates the known A1–Tc correlation, and the MR's independent contribution has not been shown.** The paper calls g(x) "the first key result" (p. 7) and "an effective coupling parameter for both transport and superconductivity" (Fig. 5 caption). In the fits, though, g(x) is the only parameter allowed to vary with x. It scales only the T-linear term (Eq. 1, Table 3), and it is assigned to that term by construction (p. 10: "This is the main reason why the coupling strength g(x) was assigned uniquely to the T-linear term"). All other parameters stay at their x = 0.159 values (Table 2). For S1, g(x) is set by matching ρ(0, 40 K) and Δρ/ρ(0) at 20 T with one free parameter, and in practice mainly by ρ(0, 40 K) (Fig. 5a,b; Table 3). For S2, g(x) is simply the measured T-linear coefficient A1 normalised to S1 at x = 0.159 (Fig. 5c open symbols, Fig. 6e, Table 4). So g(x) is essentially a renormalised A1, and the A1–Tc scaling in LCCO was already reported by Yuan et al. (Ref. 5, Fig. 1 inset). The MR fixes the *form* of the anisotropy at one doping, but it does not independently fix the *trend* in strength. Fig. 10 shows other dopings only at 40 K, with every parameter except g fixed. The authors should:
   - determine g(x) for S1 separately from (i) the MR alone, with ρ0 free, and (ii) A1 alone, and compare the two;
   - show that the MR at 20, 30 and 50 K, and its full field dependence, are reproduced at each x, not only the 40 K, 20 T magnitude;
   - state explicitly, and quantitatively, what is new relative to Ref. 5;
   - plot g(x) against measured Tc for every channel of both films, with Ref. 5's A1–Tc data overlaid on the same normalised axes.

2. **The spin-fluctuation origin of the anisotropic T-linear term is an input to the model, not an output of it.** The hotspot positions at the AFM zone-boundary crossings are imposed (p. 5, Eq. 1, HS = 27.5°). The envelope exponent ν = 12 is borrowed from a hole-doped (Tl2201) parameterisation (Ref. 19, p. 6) and described as "somewhat arbitrary". The hotspot height and width are fixed (Table 2). The alternatives in Fig. 9 are single, apparently unoptimised trial forms at one doping (x = 0.159) and one temperature window (20–50 K), judged "inferior" by eye with no goodness-of-fit measure. The paper does not exclude other T-dependent anisotropies that peak away from the antinodes. Nodal-region scattering is dismissed on p. 15 only because no wavevector connects the nodes, and anisotropic electron–phonon scattering is not considered. The authors also note (p. 23) that Boltzmann transport "provides no direct information" on spin versus other bosons. To claim that the data *identify* (π,π) scattering, the authors should:
   - treat the peak position of the anisotropic term, and ν, as free parameters, and report the best-fit position with an uncertainty for several x, including whether it tracks the FS–AFM-zone-boundary crossing as that moves with x;
   - give χ² or residuals for Fig. 4 and for each Fig. 9 alternative, with the alternatives' own parameters optimised, at several dopings and temperatures;
   - test a T-linear anisotropic term peaked somewhere unrelated to (π,π), such as near-nodal scattering, anisotropic electron–phonon scattering, or an isotropic T-linear rate combined with a T-dependent hotspot width.

   Otherwise the supported conclusion is that "an anisotropic T-linear rate somewhere between node and antinode" fits the data, not that AFM hotspots do.

3. **Going from a correlation to "the primary pairing glue" needs a quantitative coupling estimate and a quantitative Tc relation.** The abstract and conclusions (pp. 1, 8–9) say the correlation between α, λsf and Tc "identifies low-energy spin-fluctuations as the primary pairing glue". But λsf is never defined or extracted: g(x) is dimensionless and normalised to 1 at x = 0.159. No McMillan-, Eliashberg- or spin-fluctuation-exchange-type estimate shows that the inferred coupling gives a Tc of the right size or the right d-wave symmetry. The paper's own motivating analogy (Allen's λtr ≈ λph, Fig. 7) rests on comparing absolute values. Several further problems arise:
   - In Fig. 5c and Table 4, g(x) saturates at about 0.55 at x = 0.184 and 0.188, where Tc reaches zero. The coupling therefore does not vanish with Tc, and the correlation is monotonic, not proportional. No threshold (μ*-like) term is discussed.
   - In the authors' own picture the hotspots lie much nearer the nodes than the antinodes (p. 21: "the node is three times closer to the AFM boundary"). For a d_{x²−y²} gap, (π,π) scattering between near-nodal points couples regions where the gap is small. Whether this channel can drive the observed Tc is a non-trivial question the paper does not address.
   - A correlation cannot exclude a common driver, such as proximity to the AFM/FSR critical point controlling both the scattering and Tc through the density of states or another channel.

   The authors should:
   - convert α1 g(x) into a Fermi-surface-averaged (and hotspot) dimensionless coupling, λtr(x) = ħ⟨dΓ/dT⟩/(2πkB), with uncertainties;
   - estimate the d-wave-projected pairing coupling from the same Γ(φ), and compare the implied Tc(x) with experiment, including the residual g at Tc = 0;
   - plot Tc against g or λsf directly, fit it to a form comparable with weak-coupling spin-fluctuation expectations (Refs. 13, 55, 56), and place it on an Allen-style plot alongside Fig. 7.

   Otherwise the title, abstract and conclusions should be reworded to a correlation "consistent with" spin-fluctuation pairing.

4. **The Hall analysis, on which the (π,π) assignment depends, is not shown at the level claimed.** On p. 8 the text says the model captures "the key features of R_H(T,x) beyond x > x_FSR, including the tendency of R_H to go negative with increasing T", and the Hall result is offered as "the second key finding" (p. 7). Fig. 13, however, gives only four single R_H values, for four forms of Γ(φ), at x ≈ 0.16 and an unstated temperature. No R_H(T) curve is computed, no x dependence is shown, no sign change is produced, and nothing is overlaid on Fig. 11. The argument against antinodal (charge-fluctuation) scattering rests on one comparison (1.03×10⁻⁹ vs 4.3×10⁻¹⁰ m³/C), whose outcome depends on how ε(φ) and the anisotropic weight are chosen. Since the MR does not discriminate the microscopic origin, the spin assignment stands or falls with this calculation. The CVC model has several uncontrolled ingredients:
   - ξ_AFM and c are unknown, and ε(φ) is a Gaussian of unit height and width σ = 0.07 (Eq. 13), so the vertex-correction amplitude is effectively a free choice.
   - The 1/(1−ε²) prefactor is dropped.
   - Eq. (2)/(11) writes ε ≈ 1 − cξ²_AFM, which decreases as the correlation length grows, the opposite of the physical expectation. This is presumably a typo for ξ⁻², but it matters for the claimed T dependence.
   - The Hall calculation uses a Jones–Zener (low-field) expansion with the analytic k_F(φ), v_F(φ) of Eqs. 7–8 and a modified antinodal v_F. The MR calculation uses SCTIF with the Table 1 tight-binding Fermi surface. R_H, meanwhile, was measured at 14 T.
   - The Hall data in Fig. 11 come from other films, with a multiplicative factor between data sets.

   The authors should:
   - compute R_H(T) over 20–100 K at two or more dopings above x_FSR, using one Fermi surface and the same Γ(φ,T,x) and g(x) (with g(x)α1T + α2T²) that fit the MR, and overlay it on Fig. 11b;
   - show how sensitive the sign change is to ε amplitude, σ, ξ_AFM and the dropped prefactor, and state whether it appears only in a narrow band of CVC parameters;
   - show that no reasonable antinodal (charge or phonon) scenario with CVCs reproduces the trend;
   - state how ε and σ were chosen, and the temperature of the Fig. 13 values;
   - ideally, measure R_H on the same S1/S2 channels during the same high-field runs and fit ρ(H,T), Δρ/ρ(H,T) and R_H(T) jointly.

5. **Much of the order-of-magnitude drop in g(x) comes from dopings where the model's assumptions fail.** The paper places x_FSR at 0.14. Below it, a negative isotropic MR from AFM order appears (Figs. 2, 15) and the Fermi surface is reconstructed (Fig. 1). Yet the S1 fits (0.116–0.159) use the unreconstructed tight-binding Fermi surface of Table 1, a hotspot height fixed at all x, and one free parameter. In Table 3, g rises from 1.87 at x = 0.141 to 6.50 at x = 0.116, which accounts for most of the "order of magnitude". The authors themselves suggest (p. 16) that sharper or stronger hotspots below x = 0.14 "may explain the larger increase of g(x)", so g(x) at low x may not measure the same quantity as at high x. The Fig. 5 caption says the fit is restricted to where AFM signatures are "absent or weak", which does not hold for x < 0.14. The authors should refit the x < x_FSR channels with a reconstructed or partially gapped Fermi surface, or with free hotspot parameters, and report how g(x) changes. They should also state the variation of g(x) over x > x_FSR alone (roughly 1.9 to 0.55, a factor of about 3.4) and say whether the correlation with Tc holds there.

6. **Reproducibility of the MR magnitude between films is poor, which undermines MR magnitude as the quantitative constraint.** Each composition is measured on one channel of one film, and the two films overlap only at x = 0.159. There, S2's resistivity must be divided by 1.5 to match S1 (Fig. 6a), and S2's MR is about 3.5 times smaller (Fig. 10c,d). Every S2 channel also carries a low-field negative MR component of unspecified origin, in a range beyond x_FSR where long-range AFM is absent. The central modelling (Fig. 4, Fig. 5b, Table 2) fixes the anisotropy parameters from the S1 MR magnitude at x = 0.159. If the same nominal composition gives a 3.5× different MR in another film, then either a sample-dependent extrinsic contribution affects the MR or one film is not representative. Candidates include compositional inhomogeneity within a bridge, oxygen content, the negative MR term and geometry. Either way the claim that the MR magnitude is set by intrinsic inelastic anisotropy is weakened. The S2 MR is not modelled, so the 0.159–0.188 range, which carries the approach of g toward its minimum as Tc → 0, rests on A1 alone. The authors should:
   - identify the origin of the S2 negative MR, show its T and field-orientation dependence, and explain why S1 lacks it, or whether it is present there less visibly;
   - report the channel-to-channel spread within S1;
   - show MR from at least one additional film, ideally non-combinatorial (for example the x ≈ 0.15–0.16 films of Refs. 29, 31), or an overlapping doping range, to establish which magnitude is intrinsic and that g(x) is reproducible.

**B. Should address**

7. **Parameter identifiability, sensitivity and uncertainties are not reported.** At x = 0.159 the model has τimp, α1, α2, hotspot height and width (Table 2), HS = 27.5° and ν = 12. These are constrained by four ρ(H) curves between 20 and 50 K. At other x, one ρ value and one MR value at a single (T, H) set g(x). No error bars appear on g(x) in Fig. 5c or in Tables 3–4. The authors acknowledge (p. 16) that broadening the hotspot by 2× or shrinking it by 5× changes ρ by about 1 μΩcm, so part of the change in g(x) may belong elsewhere. They should:
   - give a fitting metric and the data-to-parameter ratio for the x = 0.159 fit;
   - report parameter correlations, in particular α1 against ν and against hotspot width, all of which control the anisotropy;
   - refit with the hotspot height and width, τimp⁻¹ (or ρ0), ν (for example 4–16), the HS position, the anisotropy of the T² term and the TB parameters free or varied within bounds at each x, and report the resulting spread of g(x) as error bars;
   - show that the hotspot-peaked solution is a well-defined optimum, not one of a family of adequate fits.

8. **Residual resistivity, absolute resistivity and geometry feed directly into g(x).** For S1, g(x) is fixed partly by the absolute ρ(0, 40 K), which depends on film thickness and channel geometry. τimp⁻¹ = 27 ps⁻¹ is held fixed for all x, so any doping dependence of the residual resistivity, or of the geometric factor along the film, is absorbed into g(x). No continuous ρ(T) exists for S1 (p. 10), so ρ0(x) is not shown, and Fig. 6d indicates that ρ0 varies across the S2 channels. The absolute scale is also uncertain: the new films have about one third of the resistivity of Ref. 5, S2 is rescaled by 1.5, and the Hall magnitudes carry a similar factor (Fig. 11). The absolute scattering rates in Table 2, including α1 and the Planckian comparison (concern 12), therefore carry a factor of about 1.5–3 uncertainty. In addition, Fig. 5b shows the simulation systematically underestimating Δρ/ρ(0), so the MR is not fitted with the same fidelity as ρ. The authors should:
   - state the thickness uniformity along the composition gradient and the uncertainty of each channel's geometric factor;
   - extract ρ0(x) and A1(x) for each channel from the zero-field points (Fig. 6b), refit with ρ0 or τimp free per channel, and show whether the doping dependence of g changes;
   - show g(x) with uncertainty bands that propagate the geometric-factor uncertainty;
   - report the ratio of fitted to measured MR for each channel.

9. **The model is tested only in a narrow window, so the "wholesale applicability" and the conclusions about hole-doped cuprates are overstated.** The full T and H dependence is simulated only at x = 0.159 and 20–50 K (Fig. 4); other dopings are simulated only at 40 K (Fig. 10). The most distinctive features of the data are not simulated: the MR maximum at 50–75 K and its fall at lower T (Fig. 2a–g), the breakdown of H/T scaling below 75 K (Fig. 2h–l), the doping dependence of γ1(T) (Fig. 15), and the regime x < x_FSR. The S2 MR is not modelled. The discontinuity in dρ/dT near 60 K (Fig. 6e), the low-T H-linear MR and the T² resistivity extending far above 70 K are called "not yet understood" (p. 8). Yet the discussion (p. 9) uses the "wholesale applicability" of Boltzmann theory to LCCO as "a strong counter-argument" that a corresponding parameterisation of the hole-doped strange metal should already have been found. It concludes that the higher Tc there "cannot be simply due to an enhanced coupling to spin fluctuations". Neither statement follows from measurements on LCCO alone. The authors should show simulated MR(H) and γ1(T) alongside the data at 3–4 dopings over 20–100 K, using the fixed parameters plus g(x); at minimum, give full T- and H-dependent fits in the style of Fig. 4 at a low-x and a high-x channel (for example x = 0.125 and 0.146). Otherwise they should scope the hole-doped statements as speculation.

10. **An isotropic, non-orbital MR component in the 20–50 K fitting window may be mistaken for temperature-dependent scattering anisotropy.** The key feature behind the ansatz (point 2, p. 5) is that the MR shrinks on cooling below about 60 K. The paper also documents three relevant effects. There is a negative, isotropic spin MR below x_FSR. Every S2 channel shows a low-field negative component (Fig. 10c,d). And a non-zero longitudinal MR appears at lower temperatures (p. 12), attributed to superconducting fluctuations but not shown. The longitudinal MR is shown only at 75 K (Fig. 8). A negative component that grows on cooling and persists, weakened, beyond x_FSR would reduce the low-T MR without any change in the anisotropy of the inelastic scattering. The authors should show the longitudinal (H ∥ I ∥ ab) MR at 20–50 K for several x in both films, subtract any isotropic component, and repeat the Fig. 4 and Fig. 5b analysis on the corrected transverse MR.

11. **Superconducting fluctuations may contaminate the lowest fitted temperatures.** Tc reaches about 25 K at the lowest x (x = 0.116). The fits use 20–50 K MR (Fig. 4), and Fig. 5 uses 40 K throughout, with 30 K isotherms, so they lie within about 1.2–1.6 Tc. Paraconductivity suppressed by field adds a positive, strongly T-dependent MR that the Boltzmann model omits. The paper itself attributes the low-T longitudinal MR to superconducting fluctuations (p. 12). Such a contribution bears directly on the low-T decrease of MR that the model assigns to inelastic anisotropy, and it could inflate the largest g values in Table 3. The authors should estimate the fluctuation contribution to ρ(0, 40 K) and to Δρ/ρ(0) at 20 T as a function of x. They could do this from the longitudinal MR versus T or from an Aslamazov–Larkin/Maki–Thompson estimate for the low-x channels at 20–40 K, and they should show where it is negligible.

12. **The Planckian statement uses the wrong quantity and needs numbers.** On p. 8 the paper says the T-linear rate "varies by almost one order of magnitude across the doping series. Hence, the low-T T-linear resistivity … cannot be tied to the Planckian limit." The Planckian bound concerns the FS-averaged transport rate, ħ/τ = αkBT with α ~ 1. A variation in A1 alone does not refute it, since in Refs. 14, 15 A1 also varies through m* and n. From Table 2, α1 = 0.75 ps⁻¹K⁻¹ is about 5.7 kB/ħ at the hotspot peak. With ν = 12 the mean of sin¹²(2φ) is about 0.23, so the FS-averaged slope at x = 0.159 is about 0.17 ps⁻¹K⁻¹ (about 1.3 kB/ħ), and about 6.5 times larger at x = 0.116. The authors should quote the FS-averaged and nodal T-linear coefficients, α = ħ⟨Γ_T⟩/(kBT) with the band mass, in units of kB/ħ for each x. They should include the uncertainty from the thickness calibration (concern 8), and then state precisely what is, and is not, inconsistent with Planckian scattering (Refs. 14, 19).

15. **The composition gradient within each bridge may contribute part of the persistent H-linear MR.** The model attributes high-field H-linearity to hotspots impeding cyclotron motion. The authors acknowledge (p. 8) that it is not obvious why this persists to the lowest T, and they raise compositional variation as an alternative (Refs. 50, 51). Each microbridge in a combinatorial film spans a finite Δx (the paper suggests Δx ≲ 0.003). H-linearity is a central motivation for the hotspot term (point 3, p. 5), so an extrinsic Herring/Bruls-type contribution would bias the fitted hotspot parameters.
