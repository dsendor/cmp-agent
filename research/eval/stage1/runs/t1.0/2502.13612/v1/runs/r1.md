Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports in-plane resistivity and high-field (35 T) magnetoresistance on two combinatorial La2-xCexCuO4 films covering 0.116 ≤ x ≤ 0.188 in fine doping steps. It models the data with a Shockley-Chambers Boltzmann treatment in which a T-linear, strongly anisotropic inelastic scattering term peaks at the (π,π) hotspots. The one doping-dependent amplitude of that term, g(x), falls by about an order of magnitude as Tc goes to zero. From this the authors conclude that low-energy commensurate spin fluctuations are the pairing glue in electron-doped cuprates, and that hole-doped cuprates need something beyond this picture. The data set is valuable: dense, internally consistent doping coverage at high field is rare for n-type cuprates. The step from a fitted phenomenological amplitude to "the primary pairing glue", however, rests on modelling choices more than on a discriminating measurement.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- An unusually dense doping series (19 compositions, Δx ≲ 0.003) on films grown under identical conditions. The MR is measured to 35 T from 8 K to about 190 K, with the orbital nature checked by transverse and longitudinal MR (Fig. 8).
- A clear, model-independent observation: for x > x_FSR the MR peaks at 50 to 75 K and then falls on cooling while ρ keeps falling (Fig. 2a–g, Fig. 15). This violates Kohler's rule in a direction that constrains how the scattering anisotropy depends on temperature. The breakdown of H/T quadrature scaling (Fig. 2h–l) contrasts usefully with p-type cuprates.
- A transparent set of alternative scattering scenarios (Fig. 9) and a worked attempt to connect the Hall sign change to current vertex corrections (Figs. 12–14).
- The question of linking a transport-derived coupling to Tc is important, and the n-type cuprates are a sensible place to try.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The headline correlation is essentially the known A1–Tc correlation renamed, and nothing in it is specific to spin fluctuations. In Fig. 5 and Table 3, g(x) for S1 is obtained by matching ρ(0, 40 K) and Δρ/ρ(0) at 20 T with one free parameter. For S2 (Table 4, Fig. 5c), g(x) is by construction the T-linear coefficient A1 normalised at x = 0.159. The A1–Tc scaling in LCCO is already established (Ref. 5, Fig. 1 inset). The new step is the claim that this T-linear scattering is (π,π) spin-fluctuation scattering, and that rests on two inputs placed in the ansatz by hand: Gaussian peaks at the AFM zone-boundary crossings in Eq. (1), and the sin^ν envelope with ν = 12 borrowed from hole-doped fits. Fig. 9 shows that some alternatives fit the 20–50 K MR at x = 0.159 less well, but the comparison is visual and uses one doping. It does not exclude other forms of T-dependent anisotropy that peak away from the antinodes, such as nodal-region scattering (dismissed on p. 15 only because no wavevector connects nodes) or anisotropic electron-phonon scattering. Nor does the paper compute any coupling constant: λsf appears in the abstract and discussion but is never defined or extracted. The authors should:
   - state explicitly which part of the result is new relative to Ref. 5;
   - convert g(x)α1 into a dimensionless coupling (for example via ħ/τ = 2πλkBT averaged over the Fermi surface, as in their own Fig. 7 argument for metals) and report λsf(x) with uncertainties;
   - show quantitatively, with a goodness-of-fit measure, that hotspot-centred anisotropy is preferred over the alternatives at several dopings and temperatures, not only at x = 0.159.

   Without these the title claim ("primary pairing glue") is a hypothesis consistent with the data, not a conclusion drawn from it.

2. The Hall-effect argument that places the hotspots at the (π,π) zone boundary is only qualitative, and it is not tested against data from the same films. The main text (p. 8) says the CVC model captures "the key features of RH(T,x) beyond x > xFSR, including the tendency of RH to go negative with increasing T". Fig. 13, however, gives four single RH values for x ≈ 0.16 at one implicit temperature. No simulated RH(T) curve is shown, no doping dependence, and none is overlaid on Fig. 11. There are further problems:
   - The CVC calculation sets the prefactor 1/(1−ε²) aside and fixes ε(φ) as a Gaussian of unit height and width σ = 0.07 (Eq. 13), so the amplitude of the vertex correction is effectively a free choice.
   - Eq. (2)/(11) writes ε ≈ 1 − cξ²_AFM, which decreases as the correlation length grows, the opposite of the physical expectation. This is presumably a typo for ξ^-2, but it matters for the claimed T-dependence.
   - The Hall data in Fig. 11 come from other films, and the text notes a multiplicative factor between data sets.

   The authors should compute RH(T) at fixed x from the same Γ(φ,T,x) used for the MR (with the T-dependence of g(x)α1T + α2T²) at two or three dopings above x_FSR. They should compare it with Hall data measured on the same S1/S2 channels, or at least overlay it on Fig. 11, and show how sensitive the sign change is to the chosen ε amplitude and width. If the drop of RH with T can only be reproduced for a narrow band of CVC parameters, that should be stated.

3. Much of the order-of-magnitude drop in g(x) comes from dopings where the model's assumptions do not hold. The paper locates x_FSR at 0.14 and notes that below it a negative isotropic MR from AFM order appears (Fig. 2, Fig. 15) and that the Fermi surface is reconstructed (Fig. 1). Yet the S1 fits (0.116–0.159) use the unreconstructed tight-binding Fermi surface of Table 1, a hotspot height fixed at all x, and one free parameter. In Table 3, g rises from 1.87 at x = 0.141 to 6.50 at x = 0.116, which accounts for most of the "order of magnitude". The authors themselves suggest (p. 16) that sharper or stronger hotspots below x = 0.14 could produce this rise, so g(x) at low x may not measure the same quantity as at high x. The Fig. 5 caption also says the fit is restricted to where AFM signatures are "absent or weak", which is not true for x < 0.14. The authors should:
   - refit the x < x_FSR channels with a reconstructed (or partially gapped) Fermi surface, or let the hotspot parameters vary, and report how g(x) changes;
   - state the variation of g(x) over x > x_FSR only (roughly 1.9 to 0.55, a factor of about 3.4), and say whether the correlation with Tc holds there alone.

4. An isotropic negative MR component may be mistaken for a temperature-dependent scattering anisotropy. The key feature driving the ansatz (point 2 on p. 5) is that the MR shrinks on cooling below about 60 K. The paper also documents:
   - a negative, isotropic spin MR below x_FSR;
   - a low-field negative component in every S2 channel (Fig. 10c,d), large enough that the S2 MR at x = 0.159 is 3.5 times smaller than the S1 MR at the same nominal composition;
   - a non-zero longitudinal MR at lower temperatures (p. 12), attributed to superconducting fluctuations but not shown.

   A negative component that grows on cooling and persists, weakened, beyond x_FSR would reduce the MR at low T without any change in the anisotropy of the inelastic scattering. The 3.5× discrepancy between films at the same x also shows that the MR magnitude, the input that fixes g(x) in S1, is not reproducible between films. The authors should show the longitudinal (H∥I∥ab) MR at 20–50 K for several x in both films, subtract any isotropic component, and repeat the Fig. 4 and Fig. 5b analysis on the corrected transverse MR. They should also explain why S1 lacks the negative component that dominates S2.

B. Should address

5. Parameter identifiability and uncertainties are not reported. At x = 0.159 the model has τimp, α1, α2, hotspot height and width (Table 2), hotspot angle HS = 27.5°, and exponent ν = 12 ("somewhat arbitrary", p. 6). These are constrained by four ρ(H) curves between 20 and 50 K. The temperature and field dependence are fitted only at x = 0.159; at other x only one ρ value and one MR value at a single (T, H) set g(x). No error bars appear on g(x) in Fig. 5c or Tables 3–4. The authors should give a fitting metric, parameter correlations (in particular α1 against ν and against hotspot width, all of which control the anisotropy), and the spread of g(x) when ν, the hotspot width and τimp are varied within ranges that fit equally well. They should also show at least one full T- and H-dependent fit at a low-x and a high-x channel (for example x = 0.125 and x = 0.146) in the style of Fig. 4.

6. Absolute resistivity, thickness and τimp affect g(x). g(x) is fixed partly by the absolute ρ(0, 40 K), which depends on film thickness and channel geometry. The paper reports that the new films have one third of the resistivity of Ref. 5 and that S2 had to be divided by 1.5 to match S1 (Fig. 6a). The Hall data carry a similar unexplained factor. In addition, τimp is held fixed at 27 ps⁻¹ for all x, although the residual resistivity of a combinatorial film need not be independent of x. The authors should state the thickness uniformity along the composition gradient and the uncertainty of each channel's geometric factor. They should also show ρ0(x) extracted from Fig. 6 and test whether letting τimp follow ρ0(x) changes the doping dependence of g.

7. The quantitative relation between g(x) and Tc is not established. In Fig. 5c and Table 4, g(x) saturates at 0.55 for x = 0.184 and 0.188, where Tc reaches zero. The coupling therefore does not vanish with Tc, and the "clear correlation" is monotonic rather than proportional. The paper motivates the work with the McMillan-type λ–Tc link in metals (Fig. 7), but makes no comparable test here: no plot of Tc against g or λsf, and no discussion of a threshold such as a μ*-like term. Please plot Tc against g(x) directly and fit it to a form that can be compared with spin-fluctuation weak-coupling expectations (Refs. 13, 55, 56). Discuss whether a finite coupling with Tc = 0 is consistent with the proposed pairing mechanism.

8. Few samples, and little evidence of reproducibility. Each composition is measured on one channel of one film, and the two films overlap at only one composition (x = 0.159). There, the resistivity differs by a factor of 1.5 and the MR by a factor of 3.5. Tc for each channel and the nominal-to-actual Ce calibration of each channel are not given; Tc appears only as a nonlinear top axis in Fig. 5c. The authors should tabulate measured Tc (midpoint and width) and channel composition with uncertainties. If possible they should add a second film covering part of the 0.12–0.16 range, or non-combinatorial films (as in Ref. 29), to show that g(x) is reproducible.

9. Superconducting fluctuations near Tc for the low-x channels. For x = 0.116 (Tc ≈ 25 K), the doping-dependent fits at 40 K and the MR isotherms at 30 K lie within about 1.2–1.6 Tc. Fluctuation conductivity suppressed by the field adds a positive MR there that the Boltzmann model does not include, and the paper itself attributes the low-T longitudinal MR to superconducting fluctuations. An estimate of the paraconductivity contribution to ρ(0, 40 K) and to Δρ/ρ(0) at 20 T, as a function of x, would show whether the largest g values in Table 3 are inflated.

10. The persistent H-linear MR is not explained within the model. The model attributes high-field H-linearity to hotspots impeding cyclotron motion. The authors acknowledge (p. 8) that it is not obvious why this persists to the lowest T, and they raise compositional variation as an alternative. In a combinatorial film, each microbridge necessarily spans a composition gradient. If part of the H-linear slope is extrinsic (the Bruls mechanism, Ref. 50), the hotspot parameters fitted to it are biased. The authors should estimate Δx across each voltage-probe pair and the resulting Hall-voltage inhomogeneity contribution. They should also compare the high-field slope with that of a uniform film at the same x (Ref. 29) quantitatively, not only by assertion.

11. The conclusions about hole-doped cuprates go beyond the evidence. The discussion (p. 9) argues that the "wholesale applicability" of Boltzmann theory to LCCO shows a corresponding parameterisation for p-type cuprates should already have been found. It concludes that higher Tc there cannot be due to stronger spin-fluctuation coupling. But the full Boltzmann fit is shown at one doping, the S2 MR is not modelled, the Hall effect is modelled only semi-quantitatively, and the low-T H-linear MR and high-T T² resistivity remain unexplained (p. 8). The authors should temper these statements, or support them with a fit covering all T and H for several x.

12. The Planckian statement needs numbers. The paper states (p. 8) that the T-linear scattering rate varies by almost an order of magnitude and "cannot be tied to the Planckian limit", but never reports ħ/(τkBT) averaged over the Fermi surface. From Table 2, α1 = 0.75 ps⁻¹K⁻¹ is about 5.7 kB/ħ at the hotspot peak, and the sin¹² envelope averages to about 0.23. The authors should give the Fermi-surface-averaged and nodal T-linear coefficients in units of kB/ħ for each x so readers can compare with Refs. 14 and 19.

C. Minor

13. The Hall calculation (Jones-Zener expansion with analytical kF(φ), vF(φ) from Eqs. 7–8, and a modified antinodal vF) and the MR calculation (SCTIF with the Table 1 tight-binding Fermi surface) use different Fermi surface descriptions. Please show that the RH results do not depend on this choice.

14. Table 1 gives tight-binding parameters only at x = 0.1, 0.125, 0.15, 0.175 and 0.2. State how the intermediate compositions are handled, give units for t and ε0, and give the hotspot angle HS(x) used at each doping, including whether hotspots still exist at x = 0.188.

15. Table 2 gives the hotspot width (0.05) without units, and Eq. (1) mixes HS in degrees with π/4 in radians. Please define both consistently.

16. Revision markers remain in the text and captions: "(#X.1)", "(#1.6)", "(#1.3)" and "(#1.2/1.3 (h–l)". Please remove them.

17. The abstract contains a typo, "serves to reinforces". There are also "forx" in the Fig. 14 caption, "Fig. 3/4" on p. 7, and a duplicated "[36] [37–39]" on p. 5.

18. Reference errors: Ref. 55 (Moriya and Ueda, Adv. Phys. 49) is from 2000, not 2010. Ref. 67 reads "21 k". Ref. 5 lists "Kin, K." where Jin, K. is presumably intended. Ref. 41 is cited for vF anisotropy (p. 5), but Ref. 32 is cited for the same statement elsewhere.

19. Fig. 5c uses a nonlinear Tc top axis without giving its source. Fig. 2k,l (S2) are noisy and are not modelled; say so in the caption. The Fig. 6e legend states A1(0.159) = 2A1(0.188), while Table 4 gives a ratio of 1.8; please reconcile them.

20. Eq. (4) uses a 1/4π³ prefactor with a two-dimensional integral d²k. Please state the interlayer normalisation explicitly.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a direct test of where the scattering anisotropy sits, rather than inferring hotspot locations from the ansatz. In-plane field-angle-dependent MR, or angle-dependent MR on the same compositions, would test whether the T-dependent scattering is peaked at the (π,π) crossings. A comparison with existing ARPES linewidths on LCCO (Ref. 64) as a function of x and T would also help.
2. Measure the Hall coefficient on the same S1/S2 channels during the same high-field runs. Fit ρ(H,T), Δρ/ρ(H,T) and RH(T) together with one Γ(φ,T,x) at several dopings. Quantitative agreement across all three observables would greatly strengthen the hotspot identification.
3. Report a dimensionless coupling λsf(x) with error bars and plot Tc against λsf alongside the Allen-type comparison of Fig. 7. Discuss how well the n-type points match a weak-coupling spin-fluctuation Tc formula.
4. Reorganise the presentation so that the model-independent findings (Kohler violation with MR decreasing on cooling, the absence of H/T scaling, the continuity of γ1 across x_FSR) are clearly separated from the model-dependent inferences. Present the pairing-mechanism conclusion as the hypothesis these findings support.
