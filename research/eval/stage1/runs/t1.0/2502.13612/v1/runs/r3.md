Review outline:

**1. Significance and novelty**

The authors measure the in-plane resistivity and high-field (to 35 T) magnetoresistance (MR) of two combinatorial La2−xCexCuO4 films across 19 Ce concentrations (0.116 ≤ x ≤ 0.188). They model the data with a Shockley–Chambers Boltzmann treatment, using an anisotropic scattering rate peaked at the (π,π) hotspots whose T-linear part has an amplitude g(x) that is the only doping-dependent parameter. Hall-coefficient modelling with Kontani-type current vertex corrections is added in support. They conclude that g(x) tracks Tc, that the anisotropic T-linear scattering comes from commensurate AFM spin fluctuations, and therefore that these fluctuations are the pairing glue in electron-doped cuprates. A doping-resolved MR data set of this kind on n-type cuprates is valuable. Attempting to identify a transport "λ" for cuprates, by analogy with Allen's λtr–λph correspondence, is a significant goal. However, the step from a phenomenological fit to "evidence for spin-fluctuation-mediated superconductivity" is not yet supported at the level the title and abstract claim.

**2. Potential reasons for acceptance**

- A dense, uniform doping series of high-field MR, temperature dependence and field-derivative data (Fig. 2, Fig. 15) for an electron-doped cuprate. It spans the region from near optimal doping to the edge of the dome, all grown under nominally identical conditions.
- Several clear observations that do not depend on the model and constrain any theory: no H/T quadrature scaling (Fig. 2h–l), the opposite-to-Kohler growth of Δρ/ρ(0) with T below ~60 K, the MR peaking at 50–75 K, and negligible longitudinal MR at 75 K (Fig. 8).
- A transparent minimal-ansatz Boltzmann model (Eq. 1), with a set of alternative scattering forms shown to fail (Fig. 9). This gives readers a concrete, falsifiable parameterisation.
- A potentially important contrast with the hole-doped strange metal, where the same Boltzmann approach has not succeeded.

**3. Concerns**

**A. Must address**

1. **The g(x)–Tc correlation largely restates the known A1–Tc correlation; the MR's independent contribution has not been shown.** The paper describes g(x) as "the first key result" (p. 7) and "an effective coupling parameter for both transport and superconductivity" (Fig. 5 caption). But in the fits:
   - g(x) scales only the T-linear term (Eq. 1, Table 3), and all other parameters are fixed at their x = 0.159 values (Table 2).
   - g(x) is chosen to reproduce both ρ(0, 40 K) and Δρ/ρ(0) at 20 T (Fig. 5a,b; Table 3).
   - For S2, g(x) is taken directly from the T-linear coefficient A1 of ρ(0,T) (Fig. 6e, Table 4).

   So g(x) is, by construction, close to a renormalised A1. Its correlation with Tc is then the result already reported by Yuan et al. [5] (Fig. 1 inset). The MR's role is to show that the anisotropic form is consistent across x, but that is not demonstrated independently. The authors should:
   - determine g(x) for S1 separately from (i) the MR alone, with ρ0 allowed to vary, and (ii) A1 alone, then compare the two;
   - show that the MR at other temperatures (20, 30, 50 K) and its full field dependence are reproduced at each x, not only the 20 T, 40 K magnitude (Fig. 10 shows only 40 K, with all parameters except g fixed);
   - state what the MR adds beyond the A1–Tc correlation, quantitatively.

   As it stands, the "coupling parameter" identification is not distinct from the earlier A1 result.

2. **The spin-fluctuation origin of the anisotropic T-linear term is an input to the model rather than an output of it.** The hotspot positions at the AFM zone-boundary crossings are imposed (p. 5, Eq. 1, HS = 27.5°). The width exponent ν = 12 is borrowed from a hole-doped parameterisation [19] and fixed. The hotspot height and width (Table 2) are fixed. The alternatives in Fig. 9 are single, apparently unoptimised trial forms at one doping, judged "inferior" by eye. To claim that the data *identify* (π,π) scattering, the authors need to show that the hotspot location is constrained by the data. Specifically:
   - treat the peak position of the anisotropic term (and ν) as free parameters and report the best-fit position with an uncertainty, for several x, including whether it tracks the FS–AFM-zone-boundary crossing as that moves with x;
   - give quantitative goodness-of-fit (χ² or residuals) for Fig. 4 and for each Fig. 9 alternative, with the alternatives' own parameters optimised;
   - test a T-linear anisotropic term peaked at a location unrelated to (π,π), such as near-nodal scattering or an angle-independent T-linear rate combined with a T-dependent hotspot width. Otherwise the conclusion is that "an anisotropic T-linear rate somewhere between node and antinode" fits, not that AFM hotspots do.

3. **Going from a correlation to "the primary pairing glue" needs a quantitative coupling estimate.** The abstract and conclusions (pp. 1, 8–9) state that the correlation between α, λsf and Tc "identifies low-energy spin-fluctuations as the primary pairing glue". However:
   - λsf is never extracted in absolute terms. g(x) is dimensionless and normalised to 1 at x = 0.159.
   - No calculation (McMillan/Eliashberg-like or spin-fluctuation exchange) is presented showing that the inferred coupling gives Tc of the right size or the right d-wave symmetry.
   - Correlation alone cannot exclude a common driver, such as proximity to the AFM/FSR critical point controlling both the scattering and Tc through the density of states or another channel.

   The paper's own motivating analogy (Allen, Fig. 7) rests on comparing absolute λtr and λph. The authors should convert α1 g(x), averaged over the Fermi surface (and at the hotspots), into λtr = ħ(dΓ/dT)/(2πkB), and compare it with what a spin-fluctuation Tc estimate would require. Otherwise they should retitle the paper and reword the abstract and conclusions to "correlation consistent with" spin-fluctuation pairing.

**B. Should address**

4. **The Hall-effect support is only semi-quantitative and is not compared with R_H(T, x).** On p. 8 the text says the model "capture[s] the key features of R_H(T, x) beyond x > x_FSR, including the tendency of R_H to go negative with increasing T". Fig. 13, however, gives four single R_H values for different Γ(φ) forms at unspecified T, with:
   - a separate analytic FS (Eqs. 7–8);
   - a CVC weight ε(φ) of chosen width σ = 0.07, with the 1/(1−ε²) prefactor dropped and no link to a ξ_AFM;
   - the low-field Jones–Zener expansion, compared with data taken at 14 T.

   No simulated R_H(T) curve is overlaid on Fig. 11, and no x dependence is shown. Since the Hall analysis is offered as "the second key finding" (p. 7), the authors should compute R_H(T) at several x with the MR-derived Γ(φ, T, x) and plot it against the Fig. 11 data. They should also state how ε and σ were chosen, and show how sensitive the sign change is to them.

5. **The form of the anisotropic component is described inconsistently.** In Eq. 1 the T-dependent term is sin^ν[2(φ ± (π/4 − HS))], which peaks at the hotspots. The Hall section instead describes the successful form as Γ_N, with ℓ "shorter at the nodes" (Fig. 12c caption) and the broad component "peaked … along the zone diagonals" (Fig. 13d caption). It also states that "the node is three times closer to the AFM boundary than the anti-node" (p. 21), which is hard to reconcile with HS = 27.5° unless the angular convention differs. The authors should:
   - state the angle convention;
   - show the Γ(φ) used in Fig. 13d next to Fig. 4b1 to confirm they are the same function with the same parameters;
   - give the node–hotspot and antinode–hotspot angular distances.

6. **Sample-to-sample consistency is weak, and the second film's MR is not used.** Only two films are measured. They share a single doping (x = 0.159). At that doping:
   - S2's resistivity must be divided by 1.5 to match S1 (Fig. 6a);
   - S2's MR is a factor ~3.5 smaller (Fig. 10c,d), attributed to an unexplained negative low-field component;
   - the absolute resistivities are ~1/3 of those in Ref. [5] (Fig. 6b).

   The doping range from 0.159 to 0.188, which carries the approach of g → 0 as Tc → 0, therefore rests on A1 alone. The authors should:
   - explain the origin of the S2 negative MR, and why it would not also be present, less visibly, in S1;
   - quantify geometric-factor uncertainties per channel;
   - ideally, present MR from a third film or an overlapping doping range, to show that g(x) inferred from MR is reproducible.

7. **The composition gradient within each channel, and the doping calibration, are not quantified.** The combinatorial films have a continuous Ce gradient. Within each micro-bridge there is therefore a finite Δx, and the authors themselves raise longitudinal inhomogeneity as a possible source of the residual H-linear MR (p. 8, Refs. [50, 51]). Since H-linearity is a central ingredient motivating the hotspot term (point 3, p. 5), the authors should:
   - give the Δx across each voltage-probe pair, and estimate the inhomogeneity-induced MR (Herring/Bruls-type) for that gradient;
   - state how x is assigned to each channel (from the nominal targets, or measured by EDX/WDS), and how the Luttinger-count doping used in the tight-binding fits relates to it, given oxygen and annealing effects in T′ films;
   - tabulate Tc for each channel. Fig. 5c's top axis gives only four Tc ticks, and the Tc(x) mapping used for the correlation is not shown.

8. **Superconducting fluctuations at the lowest fitted temperatures.** Tc reaches ~25 K for the lowest x, and the paper fits the 20–50 K MR (Fig. 4) and uses 40 K throughout Fig. 5. Paraconductivity and fluctuation MR are positive and strongly T-dependent near Tc. The authors note that "at lower temperatures, a non-zero [longitudinal] MR appears that we attribute to the emergence of superconducting fluctuations" (p. 12). Such a contribution would bear directly on the decrease of MR at low T that the model attributes to inelastic anisotropy. The authors should estimate the fluctuation contribution (for example from the longitudinal MR versus T, or with an Aslamazov–Larkin/Maki–Thompson estimate) for the low-x channels at 20–40 K, and show where it is negligible.

9. **The sensitivity of g(x) to the fixed parameters is unknown.** The authors acknowledge that broadening the hotspot by 2× or shrinking it by 5× changes ρ by ~1 μΩcm. They also say this "may explain the larger increase of g(x) below x = 0.14" (p. 16), implying that hotspot parameters likely vary with x. If they do, part of the order-of-magnitude change in g(x) may be absorbed elsewhere. The authors should refit with the hotspot height and width, τimp⁻¹ (or ρ0) and the TB parameters free (or varied within bounds) for each x, and report error bars on g(x). The data-to-parameter ratio for the x = 0.159 fit (Table 2 plus ν, HS) should also be stated.

10. **The conclusions about hole-doped cuprates and the Planckian bound go beyond the data.** The paper states that the wholesale applicability of Boltzmann theory to LCCO "provides a strong counter-argument" that a Boltzmann parameterisation of the hole-doped strange metal exists (p. 9), and that the higher Tc there "cannot be simply due to an enhanced coupling to spin fluctuations". Neither follows from measurements on LCCO alone. Similarly, the statement that the T-linear term "cannot be tied to the Planckian limit" (p. 8) needs the FS-averaged dΓ/dT (with the band mass) compared with kB/ħ, not the relative change in g(x). These statements should be scoped as speculation, or supported quantitatively.

11. **The evidence from Fig. 15 (continuity across x_FSR) is weak.** A smooth evolution of γ1(T) through x_FSR is taken to show that the low-x negative MR (attributed to AFM order) and the high-x anisotropic scattering "share the same origin" (pp. 8, 23). The data are sparse in T, and the high-x curves sit near zero on a ±0.3 scale (the inset is needed to see them at all). Continuity does not exclude two different mechanisms that cross over smoothly. The authors should either strengthen this with a fit that uses one parameter set across x_FSR, or soften the claim.

**C. Minor**

12. Text–figure numerical mismatch: p. 7 states "a 3-fold drop in ρ(0, 40 K)" across S1, but Fig. 5a shows ~44 → ~24 μΩcm, a factor of ~1.8. Please correct this, or clarify what is being compared.

13. Leftover response/editing markers appear throughout: "(#1.2/1.3 (h–l))" in the Fig. 2 caption, "(#X.1)" on p. 5, "(#1.6)" and "(#1.3)" on p. 6, and "(#1.3)" in the Fig. 5 caption. Please remove them.

14. The Fig. 6b,c legends cite "Ref. [1]" for the x = 0.123 and 0.129 data. These are evidently from Ref. [5] (Yuan et al.), not Allen.

15. The doping range for Fig. 2 is given as 0.115 < x < 0.16 in the text (p. 3) and 0.116 ≤ x ≤ 0.159 in the caption. Panel (k) and the caption also mention "5 different x concentrations (3 from S1, 2 from S2)", whereas panels h–l show x = 0.116, 0.131, 0.146 (S1) and 0.169, 0.188 (S2). Please make these consistent. The Fig. 9 caption labels row (b) "without hotspots", but panel b1 should be checked against this description.

16. The Fig. 4 caption reports "HS = 27.5°" without a reference direction, and the Fig. 13 R_H values are given without the temperature at which they were computed. Please also define "AΩ" in the Fig. 1 insets.

17. The Methods state the field was applied "perpendicular or parallel to the CuO2 planes", but the alignment accuracy and the effect of small misalignment on the in-plane "longitudinal" configuration (Fig. 8) are not given.

**4. Suggestions for improvement**

1. Add a figure plotting g(x) directly against measured Tc for every channel of both films, with error bars propagated from the fit-parameter sensitivity. Overlay the earlier A1/Tc data of Ref. [5] on the same normalised axes, so readers can see what the MR analysis adds.
2. Convert the fitted T-linear anisotropic rate into an absolute, FS-averaged dimensionless coupling λtr(x). Place it on an Allen-style plot alongside a theoretical spin-fluctuation λ(Tc), making the analogy to Fig. 7 explicit.
3. Present a global fit across x and T with the hotspot location free, and show that it lands on the AFM-zone-boundary crossing at each doping. That would turn the spin-fluctuation identification from an assumption into a result.
4. Bring the Hall analysis to the same footing as the MR analysis: use one Fermi surface, one Γ(φ, T, x) and one CVC parameter set, and compare R_H(T) curves directly with Fig. 11 at several dopings. Ideally, also show high-field Hall data on the same S1/S2 channels.
