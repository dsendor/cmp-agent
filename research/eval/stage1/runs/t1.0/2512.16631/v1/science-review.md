A. Must address

1. The WP assignment is not shown to be unique, and the most obvious alternative, umklapp replicas of the attractive polarons themselves, is not excluded. The central new claim (title, abstract, pp. 4–5, Figs. 1c, 2c,d) is that WP_T and WP_S are attractive polarons (APs) dressed by pairs of WC phonons, with ΔE_WP = 2ω_T ∝ n^(3/4). The paper argues (p. 4) that "there exists only one repulsive polaron and one umklapp peak because these resonances originate from exciton-electron scattering states". That explains why there is no separate RP umklapp per spin channel, but not why a bound trion (AP) moving in a periodic WC, which also has a folded band, would show no umklapp satellite. Such a replica would sit above each AP at ΔE = h²n/(√3 m_T), blueshift linearly with n, and inherit the triplet/singlet valley selectivity of its parent, so it matches every property of WP_T and WP_S used in the paper, including the polarization data of Fig. 3. The paper's own numbers support it: the RP umklapp slope is ≈2.0 meV per 10¹¹ cm⁻² with m_X ≈ 0.85 m0 (Fig. 2b); with m_T ≈ m_X + m_e ≈ 1.25 m0 an AP umklapp would have a slope of ≈1.35–1.4 meV per 10¹¹ cm⁻², essentially the linear-fit slope reported for ΔE_WP in Fig. S4 (A ≈ 1.50 meV per 10¹¹ cm⁻²); the slope ratio ~0.75 is close to m_X/m_T ~ 0.7. The ~7 meV intercept of that linear fit, the only feature distinguishing the pictures, is itself roughly the T–S splitting (the WP_S line in Fig. S4 extrapolates to the T− energy), so a feature tied to the other trion or another fine-structure satellite is also a candidate. Other alternatives (hybridization with the RP, inhomogeneous regions, Fano or interference artefacts of the dispersive line shape in a derivative map) are not discussed either. Because the paper concedes (p. 5; SI p. 2) that n^(3/4) and linear-plus-offset forms cannot be told apart over the density window shown (the WP lines are not resolved at low density), the power-law fit does not discriminate. Extracted WP energies against n with uncertainties are not shown at all: Fig. 2d only overlays theory curves on a colour map plotted against V_TG, while Fig. 2b uses n. The authors should: (i) state the expected AP-umklapp energy and its oscillator strength from the theory of Ref. 41, and explain explicitly why it is absent or negligible; (ii) extract WP peak positions with error bars against calibrated density, plotted on a common axis with the RP-umklapp data from the same spectra, and fit both the umklapp form (fixed m_T, zero intercept) and the n^(3/4) phonon model over the same window, with the AP energies and doping onset treated consistently and goodness-of-fit compared; (iii) show whether the WP splitting extrapolates to zero at the onset of doping, with data or an upper bound at the lowest densities where the forms differ most; (iv) use observables that separate the pictures: the WP/AP oscillator-strength ratio against n (Franck–Condon/Huang–Rhys factor versus structure-factor weight), the WP linewidth relative to the AP linewidth, whether ΔE_WP/ΔE_U follows the m_X/m_T scaling expected for umklapp or the n^(-1/4) scaling expected for phonons, the ratio of WP_T and WP_S splittings, and if possible the trion-mass dependence. Without this, the claim that the satellites "reveal Wigner crystal dynamics" is not established.

2. The WP features do not track crystalline order, and the data in hand point the other way. If WPs are AP-plus-WC-phonon states, they should vanish (or change qualitatively) wherever the crystal melts. Instead: under optical pumping (p. 6, Figs. 4a,b, S6, S10) the umklapp "nearly vanishes" at ~4000 nW while the WPs "remain robust", surviving with broadening and a redshift up to 8000 nW; under heating (Fig. S7) the WP features and labels are gone at 10 K, while the umklapp persists to 15 K and n* remains ~6×10¹¹ cm⁻² at 10 K (Fig. S8); along the density axis, the WPs are traced in Fig. 2d only to V_TG ≈ 1.0–1.3 V (WP_S fades near ~1.2 V in Fig. 1c), while the umklapp persists to ~1.5 V, and it is not stated whether they disappear at n_c ≈ 7×10¹¹ cm⁻². A feature that survives loss of long-range order yet vanishes under modest heating is hard to reconcile with "a direct measure of the characteristic phonon frequency of the Wigner crystal" (p. 4). Either the umklapp loss is not melting, or the WP is not specific to the crystal (it could reflect short-range correlations or local vibrations in a correlated liquid, or something unrelated to the WC). The authors should plot WP energy, linewidth and amplitude against temperature (5–15 K in fine steps), against density up to and beyond n_c, and against pump power, on the same axes as the umklapp amplitude; explain within the model of Ref. 41 why a phonon-dressed polaron would survive melting of the lattice that supplies the phonons yet be more fragile thermally than umklapp scattering; and temper the title and abstract if the WP cannot be shown to require long-range crystalline order.

3. The evidence that the umklapp feature signals a long-range-ordered WC, rather than a strongly correlated liquid or disorder-pinned glass, is thin, and the melting criterion is arbitrary. The only diagnostic is the presence of the RP umklapp, and its disappearance is defined by a fixed threshold on dR_C/dV (−0.005 to −0.0065 in Methods p. 14; ±0.005 in the Fig. S8 caption). The umklapp amplitude scales with the RP oscillator strength and linewidth, the exciton–electron coupling and the Debye–Waller factor, not only with the presence of order, and the RP oscillator strength itself changes strongly with n and pump power (Fig. S11c). The quantitative parameters are also far from expectations: melting at r_s ≈ 12 (κ = 4.5, m_e = 0.4 m0) is a factor ~3 below the clean-limit value of ~31–38, and the umklapp survives to 15–25 K (Figs. S7, S8; SI p. 3), whereas the classical criterion Γ ≈ 130 with a Coulomb energy of ~18–20 meV gives k_BT_m ≈ 0.3 meV, i.e. T_m of only ~1–few K at n ≈ 5×10¹¹ cm⁻². Both discrepancies are attributed to disorder (pp. 4–5; SI section 2) without a test. An umklapp-like satellite needs only a density modulation at k_W on the exciton's length scale, which a correlated liquid with a sharply peaked structure factor could also produce, with weight falling off gradually in n and T. The authors should normalize the umklapp amplitude to the fitted RP oscillator strength and width and fit it as a continuous function of n, T and power, showing whether it falls sharply (a transition) or gradually (a crossover); show that n* and T_m are insensitive to the choice of threshold; show the umklapp linewidth against n and T as a measure of correlation length; estimate what umklapp intensity a correlated liquid would produce; compare T_m(n) quantitatively with classical and quantum melting estimates; and discuss how the observation distinguishes a WC from short-range Wigner-like correlations. "First observation of WC in WSe2" and "zero-field Wigner crystals" should be scoped accordingly, or supported by evidence of long-range order.

4. The "optical melting" claim does not exclude heating, photodoping or probe artefacts. (i) The pulsed "pump" is the same filtered supercontinuum used as the reflectance probe (Methods p. 12: "a pulsed white laser is utilized as the pumping source"), so "pump power" is the probe power, the measurement is time-integrated over the pulse, and there is no independent pump-off reference; a probe-intensity artefact or saturation at the ~0.5 mW peak power reached at 4 µW average is not excluded. (ii) The 650–800 nm band extends to 1.91 eV, at or above the ~1.9 eV free-carrier gap quoted on p. 6, and spans the 2s/3s Rydberg states, so photo-carrier generation and photodoping via hBN or graphite defects are possible; the "no appreciable shift in RP and AP energies" argument is only qualitative. (iii) The 1s-exciton thermometer (Fig. S9) is coarse: the 1s energy barely moves below ~8 K (under 1 meV between 5 and 10 K), its ~0.2 meV resolution corresponds to ~5–8 K, comparable to the temperatures where n* starts to fall in Fig. S8, and a ~0.2 meV drop is already visible at the lowest powers. It is presumably taken at charge neutrality, not inside the WC regime, and it says nothing about the electron temperature, which the authors acknowledge may be higher (p. 6) and which matters given the steep n*(T) in Fig. S8. The authors should separate pump and probe (a weak fixed probe with a separately tunable, strictly below-gap, narrowband or time-delayed pump); overlay the RP energy against gate voltage at several powers and bound the implied density shift; map the power dependence of n* onto an equivalent temperature using Fig. S8 and compare with the lattice-temperature bound; use an electronic thermometer in the doped regime (RP or T− energy, or T−/S− or umklapp linewidths calibrated against Fig. S7); and compare the umklapp line shape at a given pump power with a thermal data set matched in RP linewidth. Unless this is done, "optical melting" should be stated as "pump-induced suppression of the umklapp resonance".

5. The injected exciton densities behind Fig. 4c are not reproducible and look internally inconsistent. The Methods (p. 13) use n_X = PαΓ/(Aħω) with peak power P = P_avg/(f_R t_p), assuming a 200 ps pulse, multiplied by the lifetime. This is valid only for lifetimes short compared with the pulse; for dark excitons with lifetimes of hundreds of ps to ns, the per-pulse density is bounded by the pulse energy times α/(Aħω), and inter-pulse accumulation over the 25 ns period must be considered, so the dark-exciton density is mis-estimated. The absorbance ("10%, for example"), spot size A, lifetimes, and the spectral overlap of the 650–800 nm pump with the absorption spectrum are not given, nor is the power corresponding to each point in Fig. 4c. The text says the dark-exciton density at threshold is "approximately 10¹¹ cm⁻²" and "still lower than n_c", yet the top axis of Fig. 4c reaches ~6×10¹¹ cm⁻², comparable to n_c, and at the highest power n_c itself has dropped to ~1×10¹¹ cm⁻². Since the conclusion that "bright excitons alone are unlikely to melt the WC" (p. 6) rests on these numbers, the authors should state every input with uncertainties, correct the formula and include pulse-train accumulation, add a pump-power axis to Fig. 4c, give error bars on the densities, reconcile the quoted numbers, and state the pump wavelength range and probe conditions explicitly.

6. The phonon-model comparison is only semi-quantitative, and "good quantitative agreement" (p. 5) is not yet earned. The SI model (section 1) is a single Einstein oscillator: the trion sits in a harmonic well with the electron's frequency, phonons are created in pairs, and 2ω_T is compared with the data. The WC has a gapless acoustic phonon continuum, and a sideband coupled to it would generally be broad and asymmetric rather than sharp. Near r_s ≈ 12, close to melting, zero-point motion is large and anharmonicity should be strong (ħω_e ≈ 9 meV against a ≈20 meV Coulomb energy per electron at 5×10¹¹ cm⁻²). The fit has one free parameter but also fixes the AP reference energies and doping onset; the fitted curves in Fig. 2d start near V_TG ≈ −0.3 V while the onset in Figs. 1b and S11 appears near 0 V. The inputs (m_e, m_T, κ, ζ = 11.034 for an unscreened lattice, gate screening) and the predicted a are not listed, each is uncertain at the tens-of-percent level, and the measured a is 1.5 times the model value, unexplained by calculation; the crystal also appears stiffer than predicted near melting, where it should soften. The ~7 meV intercept of the linear alternative is called unphysical (SI p. 2), but that argument sits only in the SI and cuts against the phonon picture as much as for it. Ref. 41, which carries the key model, is an unpublished preprint by co-authors listed without an identifier. The authors should list all parameters and give the error on a; show residuals and fit statistics for the n^(3/4) and linear fits; fit a separately for WP_T and WP_S to test the claim of identical splittings; state the density origin of the fit; show how a changes over plausible ranges of m_e, κ and gate distance; discuss the validity of the harmonic approximation (e.g., the Lindemann ratio at the measured densities); state which WP properties (sharpness, weight, equal T and S splittings) follow from the full model of Ref. 41 rather than the Einstein approximation, and give the predicted WP/AP weight ratio and linewidth for comparison with the data; move the intercept argument into the main text next to Fig. S4; and summarize Ref. 41 in a self-contained SI sufficient to reproduce the predicted a.

7. The spin-control experiment (Fig. 3, p. 5) does not isolate anything specific to the WC, and the polarization is not quantified. Valley pumping of resident electrons in WSe2 by circularly polarized light is established (Refs. 53, 54), and the helicity dependence of T− and S− reproduces it. That WP_T and WP_S follow their parent APs shows they are AP-derived, but any AP-derived satellite, including an AP umklapp (concern 1), would do the same, so Fig. 3 does not discriminate between WP models; and a polarized electron population need not be crystalline. The 635 nm (≈1.95 eV) CW pump at 4 µW lies above the ~1.9 eV free-carrier gap and can photodope or heat the sample, shifting density between the "pumped" and "no pumping" traces in Fig. 3d. The authors should extract the electron valley polarization from the T−/S− weight ratio, calibrated against a magnetic-field measurement where it saturates; give its pump-power and gate dependence; show that the RP energy (hence n) is matched in the compared traces; quantify, with uncertainty and against the model, the statement that the umklapp is "largely unaffected" (Fig. S5, currently at one density); compare with the same measurement above n_c or above the melting temperature; show that the effect reverses with pump helicity and is absent under linear pumping; specify the probe polarization and the "no pumping" detection configuration; and rephrase "all-optical control of spins in the Wigner crystal" and "valley-dependent WP scattering well above the magnetic ordering temperature" as control of the electrons' valley polarization.

9. The density calibration, gate geometry and screening are under-specified, and the uncertainty in derived quantities is understated. The hBN thicknesses, gate-to-sample distances and onset voltage are not given, though they enter the density formula (Methods). They also decide whether gate screening matters: the WC lattice constant at n_c is ~13 nm, and a gate within a few tens of nm would soften long-wavelength phonons, changing both the melting density and the WP stiffness, whereas Eq. The Methods state a "single-gate scan", while Fig. 1a shows two gates and the density formula includes both; which gate was swept in each figure and how the other was held must be stated. 10. The extraction of the umklapp energy and exciton mass (Fig. 2b, Figs. S2, S3, p. 4) needs care. For n < ~2.5×10¹¹ cm⁻², ΔE_U (< 5 meV) is smaller than the RP linewidth (~4.5–6 meV, Fig. S11b), so the umklapp position depends on subtracting the dispersive-Lorentzian RP fit, whose residuals dominate near E_X (Fig. S2). In Fig. S3 the Lorentzian-fit method gives a ~1.5 meV offset at n → 0 that the other methods do not and stops near 3.8×10¹¹ cm⁻², there are outliers at ~3.7–3.8×10¹¹ cm⁻² (17–19 meV), and the points above 6×10¹¹ cm⁻² scatter strongly. The quoted "m_X ≈ (0.85 ± 0.8) m0" carries a nearly 100% uncertainty, presumably a typo for ±0.08, but as written it voids the comparison with 0.81 m0. The RP is a dressed exciton, so the splitting probes the RP mass rather than the bare exciton mass. The authors should state the fit range, whether the fit is constrained through the origin, how outliers are treated, how the uncertainty in m_X is obtained, and discuss the RP-versus-bare-exciton mass.
