## A. Must address

1. **The Wigner-polaron (WP) resonances are not shown to be specific to the Wigner crystal; the data suggest they persist where the crystal is absent, and an electron-liquid origin is not excluded.** The central claim (pp. 4–5) is that WP_T and WP_S are attractive polarons (APs) dressed by WC phonons, so they "originate from WCs" and "probe the dynamics of a Wigner crystal". By the paper's own criterion the WC melts at n_c ≈ 7×10^11 cm^-2, where the umklapp disappears. Yet in Fig. 2d the dashed WP traces (WP_S in particular) remain visible up to a top-gate voltage of about 1.1–1.2 V, which on the figure's top axis is roughly 8–9×10^11 cm^-2, and in Fig. 1c both WP branches are drawn to about 1.4 V. The paper does not state whether the apparent fading of the WPs around 1.0–1.2 V coincides with n_c. In the optical-melting data (Fig. 4b, p. 6) the WPs "remain robust" after the umklapp has vanished. The authors rationalize this as WPs probing "local short-range correlations" in a "partially melted regime", but that argument concedes that the WPs do not need long-range crystalline order, which undercuts the title claim and the interpretation of the prefactor a as a crystal stiffness; the short-range argument is also invoked only for optical melting, not for density-driven melting. Neither the density dependence nor the polarization selectivity fixes the origin: the authors concede the n^{3/4} power law "cannot be unambiguously extracted" and that a linear fit also works (Fig. S5), and a satellite whose splitting from the trion grows linearly with n is what Fermi-sea processes give (shake-up satellites in which the trion recoils and leaves an electron–hole excitation, trion–plasmon or exciton–electron scattering continua, all scaling with E_F ∝ n). Any such satellite, or an excited or bound trion complex, would also inherit the valley selection rules of its parent trion (Fig. 3). The authors should: (i) extract WP energies, spectral weights (normalized to the parent AP oscillator strength) and linewidths by lineshape fits and plot them against density through and beyond n_c on the same axis as the umklapp weight, stating at which density each feature disappears; (ii) measure the temperature dependence of the WP and umklapp features together at fixed density through the thermal melting temperature (the Methods describe the ATC100 heater, and Figs. S8–S9 are cited only for exciton energies) and show whether the WPs vanish with the umklapp; (iii) state what the theory of Ref. 34 predicts for the AP spectrum in the electron liquid at the same density, and compare quantitatively with a Fermi-liquid shake-up or recoil satellite, explaining why such a satellite would be absent or different above n_c. If the WPs survive in the liquid, the claims should be reframed as probing short-range correlations rather than WC phonons, and the title and abstract softened accordingly.

2. **The WP assignment is not tested against the obvious alternative of AP umklapp.** (pp. 4–5, Fig. 2d, Supplementary Discussion 1.) If the WC folds the exciton band for the repulsive polaron (RP), it should also fold the dispersion of the attractive polarons/trions. That would produce satellites above T⁻ and S⁻ that blueshift with density, are similar for the two branches, and inherit their valley selectivity (Fig. 3): exactly the properties used to identify the WPs. The two pictures differ in density scaling (umklapp gives ΔE ∝ n/m*, with m* the trion/AP mass ≈ m_X + m_e; the phonon picture gives n^{3/4}, which the data do not determine) and in magnitude: with a = 4.16 meV/(10^11 cm^-2)^{3/4}, ΔE_WP ≈ 16 meV at 6×10^11 cm^-2, which exceeds the RP umklapp splitting at the same density (~12 meV, Fig. 2b), whereas a heavier-mass AP umklapp should lie below it. The authors should overlay on Fig. 2d the parameter-free AP-umklapp prediction (trion mass from the fitted m_X and m_e) together with the minimal phonon-model prediction, fit both models over the same density window, and report χ² or residuals. They should also explain why phonon dressing of the AP produces a resolved peak rather than a broad sideband, and why no analogous phonon sideband appears on the RP, since this RP/AP asymmetry ("relatively insensitive" versus strongly dressed) is central to distinguishing WPs from simple AP umklapp.

3. **The quantitative link between the WP–AP splitting and the WC phonon frequency is not shown, its magnitude is implausibly large, and gate screening is omitted.** The paper calls the WP–AP splitting "a direct measure of the characteristic phonon frequency" (p. 4) and fits ΔE_WP = a n^{3/4} with one free parameter (Fig. 2d, a = 4.16 meV/(10^11 cm^-2)^{3/4}), calling a value about 1.5 times the minimal phonon model "good quantitative agreement" and attributing the excess to a "stiffer crystal". Problems: (i) the splitting is ~13–16 meV at 5–6×10^11 cm^-2 and ~18 meV at n_c, whereas a naive unscreened WC plasma/Debye scale, ħ(e²/4πε0 ε m* a_L³)^{1/2} with m* = 0.4 m0, ε = 4.5 and lattice constant a_L ≈ 13 nm, is only a few meV (of order 5 meV at 7×10^11 cm^-2); (ii) the main text gives neither the model expression, nor the prefactor, nor which mass enters (electron or trion), nor ε, nor which phonon branch and wavevector dominate (zone-boundary, Debye average, weighted spectral function), nor whether one- or multi-phonon processes are involved, so the factor of 1.5 cannot be checked; (iii) the n^{3/4} law assumes unscreened 1/r interactions, but the hBN spacers are 20 nm and 31 nm while the WC lattice constant runs from ~13 nm at n_c to >30 nm at 1×10^11 cm^-2, so the graphite gates screen at distances comparable to or shorter than the lattice spacing, changing both the phonon spectrum and its density scaling, especially at low density; (iv) the dashed lines in Fig. 2d are overlaid on a colour map rather than fitted to extracted peak positions with uncertainties, and the fitted density range and treatment of the weak low-density signal are not stated. The authors should give the full model and parameter values (with computed transverse/longitudinal phonon energies at a few densities), include gate screening for the actual geometry, show extracted peak positions with error bars against both the n^{3/4} and linear fits with residuals, and ideally compare devices with different hBN thicknesses, where gate screening predicts a measurably different density dependence. Until then, "a direct measure of the phonon frequency" and "good quantitative agreement" are overstated.

4. **The optical-melting conclusion rests on a fixed-threshold criterion and on incomplete exclusion of heating, photodoping and probe nonlinearity.** (Abstract: "we show optical melting of the Wigner crystal"; pp. 6–7; Fig. 4; Methods p. 15; Figs. S7–S11.) (a) The critical density in Fig. 4c is the point where dR_C/dV crosses a fixed value (−0.005 to −0.0065), and its error bars reflect only the choice of threshold. This quantity depends on umklapp oscillator strength, linewidth and the gate-derivative processing; power-induced broadening (stated for the WP, Fig. S11), saturation or bleaching of the RP, inhomogeneous broadening or reduced reflectance contrast would all lower it without any change in crystalline order. (b) Lattice heating is excluded only via the 1s exciton energy (Fig. S10). The WC melting temperature and the corresponding ~2 meV expected 1s shift are not given explicitly with the 1s fit resolution, and the WSe2 gap is flat at low T (Varshni behaviour), so heating to 10–20 K is not excluded. The authors concede that the electron temperature may exceed the lattice temperature, and electronic heating is precisely the mechanism that would melt the crystal without shifting the 1s exciton. (c) The 650 nm edge of the pump band (1.91 eV) lies at the quoted free-carrier gap (~1.9 eV) and overlaps the 2s/3s states, and the graphite gates and contacts also absorb, so photodoping and photogating are not excluded; the statement that RP/AP energies do not shift does not bound the photodoped density. (d) The same pulsed supercontinuum appears to serve as both pump and probe (40 nW standard, 40–8000 nW for melting; "while simultaneously measuring their reflectance spectrum"), so a nonlinear probe response cannot be separated from an effect on the crystal; this should be stated explicitly. The authors should fit the umklapp spectral weight (normalized to the RP) and linewidth at each power and density and define n* from the integrated weight extrapolated to zero, as for the density-driven case; measure n_c(T) and compare power-induced with thermally induced loss of umklapp at equal weight to bound an effective electron temperature; show that the doping onset voltage, the RP blueshift–density relation and the 2s energy are unchanged with power; and use a separate weak probe with a pump at a different energy (resonant with 1s, below the trion energies, above the gap) at matched absorbed power. Without these controls the data should be described as power-induced suppression of the umklapp resonance, and optical melting stated as a hypothesis.

## B. Should address

5. **The exciton-density estimates behind the exciton-screening mechanism (Fig. 4c axes) are underspecified, internally inconsistent and probably wrong for dark excitons.** (p. 7; Fig. 4c; Methods p. 15.) The conversion n_X = PαΓ/(Aħω) uses an absorbance of 10% given only "for example"; the spot size A, the bright and dark lifetimes Γ, the absorbance across the 650–800 nm band and the spectral fraction of absorbed pump power are not stated, so the axes (up to ~1.5×10^9 cm^-2 bright, ~6×10^11 cm^-2 dark) cannot be reproduced. The text says the dark-exciton density is "approximately 10^11 cm^-2", while the top axis of Fig. 4c reaches ~6×10^11 cm^-2, comparable to n_c and to the electron density; at such densities dark excitons should measurably shift or broaden the RP and AP, which is not reported. Conversely, it is hard to see how ~10^9 cm^-2 bright excitons reduce n_c from 6.7 to 0.8×10^11 cm^-2. The peak-power formula assumes a lifetime short compared with the 200 ps pulse, which fails for dark excitons with ns lifetimes (Refs. 52–53) that may exceed the 25 ns repetition period; then the per-pulse density is bounded by the absorbed pulse energy and inter-pulse accumulation matters. A rough estimate for 4000 nW at 40 MHz (0.1 pJ per pulse, ~1 µm² spot, 10% absorbance) gives an absorbed photon density of order 10^12 cm^-2 per pulse, well above the quoted values. The authors should list all parameters with sources, treat pulsed versus steady-state and inter-pulse accumulation explicitly, propagate uncertainties onto the Fig. 4c axes, and then reassess whether the exciton-screening mechanism (and the conclusion that bright excitons "alone are unlikely to melt the WC") is plausible.

6. **The "all-optical control of spins in the Wigner crystal" experiment does not isolate a WC-specific effect and does not quantify the polarization.** (pp. 5–6, Fig. 3, Methods p. 13.) Under σ+ pumping the co-polarized probe favours T⁻/WP_T and the cross-polarized probe S⁻/WP_S. This is the valley selection rule T⁻ and S⁻ already obey in the electron liquid (Ref. 49 reports optically induced polarization in electron-doped WSe2 without a WC), so it shows that each WP shares the valley character of its parent AP, not that the crystal plays a role. The degree of electron valley polarization is never extracted (e.g. from the T⁻/S⁻ weight ratio against the unpumped case, or Kerr/Faraday). Fig. 3d compares a 4 µW above-gap CW pump with no pump, so heating and photodoping are confounded with polarization, and it shows a single density. The abstract's "valley-dependent Wigner polaron scattering well above the magnetic ordering temperature" implies an untested link to magnetic order and rests only on these line cuts. The authors should show density-resolved WP_T/WP_S weights under co- and cross-polarization across n_c, quantify the valley polarization, include helicity-reversed (σ⁻ pump) and linearly polarized pump controls of equal power, and quantify the statement that the umklapp is "largely unaffected" (U_RP amplitude and energy under co, cross and no pump, with error bars). If the effect does not change across n_c, the claim should be reframed as valley pumping of the electron system, probed by the WPs.

7. **The spin and melting experiments are inconsistent in pump conditions.** Fig. 3 uses a 4 µW CW pump at 635 nm (1.95 eV, above the quoted free-carrier gap), yet the umklapp branch remains clearly visible (Figs. 3b–d). Fig. 4 reports that a pulsed white-light pump of ~4000 nW average power, mostly below the gap, nearly eliminates it. The authors should explain why comparable average powers have such different effects (peak intensity, duty cycle, photon energy, absorption, spot size, different device or spot), and characterize pump-induced heating and photodoping in Fig. 3 as well. A possible reading, that melting depends on resonant exciton generation rather than absorbed power, would itself be informative and is a direct test of the heating versus exciton-density explanations in concern 4.

8. **The r_s ≈ 12 estimate and the "enhanced stability" claim rest on inconsistent parameters, lack error analysis, and ignore gate screening, finite temperature and detection bias.** (pp. 4–5; Methods p. 14.) The density is computed with ε_hBN = 3.5 (3.1 as an alternative), but r_s uses ε = 4.5 and m* = 0.4 m0, neither justified; literature WSe2 conduction-band masses span ~0.3–0.5 m0. Since r_s ∝ m*/(ε√n), these choices and the ~10% density uncertainty move r_s by several units. The comparison with clean-limit Monte Carlo values (r_s ≈ 31–38) assumes unscreened Coulomb interactions in a strictly 2D system at T = 0, whereas here the gates are 20–31 nm away, the WC lattice constant is ~13 nm at n_c, and the measurement is at 5 K. Moreover, n_c is defined as where the umklapp becomes "indiscernible": a correlated liquid with a strong structure-factor peak can also give a broadened umklapp-like feature, and a weakening feature can fall below the noise before the crystal melts. The authors should give r_s,c with an error band, justify ε (in-plane versus out-of-plane hBN) and m*, show umklapp amplitude/linewidth versus n near n_c and discuss which way each bias goes, and discuss how gate screening and finite temperature change the expected critical r_s before attributing the factor-~3 difference to disorder or Berry curvature. A temperature-dependent n_c or comparison of devices with different disorder (Fig. S1) would strengthen this; without it "even stronger than MoSe2" is not supported.

9. **The exciton-mass extraction has a wrong uncertainty and is partly circular.** (p. 4, Fig. 2b.) The text gives m_X ~ (0.85 ± 0.8) m0, a ~95% uncertainty that would make the claimed agreement with 0.81 m0 meaningless and cannot follow from the stated ~10% density uncertainty (presumably ±0.08, or ~0.09 m0 with that uncertainty, is meant). Further: the slope of ΔE_U against n tests the density calibration as much as the mass; the relevant mass is that of the RP quasiparticle, which can differ from the bare exciton mass at these densities; and the linear fit includes points at ΔE_U ≲ 2 meV, below the RP linewidth, where the umklapp cannot be spectrally separated. The authors should correct the uncertainty, state the fit range and whether the intercept was fixed at zero (and whether a free intercept is consistent with zero), fit with and without the low-density points, and report statistical and systematic (density) uncertainties separately.
