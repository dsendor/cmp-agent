Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports reflectance spectroscopy of an hBN-encapsulated, gated WSe2 monolayer at 5 K. It identifies an exciton umklapp resonance above the repulsive polaron (RP) that vanishes above n_c ≈ 7×10^11 cm^-2, taken as evidence of a zero-field electron Wigner crystal (WC). It also reports two new resonances above the triplet and singlet attractive polarons (APs), which it interprets as "Wigner polarons" (WP): APs dressed by WC phonons. Using these, the authors claim optical access to WC phonon dynamics, all-optical control of the WC valley/spin polarization, and optical melting of the WC. Extending umklapp signatures from MoSe2 to WSe2 is incremental. Resolving a spectral feature tied to WC lattice vibrations, if the identification holds, would be new and of broad interest. Whether the paper's significance holds up depends almost entirely on how firmly the WP resonances are tied to the crystal.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The data are clean. The 2s/3s Rydberg states, RP, both trions and a weak umklapp branch are resolved in one device, and the RP-umklapp splitting is linear in density over roughly 0 to 7×10^11 cm^-2 (Fig. 2b).
- Two satellite resonances (WP_T, WP_S) above T- and S- blueshift with density while their parent APs stay nearly fixed (Figs. 1c, 2d). This is a new observation.
- Under circular pumping, the satellites follow the selection rules of their parent trions (Fig. 3b-d), while the RP umklapp is largely insensitive (Fig. 3d). This is a qualitative, testable prediction of the authors' theory (Ref. 34).
- Umklapp and WP respond differently to optical excitation (Fig. 4). This raises an interesting question about static versus local correlations in a partially melted crystal.
- The topic is timely: zero-field WCs in TMDs and their excitations.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The WP resonances are not shown to be specific to the Wigner crystal; in the figures they appear to persist above the melting density. The central claim is that WP_T and WP_S are APs dressed by WC phonons, so they "originate from WCs" (p. 4). By the paper's own criterion, the WC melts at n_c ≈ 7×10^11 cm^-2, where the umklapp disappears. Yet in Fig. 2d, WP_S remains visible up to a top-gate voltage of about 1.1-1.2 V. On the top axis this is roughly 8-9×10^11 cm^-2, and in Fig. 1c both WP branches are drawn to about 1.4 V. If the WP were a phonon sideband of the crystal, it should weaken or disappear across the same melting transition, or the paper should explain why it does not. The text (p. 6-7) invokes short-range correlations only for the optically melted case, not for density-driven melting. The polarization dependence (Fig. 3) and the blueshift with density do not uniquely fix the origin either. Any satellite or excited state of the triplet and singlet trions (for example a trion-plus-electron shake-up, a bound or excited trion complex, or an AP/RP-related feature in the Fermi liquid) would inherit the same valley selection rules and could also scale with density. The authors should:
   (i) plot the WP spectral weight and linewidth, extracted by lineshape fits, against density through n_c, on the same axis as the umklapp weight;
   (ii) measure the temperature dependence of WP and umklapp at fixed density through the thermal melting temperature (the Methods describe a heater, and Figs. S8-S9 are cited only for exciton energies) and show whether the WP disappears together with the umklapp;
   (iii) state what the theory of Ref. 34 predicts for the AP spectrum in the electron liquid at the same density, so the reader can see that a liquid would not produce a comparable satellite.
   Without (i)-(iii), the identification rests on a qualitative match.

2. The quantitative link between the WP-AP splitting and WC phonon frequencies is weak and omits gate screening. The paper calls the WP-AP splitting "a direct measure of the characteristic phonon frequency" (p. 4) and fits ΔE_WP = a n^{3/4} with one free parameter (Fig. 2d, a = 4.16 meV/(10^11 cm^-2)^{3/4}). Several problems remain:
   (i) The text concedes that the power law "cannot be unambiguously extracted" and that a linear fit also works (Fig. S5), because WP energies are unresolved at low density.
   (ii) The fitted prefactor is about 1.5 times the model value. The splitting at n_c is then about 18 meV, which is large compared with a simple unscreened estimate of the WC plasma/Debye scale, sqrt(e^2 ħ^2/(4πε0 ε m* a^3)). With m* = 0.4 m0, ε = 4.5 and lattice constant a ≈ 13 nm, that estimate is of order 5 meV at 7×10^11 cm^-2. The paper does not give the explicit model expression, prefactor, mass or dielectric constant in the main text, so the factor of 1.5 cannot be checked.
   (iii) The n^{3/4} law assumes unscreened 1/r interactions. In this device the hBN thicknesses are 20 nm and 31 nm, while the WC lattice constant runs from about 13 nm at n_c to more than 30 nm at 1×10^11 cm^-2. The graphite gates therefore screen the interaction at distances comparable to or shorter than the lattice spacing, especially at low density. This changes both the phonon spectrum and its density scaling.
   The authors should give the full model and parameter values, include gate screening for the actual geometry, and state which phonon energy (zone-boundary, Debye average, or a weighted spectral function) the WP peak should track. Ideally they should also compare devices with different hBN thicknesses, where gate screening predicts a measurably different density dependence. Until then, "a direct measure of the phonon frequency" and "good quantitative agreement" are overstated.

3. The optical-melting conclusion relies on a threshold criterion and an exciton-density estimate that are not secure. This claim is in the abstract and title-level framing.
   (a) The critical density in Fig. 4c is defined as the point where dR_C/dV crosses a fixed value (-0.005 to -0.0065; Methods). With increasing power the resonances broaden (stated for WP; Fig. S11). Any power-induced broadening or loss of oscillator strength of the umklapp line would lower a fixed-amplitude cutoff without any change in crystalline order. The authors should fit the umklapp spectral weight and linewidth at each power and density and define n* from the integrated weight extrapolated to zero, as was done for the density-driven case.
   (b) The exciton densities on the Fig. 4c axes (up to about 1.5×10^9 cm^-2 bright and 6×10^11 cm^-2 dark) cannot be reproduced from the Methods. Spot size, the lifetimes Γ used, the absorbance across the 650-800 nm band, and the spectral fraction of pump power absorbed are not stated. The formula used (peak power × lifetime) is valid only when the lifetime is short compared with the 200 ps pulse, which is not the case for dark excitons with ns lifetimes. In that regime the per-pulse density is bounded by the absorbed pulse energy. A rough estimate for 4000 nW at 40 MHz (0.1 pJ per pulse, about a 1 µm^2 spot, 10% absorbance) gives an absorbed photon density of order 10^12 cm^-2 per pulse, well above the quoted values.
   (c) The 650 nm edge of the filtered band (1.91 eV) lies at the quoted free-carrier gap (about 1.9 eV) and overlaps the 2s/3s states. Photoexcited free carriers and photodoping are therefore not excluded. The statement that RP/AP energies do not shift does not quantify the bound on the photodoped density.
   (d) Heating is ruled out only through the 1s lattice-temperature proxy (Fig. S10). The text acknowledges that the electron temperature may be higher, and an elevated electron temperature is the most direct alternative explanation for loss of crystalline order.
   (e) The same pulsed white light appears to serve as both pump and probe ("while simultaneously measuring their reflectance spectrum"). Changing the power therefore also changes the probe, so a nonlinear probe response cannot be separated from the effect on the crystal.
   The authors should give all parameters of the density estimate, use a separate weak probe, or at least compare against a pump of matched absorbed power below the exciton resonance. They should also compare the power-induced loss of umklapp with the thermally induced loss at equal umklapp weight, to test whether an effective electron temperature accounts for the data. Unless these are addressed, the paper should describe the data as power-induced suppression of the umklapp resonance, not exciton-driven melting.

B. Should address

4. The "optical control of WC spin" experiment does not isolate a WC-specific effect and does not quantify the polarization. Fig. 3 shows that under σ+ pumping, the co-polarized probe favors T-/WP_T and the cross-polarized probe favors S-/WP_S. This is the same valley selection rule that T- and S- obey in the electron liquid (Ref. 49 reports optically induced polarization in doped WSe2 without a WC). So the observation establishes that each WP shares the valley character of its parent AP. It does not show that the crystal plays a role. Other issues:
   - The degree of electron valley polarization is never extracted, for example from the T-/S- oscillator-strength ratio or a Kerr/Faraday measurement.
   - The comparison in Fig. 3d is between a 4 µW CW pump and no pump, so heating and photodoping are confounded with polarization. A linearly polarized pump of equal power is the right reference.
   - The phrase "valley-dependent Wigner polaron scattering well above the magnetic ordering temperature" implies a link to magnetic order that is not tested.
   The authors should quantify the valley polarization against density through n_c and use a linearly polarized pump control. If the effect does not change across n_c, the claim should be reframed as valley pumping of the electron system, probed by the WP.

5. The spin experiment and the melting experiment are inconsistent in pump conditions. Fig. 3 uses a 4 µW CW pump at 635 nm (1.95 eV, above the quoted free-carrier gap), and the umklapp branch remains clearly visible (Fig. 3b-d). Fig. 4 reports that a pulsed white-light pump of about 4 µW average power (4000 nW) nearly eliminates it. The authors should explain why comparable average powers have such different effects: peak intensity, photon energy, spot size, or a different device or spot. This comparison is itself a useful test of the heating versus exciton-density explanations in concern 3.

6. The exciton-mass extraction is circular, and its uncertainty is unclear. The text gives m_x ~ (0.85 ± 0.8) m0. An uncertainty of about 100% would make the agreement with 0.81 m0 meaningless; presumably ±0.08 is meant. The fit has three further weaknesses:
   (i) The slope of ΔE_U against n tests the density calibration as much as the mass, since the density has a stated uncertainty of about 10% from ε_hBN = 3.5 versus 3.1.
   (ii) The relevant mass is that of the RP quasiparticle, which can differ from the bare exciton mass at these densities.
   (iii) The linear fit in Fig. 2b passes through points at ΔE_U ≲ 2 meV, smaller than the RP linewidth, where the umklapp cannot be spectrally separated.
   The authors should correct the uncertainty, fit with and without the low-density points, propagate the density uncertainty, and state whether a free intercept is consistent with zero.

7. The r_s estimate and the "enhanced stability" claim rest on inconsistent dielectric constants and ignore gate screening. The density is computed with ε_hBN = 3.5 (with 3.1 as an alternative), but r_s ≈ 12 uses ε = 4.5 and m* = 0.4 m0, neither of which is justified. Because r_s scales with m*/ε and with n^{-1/2}, these choices and the 10% density uncertainty move r_s by several units. The comparison with clean-limit Monte Carlo values (r_s ≈ 31-38) also assumes unscreened Coulomb interactions in a strictly 2D system at T = 0. Here the gates sit 20-31 nm away, the WC lattice constant is about 13 nm at n_c, and the measurement is at 5 K. The authors should give r_s_c with an error band, state and justify ε (in-plane versus out-of-plane hBN) and m*, and discuss how gate screening and finite temperature change the expected critical r_s before attributing the difference to disorder or Berry curvature.

8. Sample statistics are unclear for the key new results. The text states that multiple devices and spots give a similar n_c from the umklapp (Fig. S1). It does not say how many devices show the WP resonances, the polarization response, or the optical melting, nor which device each main figure comes from. Since WP visibility is said to be limited by disorder, the device-to-device and spot-to-spot spread of the WP energies, of the prefactor a, and of the power-dependent n* should be reported. At least the WP observation and the Fig. 4c trend should be shown in a second device.

9. The WP identification needs quantitative lineshape analysis, not colour maps alone. All WP evidence is presented as derivative maps with a nonlinear colour scale (p = 0.6) plus a few line cuts. The text says the WP oscillator strength and linewidth are informative, but neither is shown. The authors should present raw R_C spectra with multi-resonance fits including the WP. They should also give the WP energy, width and integrated weight against density and pump power with error bars. This would also clarify the low-density regime where the n^{3/4} and linear fits differ.

C. Minor

10. Fig. 2b shows ΔE_U points up to about 8×10^11 cm^-2 with growing scatter, while the text says the umklapp is indiscernible above 7×10^11 cm^-2. Clarify which points were included in the fit and how n_c was defined.

11. Figure references are inconsistent. The main text cites Fig. S5 for a linear fit of the WP splitting, while the Methods cite Fig. S5 for the ΔE_U density dependence from three extraction methods. The main text cites Figs. S2-S4 for density calibration, while the Methods cite Fig. S12 for the doping onset.

12. Fig. 1a shows top and bottom gates, but the Methods state that "a single-gate scan is used". State the back-gate voltage held during the top-gate sweeps, whether the displacement field changes, and which hBN thickness enters the density conversion for each figure.

13. The theory on which the WP interpretation rests (Ref. 34) is an unpublished arXiv preprint by an overlapping author group, with no identifier given. Give the arXiv number. Make the Supplementary Discussion self-contained, with the model Hamiltonian, the parameters, and the predicted spectral weights for both the WC and the liquid.

14. The statement that the AP energies "remain nearly unchanged" with density deserves a number. In Figs. 1c and 2d the T- and S- lines shift slightly, and Fermi-polaron theory predicts a redshift. The WP-AP splitting depends on how the AP energy is tracked.

15. The Methods give the absorbance (10%) only "for example". The pump spot size and the lifetimes used for bright and dark excitons are not given. All parameters used for Fig. 4c should be listed.

16. Language and wording:
   - p. 6: "the Wigner polarons experience remain robust but experience".
   - p. 7: "To access the magnitude" should read "assess".
   - Fig. 1 caption: "donate" should read "denote".
   - Methods: "Therefor".
   - Ref. 7 is dated 2025 with an arXiv number given as "250910624" (missing period), and Refs. 46 and 55 are dated 2026. Check the reference formatting.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add one compact figure that tracks umklapp weight, WP weight and WP-AP splitting against both density and temperature through the melting transition. This is the most direct way to show that the WP is a property of the crystal.
2. Recast the phonon comparison with a gate-screened WC phonon model for the actual hBN thicknesses, showing the predicted WP-AP splitting with no free parameters alongside the data. If possible, add a device with different gate distances.
3. Separate pump and probe in the melting experiment (a weak probe, plus a pump tuned to the 1s resonance, below it, and above the gap), report n* from fitted spectral weights, and benchmark against thermal melting at equal umklapp suppression to bound the electron temperature.
4. Quantify the optically induced valley polarization against density across n_c, with a linearly polarized pump as the reference. This would turn Fig. 3 from a selection-rule demonstration into a measurement of how the WC changes the exciton-electron scattering.
