Review outline:

**1. Significance and novelty**

The manuscript reports STM/STS of the candidate excitonic insulator Ta2Pd3Te5. It finds pairs of in-gap states (E− in filled states, E+ in empty states) at one class of atomic defects, which DFT assigns to top-layer Ta vacancies. The states extend over about 5–6 nm, disappear where the gap closes (a strained stripe, or a close tip approach), and move as the apparent gap is tuned by tip height. A tight-binding model with a dipolar impurity potential gives two in-gap states. The authors interpret these as an excitonic analog of Yu-Shiba-Rusinov (YSR) states. If that identification held, it would be the first local probe of pair breaking in an exciton condensate, and the topic is timely given the debate over whether the gap in this family of materials is excitonic. As presented, though, the data and the model do not separate a pair-breaking (YSR-type) bound state from ordinary Coulomb-bound donor/acceptor-like impurity states in a narrow-gap insulator. That distinction is the paper's central claim.

**2. Potential reasons for acceptance**

- High-quality atomically resolved topography and spectroscopy. The in-gap pairs are clear and reproducible across five defect types (Fig. 2, Supplementary Figs. S4, S8, S9).
- Useful contrast between defects with in-gap states and defects without them (Supplementary Figs. S3, S7), backed by systematic DFT modeling of the defect topographies (Fig. 4a,b; Supplementary Figs. S6–S8, S12).
- Two independent ways of changing the gap locally (the strained stripe in Fig. 3a–d and tip-height tuning in Fig. 3e–g, Supplementary Figs. S10–S12). Applied to the same defects, this is a good experimental design in principle.
- The topic is important and the material is clean enough, with a high enough transition temperature, to be a good platform.

**3. Concerns**

**A. Must address**

1. **The model cannot tell an excitonic (pair-breaking) bound state from a Coulomb-bound impurity state in any band insulator.**
   - *What is wrong.* In Eq. (1) (Methods, p. 11) the excitonic coupling Δ is a fixed, spatially uniform constant (50 meV). It is not determined self-consistently in the presence of the impurity.
   - *Why it matters.* With a constant Δ, Eq. (1) is mathematically identical to a two-band semiconductor with a single-particle hybridization gap. The result in Fig. 4f is then simply what an electrostatic potential does in a gapped two-band system:
     - an attractive region binds a state below the conduction-band edge (donor-like);
     - a repulsive region binds a state above the valence-band edge (acceptor-like);
     - so a dipole, which has both, gives two in-gap states, while a monopole of one sign gives one (Fig. 4f, bottom).
     
     None of this needs an exciton condensate, and the model contains no local suppression of the order parameter, which is the defining ingredient of YSR physics ("locally break electron-hole pairing", p. 3; "pair breaking interactions", p. 9). The text on p. 7 states the direct analogy to magnetic impurities, but the calculation does not show it.
   - *What to do.*
     - Solve the model self-consistently for Δ(r) with an interaction term, and show that Δ is suppressed locally around the impurity.
     - Show which observable differs between this case and a fixed-hybridization band insulator with the same gap. Candidates: how the bound-state energy depends on Δ; the spatial structure of the wavefunction; the spectral-weight asymmetry; the dependence on the impurity's charge sign.
     - Reproduce the experimental observables with that model.
     
     Without this, Fig. 4f supports "charged defects bind in-gap states" but not "YSR states of an exciton condensate".

2. **The way the in-gap energies scale with the gap has not been analyzed to separate the competing pictures.**
   - *What is claimed.* The claim that the in-gap states "originate from the interaction of the defects with the excitonic order" (pp. 6–7) rests on the fact that E± move with the gap edges under tip-height tuning (Fig. 3e–g; Supplementary Fig. S12/S16).
   - *Why this does not discriminate.* That co-motion is also expected for hydrogenic or Coulomb-bound states whose binding energy is fixed relative to the band edge they split from. In Fig. 3g, E− barely moves, while the filled-state gap edge is pinned by VB2. E+ follows the conduction-band edge downward. This is what a constant binding energy below the CB edge would produce.
   - *What to do.*
     - Plot each defect's binding energies (E_CB − E+ and E− − E_VB) against the gap Δ_EI. For YSR-type states the energy should scale with Δ itself (E = Δ·f(coupling)), so the binding energy shrinks in proportion as Δ closes. For a hydrogenic state it set by the effective mass and dielectric constant, not by Δ.
     - Give these quantities with error bars, for all five defect types.
   
   Without this analysis, "solidly confirms the excitonic origin" (p. 7) is not supported.

3. **Tip-induced band bending, tip electric fields and defect charge-state effects are not addressed, yet they can produce paired, tip-height-dependent in-gap features at charged defects.**
   - *Background.* This is a narrow-gap system at 4.4 K, with defects the authors themselves say are charged (p. 7). In such a system, STS peaks at defects commonly arise from tip-induced ionization or charge-state switching, or from band bending shifting defect levels. These features move with tip height and setpoint, and can appear on both sides of E_F.
   - *Evidence in the paper that the tip field matters.*
     - Supplementary Fig. S14 attributes "panning out spectral features" to the tip's strong electric field (Wannier-Stark ladder).
     - The STS gap at normal tip height (~140 meV, p. 7) is larger than the ARPES gap (~100 meV, p. 4).
   - *What to do.*
     - Show the bias-setpoint dependence of the spectra at fixed current: changing setpoint bias at fixed current changes the field.
     - Look for ionization rings or sharp current-dependent features in dI/dV maps.
     - Compare spectra taken at constant height with those taken at constant current.
     - Report how the E± energies depend on setpoint.
     - Discuss quantitatively how large the band bending is expected to be, given the carrier density and screening length.
   
   This is also needed to interpret the gap-tuning experiment (see concern 4).

**B. Should address**

4. **The mechanism and calibration of the tip-height "carrier injection" tuning are unclear, and the "quantum contact" interpretation conflicts with the data.**
   - *The mechanism.* On p. 6 the gap reduction from 140 to 60 meV is attributed to "transient carrier doping induced by the tunneling current". This mechanism has been disputed for Ta2NiSe5 (ref. 55, cited by the authors). At tip–sample separations reduced by up to 0.5 nm, other possible causes are the electric field, mechanical pressure from the tip (which is itself strain, the authors' other tuning knob), and tip–sample hybridization.
   - *The contact claim.* Supplementary Fig. S15 says the current saturates at ~500 pA at ΔZ ≈ −0.3 nm and calls this "the quantum contact regime". A point contact with conductance near G0 would carry microamperes at these biases, not 500 pA. Saturation at 500 pA suggests a limit set by the instrument (preamplifier or feedback), or something else.
   - *Why it matters.* The abrupt collapse into the "metallic pseudogap" (Fig. 3e, ΔZ ≈ −0.25 nm) coincides with the jump in current. The physical meaning of the endpoint is therefore unclear.
   - *What to do.*
     - Give the bias and setpoint used for the I–Z curves.
     - Report the conductance in units of G0.
     - Test whether the gap reduction scales with current or with ΔZ: approach at different bias voltages, so that the same ΔZ gives a different current.
     - Check whether tip–sample contact changes the tip; compare topography before and after.

5. **The strained-region control does not discriminate between the two pictures, and the paper's own data partly contradict it.**
   - *Why it does not discriminate.* On p. 6, the absence of in-gap states at defects inside the strained stripe is taken to "clearly indicate" an excitonic origin. But once the gap collapses to a metallic pseudogap, any bound state, hydrogenic or YSR, will be screened and merge with the continuum. The observation is therefore a necessary condition, not a discriminating one.
   - *The contradiction.* Supplementary Fig. S16 states that for α′ an in-gap state appears within the pseudogap in the contact regime, which argues against the strained-region conclusion.
   - *Strain characterization.* The stripe's character is only loosely established. The "2.4%" (Supplementary Fig. S13) is an FFT peak-width ratio taken over the whole image, not a local strain map. It mixes finite-size and shear effects, and gives no sign of the strain.
   - *What to do.*
     - Provide a real-space strain map (for example Lawler–Fujita analysis) across the stripe.
     - Show defect spectra as a function of distance from the stripe, where the gap is only partly reduced. This would supply intermediate points for the analysis in concern 2.
     - Discuss the α′ observation in the main text.

6. **The defect identification and the "charged vs. neutral" argument rest on DFT that does not address the charge state.**
   - *The argument.* The key physical distinction drawn (Ta vacancy charged, Pd vacancy "non-charged", pp. 7–8; Supplementary Figs. S10, S13/S17) comes from charge-density differences and a rigid 0.3 eV PDOS shift in neutral supercell calculations.
   - *What is missing.* The charge state of a vacancy is set by its charge-transition levels relative to E_F. A neutral calculation does not determine it, and a Pd vacancy in this compound would generically also be electrically active. The supercell size, slab geometry (bulk vs. surface), and whether a dipole correction was used are not given.
   - *Why it matters.* The paper argues that "DFT does not predict the pairs of in-gap LDOS" and that the DFT defect states are under 1 nm (p. 6), and uses this as evidence for many-body states. That argument cannot be assessed without the supercell size: a cell of a few nm cannot host bound states 5 nm across. DFT also underestimates the gap (Supplementary Fig. S5), which would push shallow states into the continuum.
   - *Assignment of γ.* γ is assigned to a subsurface Ta vacancy without a simulation.
   - *What to do.*
     - Report supercell dimensions, the slab model and the vacuum.
     - Compute formation energies and charge-transition levels for Ta and Pd vacancies.
     - Simulate the subsurface Ta vacancy for γ.

7. **The spatial extent of the in-gap states is not quantified or compared with the relevant length scales.**
   - *What is claimed.* The "much wider extents, up to 6 nm" are taken to "immediately suggest" a many-body origin (p. 5).
   - *Why the comparison is needed.* A shallow Coulomb-bound state in a small-gap, high-dielectric-constant material also has a Bohr radius of several nm. The width σ = 2 nm of the model's Gaussian potential (p. 11) largely sets the extent of the model states.
   - *What to do.*
     - Extract decay lengths along and across the chains for E+ and E− from the grids in Fig. 2k–o, separately for each.
     - Compare them with the excitonic coherence length (ħv/Δ, using the Dirac velocity from ARPES) and with an effective-mass Bohr radius.
     - Look for oscillatory structure, for example Friedel-like oscillations at 2k_F of the parent bands, which would favor a condensate-related state.
   - *Setpoint effect.* The maps are taken at constant current over defects that protrude strongly in topography. The setpoint effect can create apparent halos. Constant-height maps, or maps normalized for the setpoint, would settle this.

8. **The magnetic-field test is not able to exclude the alternative it targets.**
   - The absence of splitting at 2.5 T (Supplementary Fig. S14/S18) is used to rule out topological defect states (p. 8).
   - A Zeeman energy of gμ_B·B ≈ 0.3 meV (for g = 2) is far below the ~10 meV linewidth of the in-gap peaks. The test therefore has no sensitivity unless g is very large.
   - The field data were also taken at 1.26 K rather than 4.4 K, which is mentioned only in the SI.
   - *What to do.* Either estimate the expected splitting and show it would be resolvable, or drop this argument.

9. **The statistics and reproducibility of the defect energies are not reported.**
   - The text says the five defect types have "marginally different energies" (p. 5). The values in Supplementary Fig. S9b differ from those in Fig. 2k–o by up to 4–5 meV:
     - α: −34 meV vs. −30 meV;
     - β′: −48 meV vs. −45 meV;
     - γ: −22 meV vs. −20 meV.
   - Not stated: how many defects of each type were measured; the spread in E±; how many samples, cleaves and tips were used; whether the energies depend on the local environment, such as defect density and distance to other defects.
   - *What to do.* Give histograms of E+ and E− for each type. This is needed both to establish robustness and to decide whether the four DFT-assigned vacancy sites are distinguishable spectroscopically.

10. **The excitonic gap is not defined consistently.**
    - The gap is quoted as ~100 meV (ARPES, p. 4), "about 140" meV (STS at normal height, p. 7), and 2Δ = 100 meV in the model.
    - The gap edges in Fig. 3e are marked "conservatively" by a turn-on criterion that is not specified.
    - *What to do.* State one operational definition of the gap edge, and use it for the scaling analysis (concern 2) and for Fig. 3g.

11. **The temperature dependence is not shown, although it would be the most direct link to the condensate.**
    - All spectroscopy was done at 4.4 K, plus 1.26 K for the field data.
    - The transition is at ~365 K, so the gap varies substantially over a temperature range accessible to variable-temperature STM (for example 77–300 K).
    - *What to do.* Track E± versus T(Δ(T)) on the same defect type. This would test whether the bound states follow the order parameter without the tip-field complications of concern 3. If this is beyond scope, the claims should be narrowed accordingly.

12. **Several claims go beyond the evidence.**
    - "establish both the YSR physics in exciton condensates" (abstract);
    - "solidly confirms the excitonic origin" (p. 7);
    - "the presence of the interaction-driven impurity-bound states in turn manifests the excitonic nature of the present insulating phase" (p. 9), which is circular, because the excitonic nature is assumed in order to interpret the states;
    - "persisting up to room temperature" (abstract), although no measurement was made above 4.4 K.
    
    These should be reworded unless concerns 1–3 are resolved. The structural, phonon-driven or topological contributions to the gap in Ta2Pd3Te5 (refs. 49, 60, 61) should also be acknowledged as alternatives for the parent gap itself.

**C. Minor**

13. **Dipole moment values.** The dipole moment p_z = −0.45 × 10⁻¹² C·m (p. 7; Supplementary Fig. S17) is unphysical for an atomic defect: 1 e·Å ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻²⁹ C·m. Correct the units or the value, and state how the moment was computed (integration volume, reference) and in which crystal axis (p_z here vs. "along the y-axis" in the Fig. 4e caption, and "perpendicular to the chain direction" in the text).

14. **Model details.**
    - Give the numerical V0 values used in Fig. 4f (the legend shows 0–0.24 eV and 0–0.10 eV without explanation).
    - State how the 2D isotropic square-lattice parameters (t = 0.5 eV, μ = −1.5 eV) were obtained "from experimental and DFT parameters" for a quasi-1D chain compound.
    - Comment on finite-size effects: a 41 × 41 lattice with a = 0.25 nm is ~10 nm across, which is comparable to the bound-state extent.

15. **STS parameters.** Lock-in modulation amplitude and frequency, setpoint bias and current for each figure, and the normalization used for "Normalized dI/dV" are not given.

16. **Figure and text inconsistencies.**
    - The Fig. 2 caption says "approximately 5 nm"; the text says "up to 6 nm".
    - The Supplementary Fig. S13 caption refers to "Fig. 4a"; this should be Fig. 3a.
    - Fig. 3g: the line style and legend are unclear (dashed vs. dotted), and the x-axis direction (ΔZ decreasing to the right) should be stated.
    - The Fig. 1d caption calls them "ARPES intensity maps", but only guide lines are clearly visible; show the raw intensity.

17. **References.**
    - Lattice constants are "taken from experiments 61" (p. 11), but ref. 61 is an edge-state STM paper; presumably ref. 46 is meant.
    - Refs. 29 and 31 are duplicates.
    - The theoretical prediction of impurity states in excitonic insulators is attributed to "the YSR theory" (p. 3). The relevant work is Zittartz's theory of excitonic insulators with normal impurities (1967–68), which should be cited and compared with explicitly, rather than refs. 22–25 alone.

18. **The excitonic YSR analogy needs to be stated precisely.**
    - In superconductors, YSR states appear at ±E as particle–hole partners of a single BdG state.
    - Here E+ and E− are not symmetric (Fig. S9b) and, in the authors' model, arise from two spatially distinct potential lobes.
    - Clarify whether E+ and E− are one YSR pair or two separate bound states. The spatial maps (Fig. 2k–o) suggest different shapes for the two, which favors the latter.

**4. Suggestions for improvement**

1. Make the scaling of the binding energy with gap (concern 2) the central figure. Use all gap-tuning data, intermediate points near the strained stripe, and ideally variable-temperature data, and set it against predictions from a self-consistent excitonic model and from a fixed-hybridization band-insulator model with identical parameters.
2. Add a set of measurements that controls for tip-induced band bending: setpoint dependence, constant-height spectroscopy, and searches for ionization rings. Also add a quantitative calibration of what the tip-height approach actually changes (current, field, contact).
3. Report decay lengths and spatial structure of E+ and E− from the grid data and compare them quantitatively with the coherence length and the effective-mass Bohr radius.
4. Strengthen the DFT with defect charge-transition levels, supercell sizes large enough to hold extended states, and a simulation for the γ defect. Then tone down the YSR and room-temperature language to match what is established.
