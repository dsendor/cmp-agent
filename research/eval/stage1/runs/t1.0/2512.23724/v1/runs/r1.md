Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports STM/STS of pairs of in-gap states around specific point defects in the proposed excitonic insulator Ta2Pd3Te5. It assigns the defects to top-layer Ta vacancies carrying a charge dipole and interprets the states as the excitonic analogue of Yu-Shiba-Rusinov (YSR) bound states. If the assignment held, it would be the first local observation of pair-breaking impurity states in an exciton condensate, and a new local probe of the excitonic order in a material where the excitonic character of the gap is still debated. The topic is timely and the data are of good quality. However, the central step from "defect-bound in-gap states that disappear when the gap closes" to "YSR states of an exciton condensate" does not yet follow from the evidence presented.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- High-quality atomically resolved topography and spectroscopy. Five distinct defect types each show a reproducible pair of sharp in-gap resonances (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S5/table in SI Fig. 9).
- A clear contrast exists between "non-trivial" defects with in-gap states and "trivial" defects without them in the same field of view (SI Figs. 6, 7), which is a useful internal reference.
- The tip-height dependence (Fig. 3e–g, SI Fig. 16) is measured on all five defect types and on a pristine area, so in-gap energies can be compared with local gap edges under the same conditions.
- The combination of DFT defect modelling and a minimal impurity model is a reasonable framework that could become convincing with the additions below.
- The idea that charged impurities act as pair breakers in an excitonic insulator is physically well motivated and would interest a broad audience.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The evidence does not tell an excitonic YSR state apart from an ordinary band-edge impurity state in a narrow-gap semiconductor. The paper offers three arguments for an excitonic origin. (i) The states extend 5–6 nm, much farther than atomic size (p. 5). (ii) They disappear where the gap collapses, in the strained stripe and at small tip height (Fig. 3d, 3e). (iii) They move with the gap edges (Fig. 3g). Each of these is also expected for a hydrogenic or short-range impurity state split off a band edge in any semiconductor with a ~100 meV gap and light Dirac-like carriers.
   - Such states have nanometre-scale decay lengths (κ⁻¹ = ħ/√(2m*E_b)).
   - They must merge into the continuum when the gap closes.
   - They follow the band edges when the gap changes.
   - In particular, a dipole potential has an attractive region that binds a state below the conduction-band edge and a repulsive region that binds one above the valence-band edge. That is the qualitative result of Fig. 4f, top, without any many-body ingredient.

   The model of Eq. (1) does not discriminate either. Δ is a fixed, uniform hybridization, not determined self-consistently, so Eq. (1) is mathematically a non-interacting hybridized band insulator. The defining YSR ingredient, local suppression of the pair amplitude by a pair-breaking perturbation, is absent.

   The experimental pair is also not a particle-hole pair in the YSR sense:
   - E⁺ ≠ |E⁻| for every defect (SI Fig. 9 table).
   - The E⁺ and E⁻ maps have different shapes (Fig. 2k–o).
   - In Fig. 3g, E⁻ stays almost fixed while E⁺ follows the conduction-band edge.

   This behaviour looks like two independent states, each bound to its own band edge.

   The authors should:
   - (a) solve the impurity problem self-consistently, with Δ_i determined from an interband interaction, and show which observable differs from the same calculation with a fixed non-interacting gap of the same size. Candidates are the ratio E±/Δ, the decay length relative to ħv/Δ, and coherence between E⁺ and E⁻.
   - (b) measure that observable.
   - (c) plot E⁺ and E⁻ against the locally measured gap rather than against ΔZ, and compare with both scenarios.
   - (d) report the measured decay lengths along and across the chains and compare them with the excitonic coherence length estimated from the ARPES velocity and the gap.

   Without such a discriminating test the title and abstract claim is not supported.

2. The tip-height "gap tuning" (Fig. 3e–g, SI Figs. 14–16) is central to the claim, but its mechanism and possible artifacts are not addressed.
   - SI Fig. 15 shows abrupt steps in I(Z) near ΔZ ≈ −0.25 to −0.3 nm for several defects. These look like mechanical rearrangement of the tip or surface, not smooth tuning.
   - The saturation current of ~500 pA is called the "quantum contact regime". At any realistic bias, 500 pA is orders of magnitude below G₀V, so this label is not justified. The bias used for I(Z) is not stated.
   - The paper offers no quantitative argument that the gap shrinks because of "transient carrier doping". Tip-induced band bending, the strong tip field (the caption of SI Fig. 14 itself invokes Wannier–Stark effects), voltage division in a semiconducting sample, and tip-induced local pressure could all shift and smear the apparent band edges.
   - The authors cite a published Comment (Ref. 55) disputing tip-induced gap collapse in Ta2NiSe5 but do not engage with its arguments.

   The authors should:
   - show that the gap reduction is reversible on retraction and reproducible with different tips;
   - show its dependence on the bias setpoint as well as on height;
   - estimate tip-induced band bending for this gap and carrier density;
   - state the setpoint that defines ΔZ = 0.

   If the tuning cannot be tied to a change in the excitonic order parameter, the "systematic tuning" argument for an excitonic origin does not hold.

3. The charge-dipole mechanism rests on DFT quantities that are inconsistent or under-specified.
   - The dipole moment p_z = −0.45 × 10⁻¹² C·m (p. 7, SI Fig. 17) is about 10¹⁶ e·Å. Either the units are wrong or the quantity is not a dipole moment.
   - The dipole is labelled p_z, but the text says it points perpendicular to the chains and the Fig. 4e caption says it lies along y.
   - The ~0.3 eV rigid shift of the Te PDOS near a Ta vacancy (SI Fig. 10f) is used as evidence of local charge. Removing a Ta atom from a finite supercell also shifts the Fermi level (hole doping), so the shift relative to E_F may be a doping effect.
   - The Pd vacancy is called "non-charged", yet removing Pd also changes the electron count. Only its dipole is reported (SI Fig. 17), not its monopole charge.
   - This matters for the logic. The model in Fig. 4f predicts that a monopole gives filled-state in-gap states. Charged Pd or Te vacancies should therefore show such states, but the trivial defects show none (SI Fig. 7c).

   The authors should:
   - give the supercell or slab size, vacuum, and the charge state of each vacancy;
   - give how the charge density difference was defined, including atomic relaxation of the reference;
   - report monopole and dipole moments in consistent units for Ta, Pd and Te vacancies;
   - reconcile the absence of in-gap states at the trivial defects with the monopole result.

B. Should address

4. The assignment of the non-trivial defects to top-layer Ta vacancies is qualitative.
   - It relies on visual agreement of topographs (Fig. 4a,b, SI Fig. 12). The caption of SI Fig. 12 concedes that some contrast differs, and its sentence is unfinished.
   - Only Te, Pd and Ta vacancies were considered. Antisites (e.g. Pd_Ta, Te_Ta), interstitials or intercalated atoms, and extrinsic impurities were not.
   - The mapping of α, α′, β, β′ onto sites (1)–(4) is not justified beyond mirror symmetry.
   - γ is assigned to a subsurface Ta vacancy without simulation.
   - No formation energies are given to explain why Ta vacancies would be about as common as Pd vacancies (SI Fig. 6).

   The authors should:
   - include antisite and interstitial candidates;
   - give formation energies;
   - give a quantitative image-comparison metric for each assignment;
   - simulate the subsurface vacancy for γ.

5. The strained-stripe control (Fig. 3a–d, SI Fig. 13) is weaker than stated.
   - The strain value of 2.4% comes from the FWHM of an FFT Bragg peak divided by Q_B. That width includes finite-window resolution and inhomogeneous broadening, so it is not a strain measure. The same caption gives a different "local shearing of about 1%".
   - Other explanations for the bright stripe and its gapless spectrum are not excluded: a subsurface dislocation, a stacking fault or domain wall, or a local band overlap (strain-induced semimetal) with no excitonic fluctuations.
   - Calling the 30 meV feature a pseudogap of "preformed excitons" is unsupported.
   - Any bound state disappears into a metallic continuum, so the absence of in-gap states inside the stripe does not single out an excitonic origin.
   - SI Fig. 16 notes that α′ keeps an in-gap state inside the pseudogap at small tip height. This contradicts the statement that in-gap states vanish in the pseudogap phase and is not discussed in the main text.

   The authors should obtain a real-space strain map (e.g. Lawler–Fujita analysis) and temper the pseudogap interpretation. They should also discuss the α′ case in the main text.

6. The model calculation is not set up for quantitative comparison.
   - The 41 × 41 lattice with a = 0.25 nm spans only 10 nm, comparable to twice the 5 nm extent of the states, so finite-size quantization may affect the "in-gap" levels.
   - The Gaussian width σ = 2 nm exceeds the dipole separation d = 1 nm, so the dipole nearly cancels and the result may depend strongly on these unconstrained choices.
   - The lattice is isotropic and 2D, whereas the material is quasi-1D.
   - The model omits VB2 and is electron–hole symmetric, so the observed asymmetry is explained only by assertion.
   - The text says parameters come from "experimental and DFT parameters", but it is unclear how t = 0.5 eV, μ = −1.5 eV and Δ = 50 meV relate to the ARPES dispersions.
   - Fig. 4f varies V₀ at fixed Δ, while the experiment varies Δ at fixed defect.

   The authors should:
   - show convergence with system size;
   - show sensitivity to σ and d;
   - add VB2 or anisotropic hopping;
   - compute E± versus Δ at fixed V₀ and overlay Fig. 3g.

7. The magnetic-field test (p. 8, SI Fig. 18) cannot rule out the topological-defect alternative. At 2.5 T a g = 2 Zeeman splitting is ~0.3 meV, far below the ~10 meV width of the in-gap peaks. The lock-in modulation amplitude is also not given. The absence of visible splitting is therefore uninformative. The authors should state this, give the energy resolution, and either use larger fields and narrower features or remove the argument.

8. Reproducibility and statistics are not reported.
   - Methods do not say how many crystals, cleaves and tips were used.
   - They do not say how many defects of each type were measured, or what the spread of E± is within a type.
   - The table in SI Fig. 9 gives single values without uncertainties. These differ by a few meV from the energies labelled in Fig. 2k–o (e.g. α: 56/−34 vs 55/−30 meV; γ: 36/−22 vs 35/−20 meV).
   - The STS setpoint, lock-in modulation and the normalization of "normalized dI/dV" are not stated. The normalization is critical when tip height is varied (Fig. 3e, SI Fig. 16).

   The authors should add these details and give E± with standard deviations over multiple defects of each type, ideally from at least two cleaves.

9. The abstract claims a microscopic tool for exciton condensates "persisting up to room temperature", but all STS is at 4.4 K (and 1.26 K for SI Fig. 18). A YSR state bound to the excitonic order should follow Δ(T). Even a moderate temperature series (e.g. 4–80 K, matching the ARPES temperature) would directly test the link between E± and the order parameter, independent of the tip-height method. The authors should add such data or remove the room-temperature claim.

C. Minor

10. The gap size and transition temperature are quoted inconsistently.
    - The transition is given as 365 K and 370 K (p. 4; Fig. 1d is labelled 370 K).
    - The gap is "about 100 meV" from ARPES and STS (p. 4), but the STS apparent gap at normal height is ~140 meV (p. 7).

    The authors should explain why the gap differs between ARPES and STS, and how the gap edges are defined in STS.

11. The EDX table (SI Fig. 19c) lists weight percentages of 109.50, 97.82 and 191.02, which do not sum to 100. The atomic percentages may be correct, but the table needs correcting. EDX also cannot resolve vacancy concentrations at the level relevant here, so the claim of a stoichiometric crystal should not be used to bound the Ta vacancy density.

12. Figure and reference issues:
    - The caption of SI Fig. 13 refers to "Fig. 4a" instead of Fig. 3a, and its FFT line cuts have the y-axis labelled "z (nm)".
    - Fig. 3 has no title.
    - The axes in the Fig. 4e caption ("y (b)", "x-axis") do not match the panel labels.
    - The SI figures are numbered 5–20, whereas the text cites Supplementary Figs. S1–S16.
    - Refs. 29 and 31 are duplicates.
    - Methods cite Ref. 61 (a topological edge-state paper) for the lattice constants instead of Ref. 46.

13. The spatial extent of the in-gap states is given as "up to 6 nm" (p. 5) and "approximately 5 nm" (Fig. 2 caption). The authors should give a fitted decay length with uncertainty along each crystal axis.

14. The DFT Methods give only bulk k-mesh and cutoff. They should state the slab thickness, supercell size for the defect calculations, k-mesh used for defects, and whether spin–orbit coupling and spin polarization were included in the defect runs. They should also explain how STM images were simulated (Tersoff–Hamann energy window, height).

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Design a discriminating test between excitonic YSR states and conventional band-edge impurity states. Examples are a self-consistent impurity calculation predicting a distinctive E±/Δ scaling or local suppression of the gap around the defect, compared directly with E± versus the locally measured gap. A temperature-dependent STS series tracking E± as Δ(T) changes would make the case far stronger than tip-height tuning alone.
2. Map the local gap around each non-trivial defect with spatially resolved STS. A YSR-type pair-breaking defect should locally suppress the excitonic gap edges over the coherence length, which a simple potential scatterer in a band insulator would not.
3. Put the DFT charge analysis on a quantitative footing: consistent units, charge states, monopole plus dipole for all vacancy types. Then use those values, not free Gaussian parameters, as input to the impurity model.
4. Strengthen the tip-tuning experiment with reversibility, multiple tips, setpoint dependence and an estimate of tip-induced band bending. Discuss the Ref. 55 criticism explicitly.
