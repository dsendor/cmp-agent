Review outline:

**1. Significance and novelty**

The manuscript reports low-temperature (4.4 K) STM/STS of the candidate room-temperature excitonic insulator Ta2Pd3Te5. It finds pairs of in-gap resonances (E− in filled states, E+ in empty states) that extend over 5–6 nm around a class of defects assigned by DFT to top-layer Ta vacancies. The resonances vanish where the gap collapses into a ~30 meV pseudogap, either in a strained stripe or at small tip–sample distance. The authors interpret them as the long-predicted excitonic analogue of Yu-Shiba-Rusinov (YSR) states, driven by a charge dipole, and support this with a tight-binding model. If the excitonic pair-breaking origin were established, this would be a first and a useful local probe of excitonic order. As presented, however, the data show defect-bound in-gap states whose energies follow the local gap. That behaviour is expected for ordinary impurity states in any gapped semiconductor, so the evidence does not yet single out the YSR mechanism.

**2. Potential reasons for acceptance**

- The data quality is high. The atomically resolved topography matches DFT simulations (Fig. 1b,c). Defect-type-resolved STS and grid maps of both in-gap states are shown for five defect types (Fig. 2, Fig. S5 table).
- There is a useful internal contrast between defects with in-gap states (assigned to Ta vacancies) and defects without them (assigned to Pd vacancies, Fig. S3/S7).
- Two independent knobs reduce the gap, local strain (Fig. 3a–d) and tip height (Fig. 3e–g, Fig. S12). Both are applied to the same defect types, and the in-gap states track the gap under both.
- The topic is timely and the claim, if substantiated, would be of broad interest.

**3. Concerns**

**A. Must address**

1. **The data do not distinguish excitonic YSR states from ordinary shallow defect states of a gapped semiconductor.** The central inference (pp. 6–7, Fig. 3) is that the in-gap states "originate from the interaction of the defects with the excitonic order" because they shift with the gap and disappear when the gap closes. But any donor- or acceptor-like level of a charged defect in a band insulator also sits inside the gap, follows the band edges when the gap shrinks, and merges into the continuum when the gap closes. The same holds for the 5–6 nm spatial extent (p. 5). With a ~100 meV gap, light Dirac-like carriers and a large dielectric constant, a hydrogenic bound state would have an effective Bohr radius of several nanometres. A large extent is therefore not evidence of "many-body interaction".
   - The paper's own model (Eq. 1) makes the point. With a fixed, non-self-consistent Δ, Eq. 1 is exactly a hybridization-gap band insulator. Its two bound states for a dipole (Fig. 4f) are what a positive and a negative Gaussian potential each bind in any gapped two-band model. Nothing in the calculation uses the fact that Δ comes from an excitonic condensate.
   - What the authors should do:
     - Solve the impurity problem with Δ_i determined self-consistently (mean-field excitonic gap equation with a local Coulomb perturbation). Show what the excitonic mechanism predicts that a rigid band gap does not: local suppression of Δ near the defect, a specific relation between E± and the local gap, or a characteristic spatial structure of the electron-like and hole-like weights.
     - Test that prediction in the data: map the local gap edge as a function of distance from the defect, and compare E+ and E− maps against the predicted electron/hole components.
     - Without such a discriminating signature, the title, abstract ("establish the YSR physics in exciton condensates") and conclusions need to be reworded to "defect-bound in-gap states in an excitonic insulator candidate".

2. **The tip-height "carrier injection" experiment is not shown to tune the excitonic order parameter, and the I–Z data suggest a mechanical or contact artifact.** Figs. 3e–g, S10–S12 and S11 (I–Z) are the main quantitative evidence that the in-gap energies scale with the gap. Several points need resolution:
   - **No measurement of the doping.** The mechanism, "transient carrier doping induced by the tunneling current" (p. 6), is asserted, not demonstrated. No injected carrier density is estimated, and the relevant literature on Ta2NiSe5 is contested (refs. 54, 55, the latter a Comment disputing the interpretation).
   - **Tip-induced band bending is not separated out.** In a ~100 meV-gap semiconductor at 4.4 K, the measured apparent gap of ~140 meV at ΔZ = 0 (p. 7) is already larger than the ARPES gap of 100 meV. Reducing the tip height changes the voltage division and the tip field, which rescales and shifts both the band edges and any defect level. That would reproduce Fig. 3g without any change of the order parameter.
   - **The I–Z curves do not look like point contact.** In Fig. S11 the current "saturated to a consistent value of about 500 pA", which the authors call "the quantum contact regime". A point contact at ~G0 and tens of mV bias would carry microamperes, not 500 pA. The abrupt steps at ΔZ ≈ −0.25 to −0.3 nm coincide with the "abrupt" collapse to the pseudogap (Fig. 3e, Fig. S12a, where the colour maps are discontinuous at the same ΔZ). This points to a change in the tip or junction (tip–sample mechanical contact, a picked-up adsorbate, local pressure) rather than an intrinsic doping-driven transition.
   - What the authors should report:
     - the bias setpoint and current for each ΔZ, and whether the spectra are reversible on retracting the tip;
     - whether the same evolution appears when the current is raised at fixed height by changing the setpoint bias (doping and field effects scale differently);
     - an estimate of tip-induced band bending, for example from setpoint-dependent spectra;
     - an explanation of the 500 pA saturation.

3. **The DFT-based argument that the states are "many-body" is undermined by the DFT band gap.** The text argues (p. 6) that "the DFT calculations do not predict the pairs of in-gap LDOS for any of these defects" and that DFT defect states are <1 nm in extent. Yet Fig. S1 states that the DFT gap "is much smaller than the experimental excitonic gap" (essentially a semimetal in the monolayer). A calculation with almost no gap cannot produce an in-gap bound state with a 5–6 nm Bohr radius, so its absence is not evidence for an excitonic origin. In addition, the defect supercell size is not given. A supercell of a few nanometres would itself truncate any extended state.
   - What the authors should do:
     - Report the supercell dimensions and k-mesh used for the defect calculations.
     - Redo the defect calculation with a method that opens a gap of the right size (hybrid functional, DFT+U on Ta/Pd d states, or a scissor-corrected tight-binding model fitted to ARPES). Show whether a non-excitonic gap of ~100 meV already binds shallow states around a charged Ta vacancy.
     - Otherwise, remove the "DFT does not predict in-gap states, hence many-body" argument.

4. **The charge state and monopole of the Ta vacancy, which decide the model, are not established.** A Ta vacancy removes a cation and would ordinarily act as an acceptor carrying a net charge. The text reports only a dipole p_z = −0.45 × 10⁻¹² C·m (Fig. 4d,e; Fig. S13). It then chooses a "simple symmetric dipole" over a monopole because the monopole gives only a filled-state bound state (Fig. 4f). Problems:
   - The net charge of the relaxed Ta-vacancy supercell (Bader or similar) is not given, nor is its dependence on the Fermi level and charge state.
   - The dipole is computed in a charge-neutral supercell. In the real crystal at 4.4 K the vacancy can be ionized.
   - The dipole model itself is defined unclearly. Two Gaussians of width σ = 2 nm separated by d = 1 nm (Eq. 2) give a weak, nearly cancelling potential, and it is unclear how V0 (0–0.24 eV in Fig. 4f) relates to the DFT dipole.
   - The direction is also unclear. The text says p_z, "aligned perpendicular to the chain direction", while the caption of Fig. 4e says "dipole formation along the y-axis", with y identified as b.
   - What the authors should do: report the vacancy's net charge and charge-transition levels, map the DFT dipole onto V0 and d quantitatively, and show that the two-bound-state result survives a dipole plus monopole potential. This matters because the paper's discrimination between Ta and Pd vacancies, and between the one-state and two-state outcomes, rests entirely on this choice.

**B. Should address**

5. **The strained-stripe control is circular as a test of mechanism.** Fig. 3a–d shows that defects inside the stripe, where the gap has collapsed to a pseudogap, show no in-gap state. The text says this "clearly indicates" an excitonic origin (p. 6). Any in-gap state disappears when there is no gap, so the observation is necessary but not discriminating.
   - The defect identity inside the stripe also rests only on topography (Fig. 3b), and topographic contrast is itself modified by strain.
   - The strain value is loosely determined. ε ≈ 2.4% is inferred from Bragg-peak FWHM broadening (Fig. S9f), while the same figure quotes a shear of "about 1%" from a 0.57° angle. FWHM broadening reflects strain variation and finite size, not a uniform strain.
   - What the authors should do:
     - Show defects at intermediate positions across the stripe edge, where the gap is partly reduced, and plot E± against the local gap at each defect. That would give a strain-tuned version of Fig. 3g that is independent of the tip.
     - State the strain estimate as a range, with its method.

6. **The analogy to YSR physics in the energy structure is not examined.** In superconductors a YSR state appears as a particle–hole pair at ±E about the chemical potential. Here the two states sit at unrelated energies (e.g. α: +56/−34 meV; β: +70/−46 meV; Fig. S5b). E+ moves strongly with ΔZ while E− barely moves (Fig. 3g). The authors attribute the asymmetry to VB2 (p. 8), but this is not modelled. Including VB2 and the asymmetric bands in Eq. 1 would test whether the dipole model reproduces the observed E+/E− and their different ΔZ dependence. Without that test, the two-peak structure may simply reflect two independent levels (one donor-like, one acceptor-like).

7. **Statistics and reproducibility are not reported.** The paper does not state:
   - how many defects of each type were measured;
   - how many cleaves, samples and tips were used;
   - the spread of E± within one defect type.
   
   Fig. S5b gives single values without uncertainty. The ΔZ sweeps (Fig. 3g, Fig. S12) appear to be one sweep per defect type, drawn as dashed lines without error bars, and the axis is ΔZ rather than the "gap edge positions" stated in the caption. The authors should give the number of defects per type, E± as mean ± standard deviation, and E± plotted against the measured local gap (not ΔZ) with error bars.

8. **The normalization and setpoint conditions of the spectra are unspecified.** Nearly all spectra are "Normalized dI/dV" (Figs. 2f–j, 3d–f, S3, S12, S14). It is not stated whether this is (dI/dV)/(I/V), division by a background, or something else. (dI/dV)/(I/V) is ill-defined near the gap edges of an insulator and can create or sharpen peaks there. The setpoint bias and current, lock-in modulation amplitude and energy resolution are also not given. Because the in-gap peaks sit close to the band edges, the authors should show raw dI/dV for representative spectra and state the setpoint and modulation.

9. **The magnetic-field test cannot discriminate.** At 2.5 T, Zeeman splitting for g ≈ 2 is ~0.3 meV. The in-gap peaks in Fig. S14 are ~10–20 meV wide, so the absence of a visible splitting (p. 8) does not rule out a spin-degenerate topological defect state. Either quote the expected splitting against the linewidth and resolution, or remove the claim that the field data exclude the quantum spin Hall defect-state scenario.

10. **The Ta-vacancy identification is partly qualitative.** Fig. S8 says the agreement is "reasonable", and its caption sentence is incomplete ("while some contrast as guided by red circles…"). For β and β′ the simulated occupied-state images differ visibly from experiment. γ is assigned to "the other defects in the layer underneath" without any simulation (p. 6). The defect density (Fig. S2) is also not compared with the Ta deficiency expected from the off-stoichiometric flux growth (2.0:4.5:7.5) or measured by EDX (Fig. S16). The authors should simulate the subsurface Ta vacancy for γ, quantify the image agreement (for example by cross-correlation), and compare the observed defect density with the stoichiometry.

11. **The pseudogap and "preformed exciton" interpretation is asserted.** The ~30 meV pseudogap in the strained region and at small ΔZ is called evidence of "strong excitonic fluctuation, the preformed excitons" (p. 6). A soft gap at 4.4 K in a disordered or strained metallic region can also come from Altshuler–Aronov/Coulomb-gap physics or from tip-induced effects. Fig. S12 even shows an in-gap state of α′ inside the pseudogap "whose origin is not understood yet". Either support the preformed-exciton reading (for example with temperature dependence) or present it as speculation.

12. **The room-temperature claim and the excitonic nature of the gap are assumed.** The abstract ends with "persisting up to room temperature", but all STS is at 4.4 K (and 1.26 K in Fig. S14). No in-gap states are shown at elevated temperature. More basically, whether the gap in Ta2Pd3Te5 is excitonic rather than structural or phonon-assisted is still debated (refs. 42, 43, 49). The present argument partly assumes it and partly claims to confirm it ("manifests the excitonic nature", p. 9), which risks circularity.
   - What the authors should do: STS at intermediate temperatures (for example 77 K and above) showing E± tracking the temperature-dependent gap would strengthen the case considerably. The text should not claim both premises.

**C. Minor**

13. **Mismatched energies between Fig. 2 and Fig. S5b.** The map energies in Fig. 2k–o do not match the table in Fig. S5b: α −30/55 vs −34/56; β′ −45/35 vs −48/34; γ −20/35 vs −22/36. Please reconcile.
14. **Spatial extent of the states.** The text says "up to 6 nm" (p. 5), while the Fig. 2 caption says "approximately 5 nm". Give a decay length extracted from a fit to the line profiles.
15. **Transition temperature.** The transition is quoted as 365 K (p. 4, twice) and 370 K (p. 4, Fig. 1d). Please be consistent.
16. **Figure cross-reference.** The Fig. S9 caption refers to "same as in Fig. 4a"; this should be Fig. 3a.
17. **Lattice-constant reference.** The Methods cite ref. 61 (an edge-state paper) for the lattice constants; ref. 46 appears to be intended. Refs. 29 and 31 are duplicates.
18. **EDX table.** The weight-percent column in Fig. S16c sums to ~398%.
19. **Coordinate labels in Fig. 4e.** The axes are labelled y (Å) and z (Å) with Δρ_xz and Δρ_xy, while the text uses p_z and the caption says y = b. Define one coordinate system relative to the crystal axes a, b, c.
20. **Model parameters.** Eq. 1 uses a square lattice with a = 0.25 nm and t = 0.5 eV, which does not resemble the quasi-1D anisotropic band structure of Ta2Pd3Te5. State how the parameters were chosen "with the experimental and DFT parameters as input", and comment on whether the anisotropy matters.
21. **Supplementary figure numbering.** The supplementary figures are numbered 5–20 in the compiled file, but the text cites them as S1–S16.

**4. Suggestions for improvement**

1. Add a self-consistent excitonic impurity calculation alongside a rigid-band-gap control with the same gap and potential. Identify a measurable difference, such as local suppression of the gap around the defect or the ratio E±/Δ, and test it with spatial gap maps around each defect type.
2. Separate tip-induced band bending and junction changes from intrinsic gap tuning:
   - setpoint-bias versus setpoint-current series;
   - reversibility on retracting the tip;
   - an electrostatic estimate of the potential drop in the sample;
   - an explanation of the 500 pA I–Z saturation and the step at ΔZ ≈ −0.27 nm.
3. Replace ΔZ in Fig. 3g with the measured local gap, add error bars from several defects per type, and add the strain-gradient data (concern 5) as an independent tuning axis on the same plot.
4. Bring the title, abstract and conclusions in line with the evidence. "Defect-bound in-gap states tied to the excitonic gap" is supported; "establish YSR physics" and "up to room temperature" require the additional evidence above.
