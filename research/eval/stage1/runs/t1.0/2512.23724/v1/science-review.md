## A. Must address

1. **The data and the model do not tell an excitonic YSR (pair-breaking) state apart from an ordinary Coulomb-bound donor/acceptor-like impurity state in a narrow-gap semiconductor.** The paper gives three arguments for an excitonic origin: (i) the states extend 5–6 nm, much farther than atomic size (p. 5); (ii) they disappear where the gap collapses, in the strained stripe and at small tip height (Fig. 3d, 3e); (iii) they move with the gap edges (Fig. 3g; pp. 6–7, "originate from the interaction of the defects with the excitonic order"). Each of these is also expected for a hydrogenic or short-range impurity state split off a band edge in any semiconductor with a ~100 meV gap, light Dirac-like carriers and a large dielectric constant. Such states have nanometre-scale decay lengths (κ⁻¹ = ħ/√(2m*E_b)), merge into the continuum when the gap closes, and follow the band edges when the gap changes. The model does not discriminate either. In Eq. (1) (Methods, p. 11) Δ is a fixed, spatially uniform constant (50 meV), not determined self-consistently in the presence of the impurity, so Eq. (1) is mathematically a non-interacting two-band hybridization-gap insulator. Its result in Fig. 4f is what an electrostatic potential does in any gapped two-band system: an attractive region binds a state below the conduction-band edge, a repulsive region binds one above the valence-band edge, so a dipole gives two in-gap states and a monopole of one sign gives one (Fig. 4f, bottom). Nothing in the calculation uses the fact that Δ comes from a condensate, and the defining YSR ingredient, local suppression of the pair amplitude ("locally break electron-hole pairing", p. 3; "pair breaking interactions", p. 9), is absent; the analogy to magnetic impurities stated on p. 7 is not shown by the calculation. The authors should solve the impurity problem with Δ_i determined self-consistently from an interband interaction (mean-field excitonic gap equation with a local Coulomb perturbation), show that Δ is suppressed locally around the defect, and identify which observable differs from the same calculation with a fixed non-interacting gap of the same size and the same potential. Candidates are the ratio E±/Δ and how the bound-state energy depends on Δ, the decay length relative to ħv/Δ, the spatial structure of the electron-like and hole-like weights, the spectral-weight asymmetry, the dependence on the sign of the impurity charge, and coherence between E⁺ and E⁻. They should then measure that observable, for example by mapping the local gap edge as a function of distance from each non-trivial defect (a pair-breaking defect should suppress the gap over the coherence length, a simple potential scatterer in a band insulator would not), and comparing the E⁺ and E⁻ maps with the predicted electron/hole components. Without such a discriminating test, Fig. 4f supports "charged defects bind in-gap states" but not "YSR states of an exciton condensate", and the title, abstract and conclusions are not supported.

2. **The scaling of the in-gap energies with the gap has not been analyzed in a way that separates the competing pictures.** The claim that the states are tied to the excitonic order rests on E± moving with the gap edges under tip-height tuning (Fig. 3e–g; Supplementary Fig. S12/S16), plotted against ΔZ. That co-motion is also expected for states whose binding energy is fixed relative to the band edge they split from. In Fig. 3g, E⁻ stays almost fixed while the filled-state gap edge is pinned by VB2, and E⁺ follows the conduction-band edge downward, which is what a constant binding energy below the CB edge would produce. For YSR-type states the energy should scale with Δ itself (E = Δ·f(coupling)), so the binding energy shrinks in proportion as the gap closes; for a hydrogenic state it is set by the effective mass and dielectric constant, not by Δ. The authors should plot each defect's binding energies (E_CB − E⁺ and E⁻ − E_VB) and E± against the locally measured gap rather than ΔZ, for all five defect types, with error bars from several defects per type, and compare with the predictions of both scenarios (concern 1). Intermediate points from defects near the strained stripe (concern 7) and from temperature-dependent data (concern 14) would extend this into a central figure. Without this analysis, "solidly confirms the excitonic origin" (p. 7) is not supported.

3. **Tip-induced band bending, tip electric fields and defect charge-state effects are not addressed, yet they can produce paired, tip-height-dependent in-gap features at charged defects.** This is a narrow-gap system at 4.4 K with defects the authors say are charged (p. 7). In such systems STS peaks at defects commonly arise from tip-induced ionization or charge-state switching, or from band bending shifting defect levels; these features move with tip height and setpoint and can appear on both sides of E_F. The paper itself shows that the tip field matters: the caption of Supplementary Fig. S14 attributes "panning out spectral features" to the tip's strong electric field (Wannier–Stark ladder), and the STS gap at normal tip height (~140 meV, p. 7) is already larger than the ARPES gap (~100 meV, p. 4). Reducing the tip height changes the voltage division and the tip field, which rescales and shifts both band edges and any defect level, and could reproduce Fig. 3g without any change of the order parameter. The authors should show the bias-setpoint dependence of the spectra at fixed current (which changes the field), report how E± depend on setpoint, look for ionization rings or sharp current-dependent features in dI/dV maps, compare constant-height with constant-current spectra, and estimate the expected band bending quantitatively from the carrier density and screening length. This is also needed to interpret the gap-tuning experiment (concern 4).

4. **The tip-height "carrier injection" tuning (Fig. 3e–g, Supplementary Figs. S10–S12, S14–S16) is not shown to tune the excitonic order parameter, and the I–Z data suggest a mechanical or contact artifact.** The gap reduction from 140 to 60 meV is attributed to "transient carrier doping induced by the tunneling current" (p. 6), but no injected carrier density is estimated and no quantitative argument is given. This mechanism has been disputed for Ta2NiSe5 in a published Comment (ref. 55, see also ref. 54) that the authors cite without engaging its arguments. At tip–sample separations reduced by up to 0.5 nm, other causes are the electric field, voltage division, tip–sample hybridization and mechanical pressure from the tip (itself strain, the authors' other tuning knob). Supplementary Fig. S15/S11 shows abrupt steps in I(Z) near ΔZ ≈ −0.25 to −0.3 nm for several defects, and says the current "saturated to a consistent value of about 500 pA", called "the quantum contact regime". A point contact near G₀ would carry microamperes at tens of mV, so 500 pA is orders of magnitude below G₀V; the saturation suggests an instrumental limit (preamplifier or feedback) or something else. The abrupt collapse to the "metallic pseudogap" (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. S12a, where the colour maps are discontinuous at ΔZ ≈ −0.25 to −0.27 nm) coincides with the current jump, pointing to a change in the tip or junction (contact, picked-up adsorbate, local pressure) rather than an intrinsic doping-driven transition. The authors should state the bias and setpoint used for the I–Z curves and the setpoint defining ΔZ = 0; report the conductance in units of G₀; explain the 500 pA saturation and the step; show that the gap reduction is reversible on retraction and reproducible with different tips; test whether it scales with current or with ΔZ by approaching at different biases, or by raising the current at fixed height through the setpoint bias (doping and field effects scale differently); and compare topography before and after approach to check for tip changes. If the tuning cannot be tied to a change in the excitonic order, the "systematic tuning" argument does not hold.

5. **The charge state, net charge and dipole of the vacancies, which decide the model and the Ta/Pd distinction, are not established.** The key physical distinction (Ta vacancy charged, Pd vacancy "non-charged", pp. 7–8; Supplementary Figs. S10, S13/S17) comes from charge-density differences and a rigid ~0.3 eV PDOS shift of Te near a Ta vacancy (SI Fig. 10f) in neutral supercells. A Ta vacancy removes a cation and would ordinarily be an acceptor with net charge, and removing Ta from a finite supercell also shifts E_F (hole doping), so the PDOS shift relative to E_F may be a doping effect. Removing Pd also changes the electron count, and a Pd vacancy would generically be electrically active, yet only its dipole is reported, not its monopole. The charge state is set by charge-transition levels relative to E_F, which a neutral calculation does not determine; at 4.4 K the vacancy may be ionized. The text then chooses a "simple symmetric dipole" over a monopole because the monopole gives only a filled-state bound state (Fig. 4f). This creates a logical problem: if Pd or Te vacancies are charged, the model predicts filled-state in-gap states at them, but the trivial defects show none (SI Fig. 7c). It is also unclear how the DFT dipole maps onto V₀ (0–0.24 eV in Fig. 4f) and d in Eq. (2). The authors should report the net charge (Bader or similar), monopole and dipole moments in consistent units, and formation energies and charge-transition levels for Ta, Pd and Te vacancies; state how the charge-density difference was defined, including atomic relaxation of the reference; map the DFT values quantitatively onto V₀ and d; show that the two-bound-state result survives a dipole-plus-monopole potential; and reconcile the absence of in-gap states at the trivial defects with the monopole result.

6. **The argument that the states are many-body because "the DFT calculations do not predict the pairs of in-gap LDOS" (p. 6) is undermined by the DFT gap and the unknown supercell size.** The DFT defect states are said to extend less than 1 nm, but Supplementary Fig. S1/S5 states that the DFT gap "is much smaller than the experimental excitonic gap" (essentially a semimetal in the monolayer). A calculation with almost no gap cannot produce shallow in-gap states with a 5–6 nm Bohr radius; it pushes them into the continuum. The defect supercell, slab geometry, vacuum, dipole correction and k-mesh are not given, and a cell of a few nm would itself truncate any extended state. The authors should report these, and redo the defect calculation with a method that opens a gap of the right size (hybrid functional, DFT+U on Ta/Pd d states, or a scissor-corrected tight-binding model fitted to ARPES) in a supercell large enough to hold extended states, to show whether a non-excitonic ~100 meV gap already binds shallow states around a charged Ta vacancy. Otherwise the "DFT does not predict in-gap states, hence many-body" argument should be removed.

## B. Should address

7. **The strained-stripe control (Fig. 3a–d, SI Fig. 13/S9) is necessary but not discriminating, and the stripe is poorly characterized.** The absence of in-gap states at defects inside the stripe is said to "clearly indicate" an excitonic origin (p. 6), but once the gap collapses to a metallic pseudogap any bound state, hydrogenic or YSR, is screened and merges with the continuum. The strain value of 2.4% comes from the FWHM of an FFT Bragg peak over the whole image divided by Q_B; that width includes finite-window resolution and inhomogeneous broadening, gives no sign, and is not a local strain measure, and the same caption quotes a different "local shearing of about 1%" from a 0.57° angle. Other origins of the bright stripe and its gapless spectrum are not excluded: a subsurface dislocation, stacking fault or domain wall, or a local band overlap (strain-induced semimetal) with no excitonic fluctuations. The identity of defects inside the stripe rests only on topography, whose contrast is itself modified by strain. Supplementary Fig. S16/S12 notes that α′ keeps an in-gap state inside the pseudogap at small tip height, which contradicts the statement that in-gap states vanish in the pseudogap phase and is not discussed in the main text. The authors should provide a real-space strain map (e.g. Lawler–Fujita analysis), state the strain as a range with its method, show defect spectra at intermediate positions across the stripe edge where the gap is partly reduced and plot E± against the local gap there (a tip-independent version of Fig. 3g, feeding concern 2), and discuss the α′ case in the main text.

8. **The experimental pair is not a particle–hole pair in the YSR sense, and the analogy is not examined.** In superconductors YSR states appear at ±E as particle–hole partners of a single BdG state. Here E⁺ ≠ |E⁻| for every defect (SI Fig. 9/S5b table; e.g. α +56/−34 meV, β +70/−46 meV), the E⁺ and E⁻ maps have different shapes (Fig. 2k–o), in the authors' model they arise from two spatially distinct potential lobes, and in Fig. 3g E⁻ barely moves while E⁺ follows the conduction-band edge. This looks like two independent levels, one donor-like and one acceptor-like, each bound to its own band edge. The asymmetry is attributed to VB2 (p. 8) but the model omits VB2 and is electron–hole symmetric. The authors should state clearly whether E⁺ and E⁻ are one YSR pair or two separate bound states, and include VB2 and the asymmetric bands in Eq. (1) to test whether the model reproduces the observed E⁺/E⁻ energies and their different ΔZ dependence.
Raised by: r1, r2, r3

9. **The spatial extent of the in-gap states is not quantified or compared with the relevant length scales.** The "much wider extents, up to 6 nm" are taken to "immediately suggest" a many-body origin (p. 5), and the Fig. 2 caption says "approximately 5 nm". A shallow Coulomb-bound state in a small-gap, high-dielectric-constant material also has a Bohr radius of several nm, and the model extent is largely set by the Gaussian width σ = 2 nm (p. 11). The maps are taken at constant current over defects that protrude strongly in topography, so the setpoint effect can create apparent halos. The authors should extract fitted decay lengths with uncertainties along and across the chains, separately for E⁺ and E⁻, from the grids in Fig. 2k–o; compare them with the excitonic coherence length ħv/Δ (Dirac velocity from ARPES) and an effective-mass Bohr radius; look for oscillatory (Friedel-like, 2k_F) structure that would favour a condensate-related state; and use constant-height or setpoint-normalized maps to exclude setpoint artifacts.

10. **The model calculation is not set up for quantitative comparison.** The 41 × 41 lattice with a = 0.25 nm spans only ~10 nm, comparable to twice the bound-state extent, so finite-size quantization may affect the in-gap levels. The Gaussian width σ = 2 nm exceeds the dipole separation d = 1 nm (Eq. 2), so the dipole nearly cancels and the result may depend strongly on these unconstrained choices. The lattice is isotropic and 2D, whereas the material is quasi-1D, and it is unclear how t = 0.5 eV, μ = −1.5 eV and Δ = 50 meV were obtained "from experimental and DFT parameters" or relate to the ARPES dispersions. The numerical V₀ values in Fig. 4f (legends 0–0.24 eV and 0–0.10 eV) are not explained. Fig. 4f varies V₀ at fixed Δ, whereas the experiment varies Δ at fixed defect. The authors should show convergence with system size, sensitivity to σ and d, add anisotropic hopping (and VB2, concern 8), state the parameter choices, and compute E± versus Δ at fixed V₀ to overlay on Fig. 3g.

11. **The normalization and measurement conditions of the spectra are unspecified.** Nearly all spectra are "Normalized dI/dV" (Figs. 2f–j, 3d–f, S3, S12, S14), but it is not stated whether this is (dI/dV)/(I/V), division by a background, or something else. (dI/dV)/(I/V) is ill-defined near the gap edges of an insulator and can create or sharpen peaks there, which matters because the in-gap peaks sit close to the band edges, and the normalization is critical when tip height is varied (Fig. 3e, SI Fig. 16). The setpoint bias and current for each figure, lock-in modulation amplitude and frequency, and energy resolution are not given. The authors should state all of these and show raw dI/dV for representative spectra.

12. **Statistics and reproducibility of the defect energies are not reported.** Methods do not say how many crystals, cleaves and tips were used, how many defects of each type were measured, the spread of E± within a type, or whether the energies depend on local environment (defect density, distance to other defects). The table in SI Fig. 9/S5b gives single values without uncertainties, and these differ by up to 4–5 meV from the energies labelled in Fig. 2k–o (α −34/56 vs −30/55 meV; β′ −48/34 vs −45/35 meV; γ −22/36 vs −20/35 meV), while the text speaks of "marginally different energies" between types (p. 5). The ΔZ sweeps (Fig. 3g, Fig. S12) appear to be one sweep per defect type, drawn without error bars. The authors should reconcile the values, give E± as mean ± standard deviation (or histograms) per type, ideally from at least two cleaves, and add error bars to Fig. 3g. This is needed both for robustness and to decide whether the four DFT-assigned vacancy sites are spectroscopically distinguishable.

13. **The assignment of the non-trivial defects to top-layer Ta vacancies is qualitative.** It relies on visual agreement of topographs (Fig. 4a,b, SI Fig. 12/S8); the caption concedes that some contrast differs ("reasonable" agreement) and its sentence is unfinished ("while some contrast as guided by red circles…"), and for β and β′ the simulated occupied-state images differ visibly from experiment. Only Te, Pd and Ta vacancies were considered, not antisites (e.g. Pd_Ta, Te_Ta), interstitials, intercalants or extrinsic impurities. The mapping of α, α′, β, β′ onto sites (1)–(4) is justified only by mirror symmetry, and γ is assigned to a subsurface Ta vacancy without simulation (p. 6). No formation energies explain why Ta vacancies would be about as common as Pd vacancies (SI Fig. 6), and the defect density (Fig. S2) is not compared with the Ta deficiency expected from the off-stoichiometric flux growth (2.0:4.5:7.5) or the EDX composition. The authors should include antisite and interstitial candidates, give formation energies, give a quantitative image-comparison metric (e.g. cross-correlation) for each assignment, simulate the subsurface vacancy for γ, and compare the observed defect density with the stoichiometry.

14. **Temperature dependence is absent, although it is the most direct link to the condensate, and the room-temperature claim is unsupported.** The abstract claims a microscopic tool for exciton condensates "persisting up to room temperature", but all STS is at 4.4 K (1.26 K for the field data), and no in-gap states are shown at elevated temperature. A YSR state bound to the excitonic order should follow Δ(T), and with the transition at ~365 K the gap varies substantially over a range accessible to variable-temperature STM. The authors should track E± versus T (Δ(T)) on the same defect type, e.g. 4–80 K to match the ARPES temperature or 77–300 K, which would test the link to the order parameter independently of the tip-field complications of concerns 3–4. Otherwise the room-temperature claim should be removed and the claims narrowed.

15. **Several claims go beyond the evidence, and the argument risks circularity about the excitonic nature of the gap.** Examples: "establish both the YSR physics in exciton condensates" (abstract); "solidly confirms the excitonic origin" (p. 7); "the presence of the interaction-driven impurity-bound states in turn manifests the excitonic nature of the present insulating phase" (p. 9). Whether the gap of Ta2Pd3Te5 is excitonic rather than structural, phonon-driven or topological is still debated (refs. 42, 43, 49, 60, 61); the paper assumes the excitonic nature to interpret the states and then uses the states to confirm it. Unless concerns 1–4 are resolved, the title, abstract and conclusions should be reworded, e.g. to "defect-bound in-gap states tied to the excitonic gap in an excitonic insulator candidate", and the alternative origins of the parent gap acknowledged.

16. **The magnetic-field test (p. 8, SI Fig. 18/S14) cannot exclude the topological-defect alternative it targets.** At 2.5 T a g = 2 Zeeman splitting is gμ_B·B ≈ 0.3 meV, far below the ~10–20 meV width of the in-gap peaks, and the lock-in modulation and energy resolution are not given, so the absence of visible splitting is uninformative unless g is very large. The field data were also taken at 1.26 K rather than 4.4 K, mentioned only in the SI. The authors should quote the expected splitting against linewidth and resolution and either use larger fields and narrower features or remove the claim that the field data exclude the quantum spin Hall defect-state scenario.

17. **The pseudogap and "preformed exciton" interpretation is asserted.** The ~30 meV feature in the strained region and at small ΔZ is called evidence of "strong excitonic fluctuation, the preformed excitons" (p. 6). A soft gap at 4.4 K in a disordered or strained metallic region can also come from Altshuler–Aronov/Coulomb-gap physics or from tip-induced effects, and the in-gap state of α′ inside the pseudogap "whose origin is not understood yet" (Fig. S12/S16) is unexplained. The authors should either support the preformed-exciton reading (e.g. with temperature dependence) or present it as speculation.
