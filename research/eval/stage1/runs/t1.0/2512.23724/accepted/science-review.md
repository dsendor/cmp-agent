A. Must address

1. **The data and the model do not tell an excitonic, pair-breaking bound state apart from an ordinary defect level in a gapped semiconductor.** In the Methods Hamiltonian (Eq. 1) the excitonic order enters only as a uniform, fixed interband coupling −Δ Σ(c†v + v†c). Mathematically this is a band-hybridized, non-interacting semiconductor with a local electrostatic potential V_i acting equally on both orbitals. Δ is not computed self-consistently and is not suppressed near the impurity, so the defining YSR ingredient, local pair breaking (suppression of the order parameter), is absent, and nothing in the calculation of Fig. 4e–g uses the many-body nature of Δ. In any band insulator a charged donor binds a state below the conduction band edge and a charged acceptor one above the valence band edge; a local charge dipole (the authors' DFT picture, Fig. 4b) has both ends and would generically bind one level of each kind, a pair split off the band edges as in Fig. 2f–j. The two experimental tests the paper relies on do not discriminate: (i) any level whose binding energy is tied to the band edges (hydrogenic or deep) moves towards midgap as the gap shrinks (Fig. 3g); (ii) any localized state broadens or is screened away when the host becomes a metallic pseudogap (Fig. 3d,f). Other alternatives to exclude include ordinary donor/acceptor states of a Pd(Ta) antisite with an effective-mass hydrogenic envelope (the ~5–6 nm extent is comparable to an effective Bohr radius in a narrow-gap semiconductor) and resonances involving the spectator band VB2; the authors should also explain why a Ta-site bound state has the ~5–6 nm envelope with lattice-periodic modulation seen in Extended Data Fig. 3a. Hence "solidly confirms the excitonic origin" (p. 1554), "charge-exciton version of the YSR mechanism" (abstract), the title's YSR analogy, and the Conclusion's statement that the bound states "manifest the excitonic nature of the present insulating phase" (which is circular) do not yet follow. The authors should: (a) solve the impurity problem self-consistently with Δ_i determined locally from the interaction, show the local suppression of the order parameter, and look for its signature in the data (for example a spatial map of the local gap edges near the defect, compared with Fig. 2k–o); (b) repeat the Fig. 4e–g calculation with Δ = 0 and a non-interacting hybridization or structural gap of the same size and the same dipole, and state what differs (number of states, energies relative to the edges, spatial extent, scaling with the gap); (c) identify and test at least one observable that the excitonic scenario predicts and a conventional dipole-bound level does not — for example the scaling of binding energy with gap: for YSR-type states (E± − gap centre)/Δ_EI should be set by impurity strength and E± should move in proportion to Δ, whereas band-edge-bound levels keep a roughly constant binding energy from each edge. They should plot E± measured from the gap edges, and E±/Δ_EI, against Δ_EI for each defect type from Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 8 with error bars. Other candidate discriminators are the E+/E− ratio and its energy dependence, the relation between bound-state energy, gap and coherence length, wavefunction oscillation set by the Fermi wavevector of the parent semimetal bands rather than the lattice, and the dependence on dipole strength including any level crossing analogous to the YSR quantum phase transition. Without this, the observations support "defect-bound in-gap states in a gapped phase", not the YSR analogy.

2. **The tip-height "gap tuning" is not separated from tip-induced artefacts, and the gap collapse coincides with a visible discontinuity in the data.** The reduction of the apparent gap from about 140 to 60 meV and its abrupt collapse (Fig. 3e–g, Extended Data Figs. 7, 8) are attributed to carrier injection by the tip field, and this series carries most of the "excitonic origin" argument. On approach to ΔZ = −500 pm ("virtually touching" the surface) several other effects become strong: tip-induced band bending, which rigidly shifts and compresses the local bands; voltage division on a low-carrier-density gapped surface, where the fraction of bias dropped inside the sample changes with tip–sample distance and rescales every bias-referenced feature, making a gap appear to shrink and in-gap peaks move towards E_F in proportion (the authors cite exactly this debate for Ta2NiSe5, refs 42, 43, but do not address it); screening of the defect potential (the authors themselves impose a concomitant reduction of the dipole to 0.6 V0 in Fig. 4f, an admission that the defect potential changes with tip height); a change of the defect's charge state; tip–sample force and the resulting local strain (which the authors show separately closes the gap); and high current density and local heating. Each of these alone moves in-gap levels towards E_F together with the band edges, so the co-motion in Fig. 3g does not by itself show coupling to the excitonic order. The Methods state a tip field "on the order of 10 MV" and that the excitonic Stark effect is "an order of magnitude smaller than the effect of carrier doping", but give no estimate, no induced carrier density, and Extended Data Fig. 7 invokes a Wannier–Stark ladder, implying a strong field acting on the electronic structure. In every panel of Extended Data Fig. 8a,b (and Fig. 3e) there is a sharp horizontal seam at ΔZ ≈ −0.25 to −0.3 nm where colour scale and spectra change abruptly; the "abrupt collapse into a metallic pseudogap" occurs exactly there, coinciding with what Extended Data Fig. 8 calls the "quantum contact regime". The apparent gap of ~140 meV at ΔZ = 0 versus ~100 meV from ARPES and Fig. 1e already points to a setpoint-dependent energy scale (possibly band bending present at ΔZ = 0). The authors should: state whether the seam reflects a change in acquisition (stitched datasets, setpoint or gain change, tip change) or a physical transition, and explain why a carrier-driven suppression would be so abrupt; show raw I(V), current–distance curves and the junction conductance across the ΔZ range; demonstrate reversibility (retract and recover the original spectra; hysteresis); quantify the field, induced carrier density and band bending as functions of ΔZ (e.g. Poisson modelling with the known carrier density and tip radius for the PtIr tip); estimate tip-induced force and strain; show whether features outside the gap (VB2 edge, the Wannier–Stark fanning features) rescale by the same factor as the gap, which would indicate voltage division; plot E± relative to midgap and to each band edge separately, since band bending shifts both edges the same way while order-parameter reduction moves them towards each other; state which tip height corresponds to the "normal" gap and reconcile 140 vs 100 meV; and ideally add a tip-independent tuning knob (temperature, gating, or the strain gradient).

3. **The structural and charge assignment of the defect is not unique and conflicts with the formation energies.** The Extended Data Fig. 4 caption states that "the Pd substitution on a Ta site, a Ta vacancy, and the Ta-Pd interchange defect exhibit topographic features consistent with the experimental observations", while the main text says the Pd substitution "explains the observed STM images excellently"; the dipole argument is shown only for Pd_Ta versus the Pd vacancy (Fig. 4b,d). Extended Data Table 1 does not single out Pd_Ta either and has internal problems: under Pd-rich/Te-rich conditions, which match the flux composition (Ta:Pd:Te = 2.0:4.5:7.5 versus stoichiometric 2:3:5, Methods), Pd_Ta (−0.948 eV, i.e. spontaneous) and the Ta vacancy (0.148 eV) both lie below the Pd vacancy (0.756 eV), yet Pd vacancies are the majority (77.8%; 308 vs 67, about 4.6 times more numerous; Extended Data Fig. 1). The Pd-vacancy energy is identical (0.756 eV) in the generic, Pd-rich/Ta-rich and Pd-rich/Te-rich columns and lower (−0.577 eV) only in the Te-rich/Ta-rich column, although a Pd vacancy should be costliest under Pd-rich conditions; the column headings also differ from the conditions in the caption, and it is unclear how Pd-rich and Ta-rich limits are imposed simultaneously. Only neutral defects appear to be considered, although the mechanism rests on the defect charge. The Methods describe only the bulk DFT setup. The authors should: give the charge distribution and dipole for the Ta vacancy and the Ta–Pd interchange and state whether they would also give two in-gap states in the model (if several do, say so — it weakens the specific charge-dipole mechanism but may support a more generic pair-breaking claim); compute charged states and transition levels of Pd_Ta relative to the gap; state the chemical-potential limits per column and compare predicted populations with the observed 67:308 ratio; add discriminating evidence such as a quantitative comparison of the bias dependence in Extended Data Fig. 5 with simulations of each candidate; and give supercell/slab size, surface termination, k-mesh and relaxation used for the defect and STM simulations.

4. **Tip-induced charging of the defects is not ruled out as the origin of the sharp in-gap peaks and their spatial extent.** In semiconductors and narrow-gap insulators, sharp dI/dV peaks near defects often arise from the tip field switching the defect charge state, appearing as disks or rings whose radius grows with bias and setpoint. The ~5–6 nm extent (Fig. 2k–o, Extended Data Fig. 3) and its pronounced growth with tunnelling current (Extended Data Fig. 9: 30 → 100 → 200 pA, with saturated, ring-like features at 200 pA) are what that mechanism predicts; the paper reads the growth as YSR-like broadening with a smaller gap, but the maps are at fixed bias (−35 and +40 meV) while the state energies move. Several spectra in Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 8c at ΔZ = −0.2 to −0.3 nm show dip and derivative-like line shapes. The authors should show dI/dV maps at several energies across each resonance to test whether a ring disperses in radius with bias; give point spectra at fixed lateral offsets from the defect as a function of setpoint (charging peaks shift strongly with lateral distance, true bound states do not); show grid spectroscopy at more than one setpoint for an unperturbed gap; and report whether hysteresis, telegraph noise or negative differential conductance appear at the peaks.

B. Should address

5. **The model is qualitative, its parameters are not tied to the material, and the quoted dipole is wrong by many orders of magnitude.** The dipole moment "0.14 × 10⁻¹² C m for a Pd atom" (p. 1554) is about 10¹⁶ times atomic scale (e·Å ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻²⁹ C m, 1 Debye ≈ 3.3 × 10⁻³⁰ C m), presumably a unit error; since the dipole is the only link between DFT and the model, the corrected value (in e·Å or Debye), the method used (Bader charges, plane-averaged density difference, etc.) and the values for the Pd vacancy and other candidates are needed. The model parameters (t = 0.5 eV, μ = −1.5 eV, Δ = 50 meV, V0 = 0.24 eV, Gaussian width 2a, a = 0.25 nm; t_c = 1.0 eV, t_v = 0.5 eV in Fig. 4g) are neither derived from DFT nor fitted to data. The model is an isotropic, electron–hole-symmetric 2D square lattice, whereas Ta2Pd3Te5 is quasi-1D with a Dirac crossing of CB1/VB1 and an extra valence band VB2 (Fig. 1d, Extended Data Fig. 7d), and the bound states are visibly elongated along the chains (Fig. 2k–o, Extended Data Fig. 3). The measured asymmetry (E− sharper and more robust than E+, Fig. 3e,g) is reproduced only by adding a five-Gaussian charge distribution and unequal hoppings chosen ad hoc, while the band-edge asymmetry is attributed to VB2, which is absent from the model; the dipole screening with gap reduction (linear to 0.6 V0, Fig. 4f) is also imposed by hand. The authors should state which parameters are fitted and which are fixed; derive V0 and the charge geometry from the DFT charge distribution of the Pd-on-Ta defect; use anisotropic band parameters fitted to the ARPES/DFT bands of Fig. 1d and include VB2 (or show robustness to it); show the sensitivity of E± to these choices; and compare bound-state energies relative to the gap edges and the spatial extents along and across the chains quantitatively with Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 3. Otherwise the agreement in Fig. 4g carries little weight.

6. **There is no temperature dependence.** All STM data are at 4.4 K. The proposed condensate has a transition at about 365–370 K (Fig. 1d). If the in-gap states are tied to excitonic order, E± relative to the gap edges should follow Δ(T), whereas ordinary defect levels would follow the single-particle band edges. Spectra of E± and the gap edges on the same defects at one or two higher temperatures (e.g. 77 K and above 200 K, as high as the STM allows), compared with the ARPES gap at the same temperatures, would give the cleanest tip-independent test, free of the concerns in items 2 and 4. If the in-gap energies track Δ(T) as the model with fixed V0 predicts, that would strongly support the claim.

7. **The excitonic character of the gap in Ta2Pd3Te5 is taken as established, although it is debated.** The cited literature (refs 33–36, 38, 39, 47, 48) also considers a topological insulator and a structural or band-hybridization (lattice-driven) origin, and the YSR analogy requires an exciton condensate. The Introduction and Conclusion should acknowledge the open debate, and the authors should state which of their observations would also hold if the gap were mainly lattice- or hybridization-driven (see item 1).

8. **The strained-stripe control is limited.** The claim that "the in-gap states originate from the coupling of the defects with the excitonic order" (p. 1553) rests on "a few" non-trivial defects inside one stripe; only three are shown (one each of α, β, β′; Fig. 3b,d). Strain (ε ≈ 2.4%, Supplementary Fig. 10, extracted from a 0.3 nm corrugation by a procedure deferred to the supplement), local carrier density, and the defect's own structural relaxation and charge state all change together with the gap, so the absence of in-gap states could come from metallic screening or altered defect structure rather than loss of excitonic order; in a 30 meV pseudogap, broadened in-gap states may also simply be unresolvable. The authors should give the number of defects and stripes examined; show how strain is extracted; show that the defect topography (versus unstrained α/β/β′) and the DFT dipole are unchanged under 2.4% strain; estimate the energy resolution needed to resolve the predicted states inside a 30 meV pseudogap; and, most usefully, show spectra of each defect type at several positions across the stripe boundary, where the gap varies continuously, to test whether E± shifts continuously with the local gap independently of the tip.

10. **There are no statistics of bound-state energies, and sample and tip reproducibility are not given.** Fig. 2f–j and Fig. 3g show one representative (one sweep) per defect type, and the tip-height sweeps were done on "a few" defects. The authors should state how many defects of each type were measured spectroscopically and how many crystals, cleaves and tips were used; give histograms of E+, E− (relative to the local gap edges) and peak widths for each type, together with the local gap; confirm the gap-tuning sequences were repeated on several nominally identical defects and with different tips. 12. **The dI/dV normalization, measurement parameters and peak extraction are not described.** Spectra are shown as "normalized dI/dV" (Figs. 2f–j, 3d–f, Extended Data Fig. 8) without stating the normalization. Normalization by I/V or by setpoint inside a gap, and in constant-height ΔZ sweeps, can create or distort peaks near the gap edges.
