Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper reports STM/STS of the candidate excitonic insulator Ta2Pd3Te5 at 4.4 K and finds pairs of in-gap resonances (E− in filled states, E+ in empty states) bound to a subset of point defects, assigned by DFT-simulated topography to Pd substituting on Ta sites. The resonances shift with the gap when the gap is reduced by tip approach, and are absent in a strained stripe where the gap closes into a pseudogap. The authors interpret them, with a lattice model of a charge dipole in an excitonic insulator, as the charge-exciton analogue of Yu–Shiba–Rusinov (YSR) states. If the excitonic, pair-breaking origin is established, this would be the first observation of impurity-bound states of an exciton condensate and a local probe of excitonic order. That is of broad interest. As presented, however, the central step, that these states are specific to an excitonic condensate rather than ordinary defect levels in a gapped semiconductor, is supported mostly by qualitative arguments.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- High-quality atomically resolved topography and spectroscopy, with a systematic classification of defect types (Fig. 2, Extended Data Figs. 1–6) and defect statistics over an 80 × 80 nm² area (67 non-trivial, 308 trivial).
- A clear internal control: the majority defect (Pd vacancy) shows no in-gap state (Fig. 1g; Supplementary Fig. 4).
- Two independent ways of tuning the gap (local strain, Fig. 3a–d; tip height, Fig. 3e–g and Extended Data Figs. 7–8), with the in-gap states tracking the gap edges in both.
- A concrete microscopic model (DFT charge dipole plus a tight-binding excitonic-insulator Hamiltonian) that reproduces the pair structure, the inward shift with decreasing gap (Fig. 4f) and a bound-state size of the right order (~6.5 nm against ~5–6 nm observed).
- A timely topic: excitonic insulators and how to verify them, Ta2Pd3Te5 in particular.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The states are not shown to be unique to an excitonic condensate, as opposed to conventional defect levels in a gapped semiconductor. In any band insulator, a charged donor binds a state below the conduction band edge and a charged acceptor binds one above the valence band edge. A local charge dipole (the authors' own DFT picture, Fig. 4b) has a positive end and a negative end, so it would generically bind one donor-like and one acceptor-like level: a pair split from the conduction and valence edges, as in Fig. 2f–j. The two tests the paper relies on do not discriminate. (i) The states follow the gap edges under tip tuning (Fig. 3g). Hydrogenic or deep levels tied to the band edges would also do this. (ii) The states vanish when the gap collapses into a metallic pseudogap (Fig. 3d, 3f). Any bound state is screened or hybridised away once the host becomes metallic. The model in Fig. 4e–g is also not tested against this alternative.
   - The authors should run the same tight-binding calculation with Δ = 0 and a single-particle (non-excitonic) hybridisation or band gap of the same size, using the same dipole, and show what differs: the number of states, their energies relative to the edges, their spatial extent, and how they scale with the gap.
   - Experimentally, the most direct discriminator is how the binding energy scales with gap size. For YSR-type pair-breaking states, (E± − gap centre)/Δ_EI should be set by the impurity strength and the energies should move in proportion to Δ. For band-edge-bound levels, the binding energy measured from each band edge should be roughly constant. The authors should plot E± measured from the gap edges, and E±/Δ_EI, against Δ_EI for each defect type from the Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 8 data, with error bars.
   - If available, variable-temperature STS (for example at 77 K or higher, where the excitonic gap is expected to shrink toward the 365–370 K transition) would be a cleaner tuning knob than the tip.
   - Without this, the title claim ("analogue of the Yu–Shiba–Rusinov state") is not established, and the paper's secondary claim that the bound states "manifest the excitonic nature of the present insulating phase" (Conclusion) is circular.

2. Tip-height gap "tuning" may be partly a measurement artefact (voltage division and tip-induced band bending), not a change of the excitonic order parameter.
   - On a low-carrier-density gapped surface, part of the applied bias drops inside the sample, and that fraction changes with tip–sample distance. This rescales every bias-referenced feature, which can make a gap appear to shrink and in-gap peaks appear to move toward E_F in proportion (Fig. 3e,f; Extended Data Figs. 7, 8). The authors cite precisely this debate for Ta2NiSe5 (refs 42, 43), but neither the main text nor the Methods addresses it.
   - The Methods state that the tip field is "on the order of 10 MV" (units missing) and that the excitonic Stark effect is "estimated to be an order of magnitude smaller" than carrier doping. Neither estimate is shown, and no estimate of the induced carrier density is given.
   - The authors should:
     - show whether features outside the gap (the VB2 edge, and the fanning features attributed to a Wannier–Stark ladder in Extended Data Fig. 7) rescale by the same factor as the gap; a uniform rescaling would indicate voltage division;
     - quantify tip-induced band bending, for example from Poisson modelling with the known carrier density and the tip radius;
     - report the junction conductance across the ΔZ range, especially near the abrupt collapse at ΔZ ≈ −0.25 nm, which coincides with what Extended Data Fig. 8 calls the "quantum contact regime".
   - The apparent gap of ~140 meV at ΔZ = 0 (Fig. 3e; text p. 1554) against ~100 meV from ARPES and from the STS in Fig. 1e already points to a setpoint-dependent energy scale. Because the "excitonic origin" argument depends heavily on this tuning series, it must be shown to reflect a real change of Δ_EI.

3. Tip-induced charging of the defects is not ruled out as the origin of the sharp in-gap peaks and their spatial extent.
   - In semiconductors and narrow-gap insulators, sharp dI/dV peaks near defects often come from the tip field switching the defect's charge state. Such peaks appear as disks or rings around the defect whose radius grows with bias and setpoint.
   - The observed ~5–6 nm extent (Fig. 2k–o; Extended Data Fig. 3) and its pronounced growth with tunnelling current (Extended Data Fig. 9: 30 → 100 → 200 pA) are what one would expect from that mechanism.
   - The paper reads the growth as YSR-like broadening with a smaller gap. But at 200 pA the maps show saturated, ring-like features, and the growth also occurs at fixed bias (−35 and +40 meV), where the state energies themselves move.
   - The authors should:
     - show dI/dV maps at several energies across each resonance, to test whether a ring disperses in radius with bias;
     - give point spectra at fixed lateral offsets from the defect as a function of setpoint, to test whether peak energies shift with lateral distance (charging peaks shift strongly; true bound states do not);
     - report whether hysteresis, telegraph noise or negative differential conductance appears at the peaks (several spectra in Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 8c show dip and derivative-like line shapes at ΔZ = −0.2 to −0.3 nm).

4. The defect assignment (Pd on a Ta site) is not unique, and the dipole picture depends on it.
   - Extended Data Fig. 4 itself states that "the Pd substitution on a Ta site, a Ta vacancy, and the Ta-Pd interchange defect exhibit topographic features consistent with the experimental observations".
   - The formation energies in Extended Data Table 1 do not single out the Pd substitution either. Under Pd-rich/Te-rich conditions, which match the stated growth ratio of Ta:Pd:Te = 2.0:4.5:7.5 against the stoichiometric 2:3:5, the Ta vacancy (0.148 eV) and the Pd substitution (−0.948 eV, that is, spontaneous) both lie below the Pd vacancy (0.756 eV). This contradicts the observed population, in which 77.8% of defects are Pd vacancies (Extended Data Fig. 1).
   - The authors should:
     - give the charge distribution and dipole moment computed for the Ta vacancy and the Ta–Pd interchange, and state whether those defects would also give two in-gap states in the model;
     - explain which chemical-potential limits apply and how the predicted populations compare with the observed 67 : 308 ratio;
     - add discriminating evidence for the assignment, for example quantitative comparison of the bias dependence in Extended Data Fig. 5 with simulations for each candidate.
   - If several defect types with different charge distributions all give the same pair of in-gap states, that should be stated. It would weaken the specific "charge dipole" mechanism but could strengthen a more generic pair-breaking claim.

B. Should address

5. The model is qualitative, and its parameters are not tied to the material.
   - The Hamiltonian (Methods, Eq. 1) is a 2D square lattice with electron–hole-symmetric bands (t = 0.5 eV, μ = −1.5 eV, Δ = 50 meV, a = 0.25 nm). The real system is quasi-1D, with a Dirac crossing of CB1/VB1 and an extra valence band VB2 (Fig. 1d; Extended Data Fig. 7d).
   - The dipole strength V0 = 0.24 eV and width σ = 2a are not derived from the DFT charge distribution. The filled/empty asymmetry (Fig. 4g) is obtained by adding a five-Gaussian charge distribution and unequal hoppings (t_c = 1.0 eV, t_v = 0.5 eV) chosen ad hoc. The screening of the dipole with gap reduction (linear down to 0.6 V0, Fig. 4f) is also imposed by hand.
   - The authors should:
     - derive V0 and the spatial charge distribution from the DFT result for the Pd-on-Ta defect;
     - use band parameters fitted to the ARPES dispersion in Fig. 1d;
     - show the sensitivity of E± to these choices;
     - say whether the anisotropic, chain-aligned shape of the bound states (Fig. 2k–o) is reproduced.
   - The 6.5 nm size estimate from ħv_F/Δ needs the v_F and Δ that were used to be stated. For a quasi-1D system the estimate should differ along and across the chains.

6. The excitonic character of the gap in Ta2Pd3Te5 is taken as established. It remains debated in the cited literature, which also considers a topological insulator and a structural or band-hybridisation origin (refs 33–36, 38, 39, 47, 48). The YSR analogy requires an excitonic condensate. The Introduction and Conclusion should acknowledge the open debate, and the authors should state which of their observations would also hold if the gap were mainly lattice-driven or hybridisation-driven. This links to concern 1.

7. The strained stripe is not a clean control.
   - In the stripe (Fig. 3a–d), strain (ε ≈ 2.4%, Supplementary Fig. 10), local carrier density and possibly the defect charge state all change together with the gap. The absence of in-gap states there could come from metallic screening of the defect charge rather than from the loss of excitonic order.
   - Only "a few" non-trivial defects lie in the stripe, and only three are shown (Fig. 3b,d).
   - The authors should give the number of defects examined, show spectra for each type at several positions across the stripe boundary (where the gap varies continuously), and test whether the in-gap energies scale with the local gap along this spatial gradient, independently of the tip.

8. The text contradicts the data on whether in-gap states disappear in the pseudogap regime. The main text says that "In the pseudogap metallic regime, the in-gap states disappear or are greatly reduced" and that this "solidly confirms the excitonic origin". Yet Extended Data Fig. 8 states that for defect α′ "an in-gap state appears within the pseudogap in the quantum contact regime, whose origin is not understood yet". Several panels of Extended Data Fig. 8c at ΔZ = −0.3 nm also show sharp features near E_F. The authors should discuss this openly in the main text and tone down "solidly confirms".

9. Energy referencing and particle–hole structure.
   - The YSR analogy concerns a pair of states at ±E about the gap centre. Here the gap is asymmetric about E_F (Fig. 1e; Extended Data Fig. 7), and E± are quoted relative to E_F (for example −35/+40 meV for α in Fig. 1g; −30/+55 meV for α in Fig. 2k).
   - The energies should be referenced to the gap midpoint and given as fractions of Δ_EI for every defect type, with spreads over defects of the same type.
   - The labelled energies are also inconsistent: Fig. 1g maps α states at −35/+40 meV, while Fig. 2k maps α at −30/+55 meV. Please explain, for example by defect-to-defect variation or setpoint.

10. Statistics and reproducibility are not given. The paper does not state how many defects of each type were measured spectroscopically, how many cleaves and samples, or how many tips. It also gives no distribution of E± and peak widths. Fig. 3g shows one sweep per defect type. Please report the number of defects, cleaves and samples, and the spread of E± and Δ_EI across them, and confirm that the gap-tuning behaviour was reproduced with different tips.

11. The room-temperature and device claims go beyond the data. The abstract claims "a microscopic tool to probe and control quantum properties in exciton condensates persisting up to room temperature", and the Conclusion mentions dissipation-less devices and coherent optoelectronics. All in-gap state measurements are at 4.4 K. Either show the in-gap states at elevated temperature or restrict these statements.

C. Minor

12. The dipole moment "0.14 × 10⁻¹² C m for a Pd atom" (p. 1554) is many orders of magnitude too large: 1 e·nm ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻²⁸ C m, and 1 Debye ≈ 3.3 × 10⁻³⁰ C m. Please correct the value or the units and give it in e·Å or Debye.

13. The y axis of Fig. 3g is labelled "Bias (mV)" but runs from −0.10 to 0.10, which are values in V. The x axis, ΔZ, runs from 0.1 to −0.5 nm in an unconventional direction. Please fix the units and clarify the sign convention for ΔZ. The Methods define it via "Δz = −100 pm from z0" for the normal height while the figures place ΔZ = 0 at the normal height, which is confusing.

14. "Tip field … (on the order of 10 MV)" in the Methods lacks units per length (presumably MV/cm or V/nm). Please give units and show how the value was estimated.

15. The Methods state that "The lattice constants were taken from experiments⁴⁸", but ref. 48 is a paper on topological edge states. Ref. 37 appears to be intended.

16. Extended Data Table 1: please define the chemical-potential limits for each column and say whether charged defect states were considered. A formation energy that depends on the Fermi level matters for a "charged impurity" picture. The caption's "rich PD" should read "rich Pd".

17. The γ defect is attributed to a subsurface defect "thought to be similar" (p. 1553; Extended Data Fig. 6) without supporting DFT. Please either support this or label it speculative.

18. Fig. 2f–j: the spectra are normalised and offset, and the normalisation procedure is not given. Please state how dI/dV was normalised (for example by I/V or by the setpoint) and give the setpoint and lock-in modulation for each figure. Lock-in modulation sets the resolution of peak widths and energies.

19. The Methods say the ARPES "energy resolution better than 0.6 meV". For a laser ARPES system at 80 K this seems optimistic; please check whether it refers to the analyser only.

20. Fig. 4f shows "1.0Δ_EI" and "0.6Δ_EI", while the caption describes reducing the gap to 0.6Δ_EI and the dipole to 0.6V0 at the same time. Please clarify whether these two are tied by assumption.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a side-by-side calculation of the same charge dipole in a non-excitonic band insulator, and a quantitative plot of E± (gap-centred and normalised to Δ_EI) against Δ_EI from experiment. Together these would turn the YSR analogy from qualitative into a testable claim.
2. Separate a genuine change in the gap from tip-induced artefacts: model the voltage division and band bending, and map the in-gap features against energy and lateral distance to rule out charging rings.
3. Make the defect assignment more robust by computing charge distributions for all topographically compatible candidates (Pd on Ta, Ta vacancy, Ta–Pd interchange) and reconciling the formation energies with the observed defect populations and growth conditions.
4. Use the spatial gap gradient at the edge of the strained stripe, or variable-temperature STS, as a tip-independent way to vary Δ_EI, and report statistics over many defects and cleaves.
