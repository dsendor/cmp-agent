Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors use STM/STS at 4.4 K on the excitonic-insulator candidate Ta2Pd3Te5 to find pairs of in-gap states (E− in filled states, E+ in empty states) at a minority class of point defects, which they assign to Pd substituting on Ta sites. The in-gap energies follow the gap edges when the gap is reduced by local strain or by tip-height-controlled carrier injection. The authors interpret the states as an excitonic analogue of Yu–Shiba–Rusinov (YSR) states, produced by a local charge dipole. If the excitonic, pair-breaking origin were established, this would be the first observation of a long-predicted impurity bound state in an exciton condensate and a new local probe of excitonic order. That would be of broad interest. As the manuscript stands, the data establish defect-bound in-gap states whose energies scale with the gap. They do not yet establish that the binding requires excitonic coherence rather than a gap of any origin.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- High-quality atomically resolved topography and grid spectroscopy. The in-gap states are clear and reproducible across five defect types (α, α′, β, β′, γ; Fig. 2), with defect statistics over a large area (Extended Data Fig. 1: 67 non-trivial, 308 trivial, 21 unidentified).
- A built-in comparison between defect classes. The majority defect (assigned to Pd vacancies) shows no in-gap states, and the minority Ta-site defects all do (Fig. 1g, Supplementary Fig. 4).
- Two independent ways of reducing the gap: a naturally strained stripe (Fig. 3a–d) and tip-height-controlled carrier injection (Fig. 3e–g, Extended Data Figs. 7, 8). In both, the in-gap states follow the gap edges and vanish or weaken when the gap collapses into a pseudogap.
- A DFT defect search over plausible point defects (Extended Data Fig. 4, Extended Data Table 1) combined with a simple tight-binding impurity model (Fig. 4e–g). The model qualitatively reproduces the pair of states, their shift towards E_F as the gap shrinks, and their asymmetry.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The model, and hence the experiment, does not separate excitonic pair breaking from ordinary impurity states in any two-band insulator. In the Methods Hamiltonian (Eq. 1), the excitonic order enters only as a uniform, fixed hybridization term −Δ Σ(c†v + v†c). Δ is not computed self-consistently and is not suppressed near the impurity. Mathematically, Eq. 1 is a band-hybridized (non-interacting) semiconductor with a local electrostatic potential V_i acting equally on both orbitals. Any such insulator binds states inside its gap at a charged dipole, and those states move with the gap edges when the gap shrinks. The paper's central claim is a "charge-exciton version of the YSR mechanism" (abstract; Fig. 4). It therefore needs an ingredient that is specific to an exciton condensate. The authors should:
   - solve the impurity problem self-consistently, with Δ_i determined locally from the interaction, and show the local suppression of the order parameter that is the hallmark of pair breaking; and
   - identify an observable that differs between the self-consistent excitonic case and a fixed-hybridization (or structurally gapped) insulator with the same band dispersions. Examples are the ratio and energy dependence of E+ and E−, a spatial profile of Δ_i visible as gap-edge suppression around the defect, or the predicted dependence of the bound-state energy on dipole strength, including any level crossing analogous to the YSR quantum phase transition.

   Without this, the observations support "defect-bound in-gap states in a gapped phase" but not the YSR analogy in the title.

2. The tip-height experiment cannot separate a reduced excitonic order parameter from tip-induced band bending and screening. The "systematic tuning of the excitonic gap" in Fig. 3e–g and Extended Data Figs. 7 and 8 uses tip approach. The authors state that this produces local carrier doping and a strong field "on the order of 10 MV" (Methods). The same tip field and local doping will:
   - rigidly shift and compress the local bands (tip-induced band bending);
   - screen the defect potential (the authors themselves include this as a reduction of V0 to 0.6V0 in Fig. 4f); and
   - possibly change the defect's charge state.

   Each of these, on its own, makes in-gap levels move towards E_F together with the band edges. The co-motion in Fig. 3g therefore does not by itself show coupling to the excitonic order. The Methods claim that the Stark effect is "an order of magnitude smaller than the effect of carrier doping", but no estimate or calculation is given. Extended Data Fig. 7 attributes additional fanning features to a Wannier–Stark ladder, which implies a strong field acting on the electronic structure. The authors should:
   - quantify the field and induced carrier density as a function of ΔZ;
   - show how the in-gap energies behave relative to the midgap and to each band edge separately, since band bending shifts both edges in the same direction while order-parameter reduction moves them towards each other; and
   - ideally, add a gap-tuning knob that does not involve the tip, such as temperature (concern 3) or electrostatic gating.

   In Extended Data Fig. 8a (and Fig. 3e), the abrupt "collapse" at ΔZ ≈ −0.25 nm appears as a sharp horizontal discontinuity in every panel at nearly the same height. The authors should confirm that this is not a stitching boundary between separate data sets or a tip change, and should explain why the transition is so abrupt if it reflects carrier-density-driven suppression of excitonic order.

3. There is no temperature dependence. The order parameter of the proposed exciton condensate has a known temperature scale (transition at about 365–370 K; Fig. 1d). The cleanest test that the bound states are tied to excitonic order, rather than to a gap of structural or other origin, is to follow E± and the gap edges with temperature (for example 4.4 K, 77 K and as high as the STM allows). This should be compared with the ARPES gap at the same temperatures. If the in-gap energies track Δ(T) as predicted by the model with fixed V0, that would strongly support the claim. All STM data here are at 4.4 K only. The abstract's phrase "exciton condensates persisting up to room temperature" rests on literature transport and ARPES results, not on the in-gap states reported here. The authors should either add temperature-dependent STS or remove the room-temperature framing tied to the in-gap states.

4. The defect assignment and the dipole that drives the model are under-constrained.
   - Extended Data Fig. 4 states that "the Pd substitution on a Ta site, a Ta vacancy, and the Ta-Pd interchange defect exhibit topographic features consistent with the experimental observations". The main text, by contrast, says the Pd substitution "explains the observed STM images excellently". The assignment therefore rests on formation energies.
   - Extended Data Table 1 has internal problems. The Pd-vacancy formation energy is the same (0.756 eV) in the generic, Pd-rich/Ta-rich and Pd-rich/Te-rich columns. It is lower (−0.577 eV) only in the Te-rich/Ta-rich column, although a Pd vacancy should be most costly under Pd-rich conditions. The column headings also differ from the conditions discussed in the caption.
   - The crystals are grown from a Pd- and Te-rich flux (molar ratio 2.0:4.5:7.5; Methods). Under Pd-rich/Te-rich conditions the table gives −0.948 eV for the Pd substitution, which would make it the most abundant defect. Yet Pd vacancies are about 4.6 times more numerous (Extended Data Fig. 1).
   - The dipole moment is quoted as "0.14 × 10⁻¹² C m" per Pd atom (p. 1554). That is about 10¹⁶ times larger than an atomic-scale dipole (e·Å ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻²⁹ C m), so it must be a unit error. Because the dipole is the only link between DFT and the model, the correct value, the method used to obtain it (Bader charges, plane-averaged density difference, etc.) and the corresponding values for the Pd vacancy and the other candidate defects all need to be given.
   - The authors should show that the dipole strength V0 = 0.24 eV and the geometry (±2a with a = 0.25 nm) used in Fig. 4e follow from the DFT charge distribution rather than being chosen to reproduce the observed energies.

B. Should address

5. Uniqueness of the signature with respect to other defect-state mechanisms. Before concluding that the "charge-exciton YSR scenario is most plausible", the alternatives should be ruled out with data:
   - Ordinary donor- or acceptor-like states of a Pd(Ta) antisite with an effective-mass hydrogenic envelope. The ~5–6 nm extent is comparable to an effective Bohr radius in a narrow-gap semiconductor.
   - Tip-induced ionization of the defect. Charging rings or peaks in STS often appear at defects in narrow-gap materials and shift with setpoint.
   - Resonances involving the spectator band VB2.

   In particular, the authors should show grid spectroscopy at more than one setpoint for an unperturbed gap, to exclude tip-induced charging, and should explain why a Ta-site defect bound state should have the ~5–6 nm envelope with lattice-periodic modulation seen in Extended Data Fig. 3a.

6. The strained-stripe null result rests on very few defects and on a different local structure. The claim that "the in-gap states originate from the coupling of the defects with the excitonic order" (p. 1553) relies on "a few non-trivial defects" inside one stripe (Fig. 3b,d: one each of α, β, β′).
   - At 2.4% strain the defect's structural relaxation and charge distribution may themselves change.
   - In a 30 meV pseudogap, broadened in-gap states may simply be unresolvable rather than absent.

   The authors should state how many defects were measured in strained regions and in how many stripes. They should also show that the defect topography and the DFT dipole are unchanged under 2.4% strain, and estimate the energy resolution needed to see the predicted bound states inside a 30 meV pseudogap.

7. The observations do not agree fully with the stated trends. The main text says that in the pseudogap regime "the in-gap states disappear or are greatly reduced in intensity" for "all non-trivial defects". Extended Data Fig. 8 instead notes that for α′ "an in-gap state appears within the pseudogap in the quantum contact regime, whose origin is not understood yet". In addition, Fig. 3g shows the five defect types with different E+ and E− at ΔZ = 0, but the model offers no explanation of the defect-to-defect differences. The authors should discuss the α′ anomaly in the main text. They should also test whether the energy differences among α, β and γ (for example, γ being subsurface) correlate with the computed dipole or screening for each configuration. That would be a quantitative test of the model.

8. Statistics and reproducibility.
   - How many defects of each type were measured spectroscopically, and what is the spread of E+ and E− within a type?
   - How many cleaves, crystals and tips were used?
   - Were the gap-tuning sequences (Fig. 3e, Extended Data Fig. 8) repeated on several nominally identical defects, and with tip approach and retraction (hysteresis)?

   A histogram of E± per defect type, together with the local gap edges, would substantiate the "correlation" stated in the abstract.

9. Mismatch in the gap value and normalization of spectra.
   - ARPES gives a gap of about 100 meV at 80 K (Fig. 1d). STS at the normal setpoint gives an "apparent gap" of about 140 meV that decreases to 60 meV on tip approach (p. 1554). Part of the difference may be tip-induced band bending already present at ΔZ = 0, which bears directly on concern 2.
   - Most spectra are "normalized dI/dV", but the normalization procedure is not described. In constant-height ΔZ sweeps, normalization by setpoint or by I/V can distort apparent peak positions near band edges.

   Please specify the normalization, provide raw spectra for at least one ΔZ series, and discuss the 100 vs 140 meV discrepancy.

10. The spatial-extent argument is only semi-quantitative.
    - The text quotes an estimated 6.5 nm, "agreeing well with Fig. 1g". The Fig. 2 caption gives about 5 nm, and Extended Data Fig. 3 gives envelopes up to about 6 nm.
    - The Fermi velocity and gap used for the estimate should be stated.
    - The broadening with increased current in Extended Data Fig. 9 is shown only as colour maps at fixed bias (−35 and +40 meV). Because the in-gap energies move with current, maps at fixed bias sample different parts of the resonance. Increased current also changes the tip-induced potential footprint.

    Please extract the decay length at the in-gap peak energy for each setpoint and plot it against the measured gap, alongside the ξ ∝ v_F/Δ prediction.

11. The asymmetry of E+ and E− is reproduced only with extra free parameters. Fig. 4g introduces a five-Gaussian charge distribution and different hoppings (t_c = 1.0 eV, t_v = 0.5 eV) to obtain the asymmetry. The two-dimensional square lattice also ignores the quasi-one-dimensional chain anisotropy evident in all maps. The authors should state which parameters are fitted and which are fixed by DFT or ARPES. They should also show that the asymmetry and the anisotropic spatial shape follow from realistic band parameters, for example a tight-binding model fitted to the DFT or ARPES bands of Fig. 1d.

C. Minor

12. In Fig. 3g the vertical axis is labelled "Bias (mV)" with tick values ±0.05 and ±0.10. It should be V, or the values should be in mV.

13. In the Methods, the field "on the order of 10 MV" lacks a length unit, presumably MV cm⁻¹ or MV m⁻¹.

14. In the Methods, the lattice constants are attributed to ref. 48, which is a quantum spin Hall paper. The structural reference is ref. 37.

15. The caption of Extended Data Table 1 contains "rich PD". The formation-energy columns should state explicitly the chemical potentials used for each condition.

16. Fig. 4 caption and Methods: the units of V0 should be stated consistently, and the charge-distribution parameters for Fig. 4e versus 4g should be given in one place, not split between text and Methods.

17. The use of the terms "pseudogap" and "preformed excitons" for the strained and close-tip regimes (p. 1553) is suggestive but untested here. They should be either supported (for example by temperature dependence) or softened.

18. Extended Data Fig. 2 contains identical top and bottom images. Merging them with dual-colour markers would avoid redundancy.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add temperature-dependent STS of E± and the gap edges on the same defects. This is the most direct test linking the bound states to the excitonic order parameter, and it is independent of tip-induced artefacts.
2. Extend the theory to a self-consistent excitonic mean field around the impurity. Contrast it with a fixed-hybridization insulator with identical bands, and state which measured observable distinguishes the two.
3. Quantify tip-induced band bending: use an electrostatic model or reference measurements on pristine areas, and plot gap-edge positions and E± against the midgap. This will separate band bending from gap reduction in Fig. 3g.
4. Provide a quantitative defect-by-defect comparison. Measure E± and decay length statistically for α, α′, β, β′ and γ, and compare them with the corresponding DFT dipole or screening values, so that the model is tested beyond a qualitative level.
