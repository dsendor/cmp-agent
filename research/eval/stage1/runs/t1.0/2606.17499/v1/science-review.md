A. Must address

1. The field-reversal SP-STS does not separate tip-spin contrast from field effects on the sample or from set-point artifacts (Figs. 4f, 5k,l). The tip magnetization is reversed by reversing the external out-of-plane field (+1 T, −1 T, +1 T in Fig. 4f; ±1.2 T in Fig. 5k), and each spectrum is recorded in that same field. Several effects other than spin contrast could produce the differences. First, at ±1 T Fe(Te,Se) is in the mixed state, with a vortex spacing of roughly 45–50 nm. Vortices may relocate after each reversal, may pin at the very defects studied, and a vortex a few tens of nm away changes the in-gap DOS through supercurrent (Doppler) shifts, quasiparticle leakage and nearby vortex-core states. Trapped flux, field history and the tip's own response could do the same. Second, if the impurity moments responsible for the in-gap states are paramagnetic and follow the field, tip and sample reverse together and no contrast is expected even for a strongly spin-polarized state. Likewise, Ref. [59], cited for MBS spin polarization, gives a vortex-MBS spin set by the field direction, so a field-induced MBS would also reverse with the tip. Conversely, the contrast at the bend and at P1 requires the impurity moments to be pinned (anisotropy or coupling), which is never shown. Third, the spectra are stabilized at −10 mV / −300 pA (Fig. 4f) or −40 mV (Fig. 5) with a spin-polarized tip. Any spin polarization of the integrated LDOS between 0 and V_stab changes the tip height between polarities and rescales the whole spectrum, which can create an apparent bias-dependent "polarization", including a sign change and a shifted crossing energy. At position 3 of Fig. 4f the two traces differ by a nearly rigid offset across the whole ±2 mV window, which points to exactly this. Finally, the Methods say the Cr tip is "dipped" into Fe on W(110) to make it switchable, but it is not shown in the main text that the tip reverses between ±1 T, that its switching field is well below 1 T, or that it does not switch at lower fields during a sequence (Figs. S7/S8 were not available). Since the claim that the bend and P1 states are spin-polarized YSR states is the central new result, the authors should: repeat the identical field sequence at the same atomic positions with a non-magnetic (W or PtIr) tip, or with an uncoated Cr tip; repeat it with the same SP tip on a defect-free bulk-gap reference area, where no contrast is expected, and on a known Fe-adatom YSR state; image vortex positions (low-magnification zero-bias maps) at each field step, or confirm no vortex lies within a few coherence lengths; analyze set-point dependence by repeating at several V_stab or normalizing by the conductance outside the gap, and show full-range spectra for both polarities with background and coherence peaks overlapping; state whether the impurity moments are expected to be pinned or field-following and what each scenario (YSR or MBS) then predicts for the contrast; and summarize the tip switching field and polarization in the main text.

3. The abstract's claim for step edges and wrinkles is not backed by spin-resolved data. The abstract states that "by using spin-polarized scanning tunneling spectroscopy, we demonstrate that the near-zero-energy localized states on step edges and line defects originate from topologically trivial Yu-Shiba-Rusinov states." SP-STS is shown only at the Type II bend (Fig. 4f) and at the two subsurface sites (Fig. 5k,l). The step-edge assignment (Fig. 2) rests on non-spin-resolved line spectra and a toy model, and the Type I wrinkle assignment (Fig. 3a–d) rests on one cut showing "two peaks with symmetrical energy", a generic signature, with no field or spin dependence. The YSR (magnetic) assignment is not unique: in a sign-changing s± superconductor, nonmagnetic scatterers, including edges and structural defects, can also bind in-gap states, so the defining test of magnetism is exactly the spin- or field-resolved measurement that is missing. The "unpaired electrons" step-edge model of Fig. 1j is likewise unsupported by any measurement. The authors should either add SP-STS (with the controls of concern 1) and field-dependent spectra (0–3 T) at the step-edge ZBP (path B, point 0, Fig. 2a) and at the wrinkle hotspot (white box, Fig. 3a,b,d), or restrict the abstract and Discussion so that the step-edge and wrinkle assignments are stated as "consistent with" trivial states and explicitly as inferences from spatial structure and models.

The conclusion that the defect-free-region ZBPs "cannot be attributed to the presence of Majorana bound states" (abstract) and that "none of them can be explained by MBS" (Discussion) is not supported, especially for point 2. For P1 the argument is the bias-dependent shift along line C, which appears only in the last ~2 nm of the line (yellow region, Fig. 5i,j). The P2 peak does not disperse, so the case rests entirely on the absence of a field response in Fig. 5l: changes of a few percent, no error bars, and a field ramp (0, −0.2, −0.6, −1.2, +0.2, +0.6, +1.2 T) different from P1's ±1.2 T reversals, so the two cannot be compared and the small-field steps may lie below the tip's switching field. The authors themselves note (p. 10) that a deeper defect reduces spin sensitivity and that "other possible explanations … may exist", so a null spin result is not discriminating when the sensitivity is unknown; moreover, as noted in concern 1, a field-induced MBS whose spin follows the field would also give no contrast. The paper also does not address the proposal that interstitial Fe in Fe(Te,Se) can host zero-energy modes without a vortex ("quantum anomalous vortex" scenarios; cf. Ref. 47), for which a magnetic subsurface impurity is the expected host rather than evidence against MBS. The authors should measure P2 with the same ±1.2 T reversal protocol as P1, repeated several times, with full spectra at each field; quantify the expected MBS spin polarization at P2 for the measured tip polarization so that a null result can be judged against a definite detection threshold; discuss the anomalous-vortex scenario; and bring the abstract and Discussion in line with the cautious statement on p. 10.

5. There is no positive control, so no discriminating criterion is demonstrated. The title promises to distinguish Majorana zero modes from trivial states, but the paper shows only cases it classifies as trivial, and the criterion changes from site to site: peak splitting (step edge), spatial localization along the defect (wrinkle), a phase shift ≠ π plus SP sign reversal (bend), and dispersion and SP response (Fig. 5). Without a measurement of what a candidate MBS looks like in SP-STS with the same tip and protocol, the reader cannot judge whether the method would ever return a "Majorana" verdict. The obvious reference is a vortex-core ZBP in the same sample, which the authors compare with only at the level of linewidth (Ref. [58]). The authors should add SP-STS on a vortex-core ZBP (and on a known Fe-adatom YSR state), or at minimum state the criteria explicitly as predictions for YSR versus MBS, apply them uniformly to every defect class and tabulate the outcome. If neither is possible, the title should be revised.

6. The step-edge "splitting" is at or below the energy resolution, and the toy-model "fit" is partly circular (Fig. 2d–f). The model uses Lorentzians at fixed ±0.14 mV, a separation of 0.28 mV, whereas at T = 1.1 K the thermal broadening alone is about 3.5 k_BT ≈ 0.33 mV FWHM, before lock-in broadening and any electron-temperature excess. The claim that the ZBP "splits" into two YSR peaks (first 6 spectra of Fig. 2d) and that "the ZBP at the step edge results from a summation of two YSR peaks" is therefore an interpretation of peak shape and weight, not a resolved splitting. Fig. 2f is called a fit ("to get the best fit, we used two different coupling strengths for spectra 1–2 and 3–6"), yet the caption says the peaks are fixed at ±0.14 mV with heights "proportional to the first 6 points shown in e"; the model thus reproduces the measured intensities by construction, the two descriptions are inconsistent, and the measured spectra are never overlaid on the model. The argument from "spectral weight transfer from negative to positive energy" (p. 6) is not unique to YSR, since asymmetric weight can come from tip DOS, set-point normalization or a nearby distinct state, and in Fig. 2e the ±0.14 mV traces appear to decay together. The authors should state the effective energy resolution (including V_osc and electron temperature, for example from the gap edge of a known reference); fit the measured lineshapes with resolution-convolved models, comparing a single zero-energy peak with two split peaks using a goodness-of-fit criterion, and show whether the peak width or shape changes significantly along path B; give all model parameters (J, impurity positions, broadening), how many were fitted, and overlay data and model; clarify the spectral-weight argument; and, ideally, check another step edge.

7. The arguments against Andreev bound states and topological edge states are not sound (p. 6). The statement that Fe(Te,Se), as an s±-wave superconductor, has "no quasiparticles inside the gap causing Andreev reflection" is not a valid exclusion: Andreev reflection does not require in-gap quasiparticles, and in a sign-changing s± state, scattering at a surface or step that mixes electron and hole pockets, or at nonmagnetic defects, can itself bind in-gap Andreev states. The requirement that Andreev bound states "require two reflection walls" applies only to a particular confined geometry; single-surface bound states exist for sign-changing order parameters. Excluding topological edge states because peaks are seen "instead of an enhanced DOS within the whole superconducting gap" overlooks finite-size effects and the geometry here, a 1.23 nm step of two triple layers rather than a true sample boundary. Since Andreev surface states are a real competitor at step edges, the authors should remove or correct these arguments and support the exclusion with data or calculations, for example the dependence on step height or edge orientation across several edges, or a test that discriminates magnetic from nonmagnetic in-gap states (Zeeman shift or splitting, SP-STS).
