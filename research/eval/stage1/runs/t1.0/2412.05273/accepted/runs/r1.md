Review outline:

Note on scope: only the main article was available to me. Several key analyses are deferred to Supplementary Notes 1–8, which I could not see. Where a concern may already be answered there, I say so; the main text should still carry the evidence that the headline claims rest on.

1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript reports magnetotransport in MBE-grown Cd3As2 films (210–560 nm) with low-field Hall densities of 2.9–23 × 10^16 cm^-3. It argues that shoulder-like features in ρyx(H) and lump-like features in ρxx(H) are signatures of the 3D quasi-quantum Hall effect (QQHE), and that these signatures are increasingly masked by screened Coulomb disorder as the Fermi level is lowered. If the result holds, it gives a useful practical message for the field: lowering the carrier density to reach the quantum limit also weakens the screening of charged defects, so there is an optimum density rather than "lower is better". This framing of QQHE visibility in terms of eV0/EF is new for Cd3As2 and could guide materials work. However, the identification of the features as QQHE and the split of ρxx into "QQHE" and "Coulomb disorder" parts both rest mainly on phenomenological fitting, which limits how strongly the conclusions are established.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- A carrier-density series in a single material system (Samples A–F), all measured with the same protocol, reaching densities low enough for the quantum limit to fall at a few tesla.
- A high-field measurement (Sample D, up to 31.5 T at 0.4 K, Fig. 4k) showing a final ρyx plateau, with a quoted value of 155 mΩ cm compared against the k·p prediction.
- A k·p Landau-band calculation of σxy and σxx for Cd3As2 (Fig. 1e,f; Methods), which the authors describe as the first of its kind for this material.
- A clearly stated and testable conceptual framework (Fig. 4m: eV0 versus EF, with a "visible QQHE" region) linking defect screening to QQHE visibility, together with concrete suggestions (gating, passivation, lowering the defect density).
- The 2D-type scaling ρxx ∝ H ∂Hρyx describes Sample E strikingly well (Fig. 4e,j), which is suggestive.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. **The ρyx plateau is compared with theory in a regime where ρyx is not set by σxy.** Fig. 4k, Sample D: at 25–31.5 T, ρxx is roughly 700–850 mΩ cm while |ρyx| is about 150–250 mΩ cm, so ρxx/|ρyx| is about 3–5 and tan θH is well below 1. The QQHE quantization is a statement about σxy = (e²/πh) kF,z (Fig. 1, Methods). The relation ρyx ≈ 1/σxy, which links the measured ρyx to that prediction, needs σxy ≫ σxx. The authors themselves say Coulomb scattering "spoils" this approximation (Introduction). Under these conditions, agreement of the measured ρyx with πh/(e² kF,z) is not the expected outcome of QQHE, and could be coincidental. Low-field tan θH is also only about 3–4 (Fig. 3c,f).
   - Invert the tensor and plot σxy(H) = ρyx/(ρxx² + ρyx²) and σxx(H) for Sample D, and for Samples A–E up to 14 T.
   - Compare σxy in the quantum limit with e²kF,z/(πh), giving kF,z and its uncertainty (from n and the known anisotropy of the Cd3As2 Fermi surface, or from the SdH frequency where available).
   - If σxy is not plateau-like, the claim of an "excellent agreement" final QQHE plateau must be withdrawn or restated.

2. **The ρyx and ρxx features are not uniquely tied to QQHE.** For Samples A–C, the evidence is a change of slope in ρyx near ~5 T (Figs. 3e, 4f–h) together with a coincident lump in ρxx. Several alternatives are dismissed only qualitatively (Results, p. 3):
   - Parallel conduction from a surface or interface accumulation layer, or from the ZnxCd1−xTe buffer. A low-mobility second channel produces a nonlinear Hall slope and a kink in FMR without any quantization. The authors' argument against a two-band model ("nonmonotonicity is typically not observed") is not a quantitative test.
   - Magnetic freeze-out or field-induced changes in the effective carrier number in the quantum limit, which are known for low-density Dirac materials (ref. 38).
   - Zeeman-split Landau-band crossings.

   What the authors should do:
   - For each sample, compute the quantum-limit field expected from n (with the anisotropic Fermi surface and g-factor) and show that the ρyx shoulder and ρxx lump sit at that field, scaling as n^(2/3).
   - Show that the shoulder value of ρyx (or better σxy, see concern 1) follows kF,z for each sample.
   - Show a two-carrier fit of ρxx and ρyx together and explain why it fails.
   - The thickness series already exists (210–560 nm). Show whether the features scale with thickness, as a surface or interface channel would predict.

3. **Equation 1 has too many free and fixed parameters to separate the "QQHE" and "Coulomb disorder" contributions.** Equation 1 has ρ0, α, β, γ and δ free per sample, plus a crossover function with Hc = 9.6 T and w = 1.5 T fixed globally with no physical justification given in the main text. Problems:
   - α is "determined from a fit, often matching the size of the lump-like feature" (p. 4). The QQHE contribution is therefore tuned to the feature it is meant to explain.
   - Hc = 9.6 T falls inside the measured range of every sample, so it shapes the fitted curve rather than being tested by it.
   - With only Samples A–D fitted (Fig. 4l), the trends in β and γ with n could simply reflect the growing MR at lower n, whatever the mechanism.

   What the authors should do:
   - Give uncertainties and parameter correlations for α, β, γ and δ.
   - Show fit residuals.
   - Show how the results depend on Hc and w.
   - Compare against at least one alternative background (for example a two-band or guiding-center linear-MR form plus the same QQHE term).
   - Most importantly, test whether β, γ and δ agree quantitatively with ref. 25, using the independently estimated eV0 and EF. The claim that "as theoretically predicted, Coulomb disorder is an essential ingredient" (Abstract) currently rests on qualitative agreement.

4. **The eV0/EF estimates, and hence the "strong Coulomb disorder" classification, rest on an assumed defect density.** Fig. 2 assumes a charged-defect concentration of about 10^18 cm^-3, the same for all samples. That is two orders of magnitude above n, which implies heavy compensation, but no evidence for it is shown. The mobilities in Fig. 2c are non-monotonic in n (A 1.10, B 0.67, C 1.52, D 1.01, E 1.22 × 10^4 cm²/Vs), and the thicknesses differ (210–560 nm). Both suggest that defect content varies from sample to sample, which undermines the "average concentration" assumption. The Methods also do not say how n was tuned (growth conditions? extrinsic doping, ref. 62?). This bears directly on whether the series isolates EF as the only variable.
   - State the tuning parameter for each sample.
   - Give an independent estimate of the disorder potential: for example the Dingle temperature or quantum lifetime from the SdH data, the transport-to-quantum lifetime ratio (large for long-range Coulomb scattering), or the n-dependence of mobility.
   - Propagate a realistic range of defect density into Figs. 2b and 4m as error bars.
   - The pentatelluride point in Fig. 4m ("a few meV") needs the same treatment or should be marked as illustrative.

5. **The theory used to motivate plateaus is for fixed chemical potential; at fixed carrier density, it shows no plateaus.** The Methods state that "for fixed charge density calculations, the 3D QQHE plateaus disappear and one recovers the (quasi)classical dependence". A thin film with a fixed number of carriers is closer to the fixed-n case unless the Fermi level is pinned by a reservoir: charged defects, surface or interface states, or the buffer. This is central to whether QQHE plateaus should appear in these samples at all. The Fig. 1e,f calculations also use only a phenomenological broadening η and do not include the Coulomb disorder potential the paper is about.
   - State which reservoir pins μ in these films and give evidence for it.
   - Show the fixed-n calculation alongside the data.
   - Ideally, add a disorder-averaged calculation with a screened Coulomb potential of the estimated eV0, so the comparison with Fig. 4 becomes quantitative rather than visual.

B. Should address

6. **Symmetrization and contact-misalignment admixture are not described.** Fig. 4a–e shows raw ρxx for H < 0 only, and the Methods do not say whether ρxx was symmetrized and ρyx antisymmetrized. With 6-contact Hall bars and ρyx comparable to or larger than ρxx at high field, a small longitudinal offset of the Hall contacts mixes ρyx into ρxx (and vice versa). This can create or distort the "lumps" that Eq. 1 attributes to H ∂Hρyx.
   - Show ρxx(+H) and ρxx(−H) together for each sample.
   - State the symmetrization procedure.
   - Show that the lump persists in the symmetrized data.

7. **Temperatures are mismatched across the analysis.** Fig. 3 is at 2 K, Figs. 4a–c and 4e at 5 K, Sample D in Fig. 4d at 0.4 K, and Fig. 4k at 0.4 K. The fits in Fig. 4l compare Sample D at 0.4 K with the others at 5 K, while LB broadening and the visibility of the features are temperature dependent (Fig. 3d–f; Supplementary Note 4).
   - Fit all samples at a common temperature.
   - Show how α, β and γ depend on temperature for at least one sample. If the lump is QQHE, α should fall with T as the features smear, while the disorder terms should be less sensitive.

8. **There is one device per density, with no reproducibility evidence.** Each carrier density appears to be represented by a single Hall bar, and the key trend (Fig. 4l) rests on four points. Device-to-device spread from the same wafer, and repeat cooldowns, would show whether the ρyx kink and the fitted parameters are reproducible. Without them, wafer-specific inhomogeneity cannot be ruled out. Please state the number of devices measured per film and show a second device for at least one low-density sample.

9. **The low-field Hall density may be biased by puddles.** In the strong Coulomb disorder regime the paper invokes (eV0 ≳ EF), charge puddles, possibly including hole puddles near the Dirac point, make the low-field Hall slope an unreliable measure of n. The extracted EF values (Fig. 2c) and the plateau prediction depend on this. Please cross-check n against the SdH frequency where oscillations are visible (Supplementary Note 6), and discuss how puddling affects n_2K, EF and kF,z.

10. **The claim that the 2D scaling relation "works remarkably well" for Sample E is not quantified.** Fig. 4e,j, p. 4. Please give the fitted α and the residual for Sample E. Explain why the scaling relation, which in 2D rests on specific disorder-averaging arguments (ref. 28), should hold in a 3D system with dispersive Landau bands.

11. **Weyl-orbit and surface-state transport are dismissed only on the basis of thickness.** Discussion, p. 6. Weyl-orbit and surface-related quantum oscillations have been reported in Cd3As2 structures of comparable thickness (refs. 31–35). An angle-dependent measurement (tilting the field away from (001)) would separate a 3D QQHE, which follows the 3D Fermi surface, from 2D surface contributions, which follow the perpendicular field component. Please add this, or at least the angular dependence of the ρyx kink for one sample.

C. Minor

12. **Sign of the scaling relation.** The text on p. 4 writes ρxx = −α H ∂Hρyx, while Eq. 1 uses +α H ∂Hρyx. Please make the sign convention consistent, given that ρyx is negative for electrons.

13. **Figure 4 caption.** It refers to "e–h Hall resistivity", but the Hall panels are f–j. The Discussion refers to "Fig. 4j" for the pentatelluride disorder estimate, which is in Fig. 4m.

14. **Missing per-sample quantities.** Please add to the Fig. 2c table, for each sample, the expected quantum-limit field, kF,z, the predicted final plateau value, the thickness and the measurement temperature. This would let readers check the QQHE assignment directly.

15. **Unclear sentence on δ and α.** The sentence "We find similar values of δ ∼ 0.32 T^-1/2 for Samples A–D. The QQHE scaling factor lies between α ∼ 0.16–0.35" is easy to misread. Please list the fitted parameters with uncertainties in a main-text table rather than only in Supplementary Note 3.

16. **Unlabelled temperature traces in Fig. 3.** The legends list 2, 20, 40 and 80 K, but the traces are hard to tell apart, and the "Remnants … visible up to 80 K" claim cannot be read from the figure. Please add an inset or derivative plot.

17. **Fig. 1e "weak disorder" trace.** Please state the η values used for "weak" and "moderate" disorder, and how they relate to the measured mobilities.

18. **Data availability.** Data are available on request only. Depositing the transport data and fit code would help readers reproduce the Eq. 1 decomposition.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. **Recast the central analysis in conductivity.** Plot σxy(H) and σxx(H) for all samples, and test QQHE quantitatively: σxy in the quantum limit against e²kF,z/(πh), and the quantum-limit field against n^(2/3). This is the most direct way to show that the features are QQHE and not a multiband or freeze-out effect.
2. **Pin down eV0 independently.** Use quantum-versus-transport lifetimes from the SdH data, and a defect-density estimate (for example from compensation analysis or the growth conditions) for each sample. Put uncertainty bands on Figs. 2b and 4m.
3. **Tune EF within a single device.** Use a gate (as the authors propose), or else a thickness, angle or temperature series in one device. This separates the Fermi-level dependence from sample-to-sample changes in defect content.
4. **Put the empirical decomposition on firmer ground.** Fit all samples at one temperature, report uncertainties and residuals, justify or vary Hc and w, and compare β, γ and δ quantitatively with the strong-disorder theory of ref. 25 using the independent eV0.
