## A. Must address

1. **The high-field ρyx plateau is compared with theory where ρyx is not set by σxy, and other quantum-limit mechanisms are not excluded (Fig. 4k, Results p. 5, Supplementary Note 7).** For Sample D at about 20–31.5 T and 0.4 K, ρxx rises to roughly 600–850 mΩ cm while |ρyx| sits near the claimed ρyx^FP ≈ 155 mΩ cm (about 150–250 mΩ cm), so ρxx/|ρyx| ≈ 3–5 and tan θH ≪ 1. Low-field tan θH is also only about 3–4 (Fig. 3c,f). The QQHE is a statement about σxy = (e²/πh) kF,z (Fig. 1, Methods), and identifying ρyx with πh/(e² kF,z) needs ρyx ≈ 1/σxy, i.e. σxy ≫ σxx. The Methods state this condition ("In the weak-disorder case, we always find σxy ≫ σxx"), and the Introduction says Coulomb scattering "spoil[s] the approximation ρxy ≈ σxy^-1". By the authors' own reasoning, agreement of ρyx with ρyx^FP in this regime is not the expected outcome of QQHE and could be coincidental. A rising ρxx with a flattening ρyx in the quantum limit is also the textbook behaviour of magnetic freeze-out or field-induced localization (cited by the authors for ZrTe5, ref. 38), or of two-carrier conduction (electron–hole puddles, the buffer) setting in at high field. The "excellent agreement" also depends on kF,z,0, which is taken from the k·p model at the Hall density with no uncertainty, and the predicted and measured numbers are not both given in the main text. The authors should: invert the tensor and plot σxy(H) = ρyx/(ρxx² + ρyx²) and σxx(H) for Sample D up to 31.5 T (and for Samples A–E up to 14 T); test whether σxy, not ρyx, plateaus at e²kF,z/(πh); give kF,z and its uncertainty (from the n uncertainty and the known Fermi-surface anisotropy, or from the SdH frequency where available) and hence the uncertainty on 155 mΩ cm, including the uncertainty from film thickness and Hall-bar geometry in converting resistance to resistivity; state the measured plateau value with its uncertainty and the predicted value in the main text; show the temperature dependence of the high-field ρxx to separate a freeze-out or insulating upturn from a QQHE plateau; and report whether the plateau appears in more than one device or cooldown. If σxy is not plateau-like, the claim of a final QQHE plateau in "excellent agreement" with theory must be withdrawn or restated.

2. **The sub-14 T ρyx kink and ρxx lump are not quantitatively tied to Landau-band depopulation (Figs. 3d–f, 4a–j; Results pp. 3–5; Supplementary Note 4).** For Samples A–E the central signature is "the step-like slope change in ρyx(H) visible in all Samples A–E below 14 T", read as "a broadened plateau at higher filling factor" (p. 5). No filling index is assigned, no expected plateau value or field position is given, and the field positions are not compared with the Landau-band crossing fields calculated for each sample's density or shown to scale with n as the 3D model predicts. The authors say the QQHE features "evolve from standard Shubnikov–de Haas oscillations", so without a quantitative criterion the feature cannot be told apart from the final SdH extremum or from any field-dependent change in the Hall slope. For each sample the authors should: (i) estimate kF,z from n, or better from SdH frequencies, using the known Fermi-velocity anisotropy and g-factor of Cd3As2; (ii) predict the fields at which the N = 1→0 (and N = 2→1) Landau bands depopulate, and show that the ρyx shoulder and ρxx lump sit there and scale as n^(2/3); (iii) predict the corresponding ρyx ≈ (h/e²)(π/kF,z)/ν (or better σxy) values and show that the shoulder value follows kF,z; (iv) overlay these on Figs. 3e and 4f–j, with a table of predicted against observed fields. A Landau-index fan plotting the kink field, the ρxx lump (e.g. ~5.7 T in Sample E) and the oscillation minima together would directly test the Landau-band origin.

3. **Multicarrier, parallel-conduction and freeze-out explanations are dismissed only qualitatively (Results p. 3).** The authors argue that two-band models cannot give a rapidly changing, nonmonotonic FMR or an abrupt Hall-slope change because these are "not typical"; this is not a quantitative test. At n ≈ 3×10^16 cm^-3 in a 210–560 nm film, the bulk sheet density is only ~10^12 cm^-2, comparable to typical interface-accumulation, surface-state or buffer sheet densities. Candidate channels include the ZnxCd1−xTe buffer or the GaAs interface, topological surface (Fermi-arc) states, and a depleted or accumulated surface layer, which matters especially because the films are uncapped (p. 6 notes that depositing dielectrics has been challenging). A low-mobility second channel, or one that freezes out or enters its own quantum limit at a few tesla, produces a nonlinear Hall slope, a shoulder in ρyx and a kink in FMR without any quantization. The paper's own picture also implies multi-carrier transport: when eV0 ≳ EF (Sample A has eV0/EF ≈ 1, Fig. 2b), charge puddles mean coexisting electron and hole regions. Magnetic freeze-out or field-induced changes in the effective carrier number in the quantum limit (ref. 38) and Zeeman-split Landau-band crossings are further alternatives. The authors should: show an explicit two-carrier (or mobility-spectrum) fit of ρxx and ρyx (or σxx and σxy) together for at least Samples A–C and explain where it fails; give the expected conductance of the buffer, ideally from a bare-buffer reference sample; and use the existing thickness series (Sample B 210 nm, others 350–560 nm) to test whether the features scale as bulk (3D) or sheet (2D) quantities.

4. **The theory that motivates plateaus assumes a fixed chemical potential; at fixed carrier density it shows no plateaus (Methods p. 7, Fig. 1e,f).** The Methods state that "for fixed charge density calculations, the 3D QQHE plateaus disappear and one recovers the (quasi)classical dependence", while Fig. 1e,f, which motivates the whole interpretation, fixes EF at 20 meV. A thin film with a fixed number of carriers is closer to the fixed-n case unless the Fermi level is pinned by a reservoir (charged defects, surface or interface states, or the buffer), the 3D analogue of the localized-state reservoir in the 2D QHE. Whether QQHE plateaus should appear in these samples at all therefore depends on an assumption the paper does not justify. The authors should identify the reservoir that pins μ in their films, give evidence for it and its expected capacity, and show the fixed-n calculation alongside Fig. 1e,f and the data in the main text.
Raised by: r1, r3

5. **The eV0/EF estimates, and hence the "strong Coulomb disorder" classification, rest on an assumed, sample-independent defect density (Figs. 2b, 4m; Supplementary Note 5).** eV0 is calculated "assuming an average concentration of charged point defects" of about 10^18 cm^-3, the same for all films. This is two orders of magnitude above n, implying heavy compensation, for which no evidence is shown. In Cd3As2 the electron density is set by native donors (Cd interstitials, As vacancies) and acceptors (Cd vacancies) (refs. 21, 62), so films spanning 2.9×10^16 to 2.3×10^17 cm^-3 must differ in defect population or compensation; highly compensated low-n films would sit much further into the strong-disorder regime than uncompensated ones. The mobilities in Fig. 2c are non-monotonic in n (A 1.10, B 0.67, C 1.52, D 1.01, E 1.22 × 10^4 cm²/Vs) and the thicknesses differ (210–560 nm), both suggesting sample-to-sample defect variation not captured by a shared concentration. The Methods also do not say how n was tuned (growth conditions, post-growth treatment, extrinsic doping as in ref. 62), which bears directly on whether the series isolates EF as the only variable. No measured quantity independently tracks eV0 or the Landau-band broadening. The authors should: state the tuning parameter for each sample; give an independent estimate of the disorder potential or N_imp per sample, e.g. the Dingle temperature or quantum lifetime from SdH data, the transport-to-quantum lifetime ratio (large for long-range Coulomb scattering), the mobility through Coulomb-scattering theory, the temperature scale over which the features fade, or defect spectroscopy, and show it tracks the calculated eV0/EF; explain the non-monotonic mobility within the picture; and propagate a plausible range of defect density into Figs. 2b and 4m as error bars.

6. **Equation 1 has too many free and fixed parameters to separate the "QQHE" and "Coulomb disorder" contributions (p. 4–5, Fig. 4a–d, 4l, Supplementary Notes 2–3).** Eq. 1 has ρ0, α, β, γ and δ free per sample, plus a Fermi-function crossover between the H² and H log(δ√H) regimes with Hc = 9.6 T and w = 1.5 T fixed globally without justification. α is "determined from a fit, often matching the size of the lump-like feature", so the QQHE term is tuned to the feature it is meant to explain. Hc falls inside the measured range of every sample, so it shapes the fitted curve rather than being tested by it, and it is not linked to any physical scale (the quantum-limit field, or the field where the magnetic length equals the disorder correlation length), although in the theory of ref. 25 the crossover should depend on n and eV0. The fits are shown only as gray curves on the positive-field half of each panel, with no residuals, uncertainties or parameter correlations; Fig. 4l has no error bars; only Samples A–D are fitted, and E is excluded by construction. The trend of β and γ with n (Fig. 4l) could simply reflect the growing MR at lower n, the change in mobility and Fermi-surface size, or classical MR scaling as ρ0(μH)² with non-monotonic μ and differing ρ0, so it is not independent evidence of a strong-to-weak disorder crossover. The Abstract's claim that "as theoretically predicted, Coulomb disorder is an essential ingredient" rests on qualitative agreement. The authors should: report uncertainties and correlations (especially between α and β/γ) and residuals; fit with Hc and w free, or show how the results depend on them and whether they stay constant or follow the expected scaling; show that β is not simply explained by ρ0μ²; compare against alternative backgrounds with matched parameter counts (classical two-band MR, guiding-centre linear MR of ref. 24, a polynomial) plus the same QQHE term; test whether β, γ and δ agree quantitatively with ref. 25 using each sample's independently estimated eV0, EF and mobility; and show that the conclusion that "the QQHE component comprises a larger portion … with increasing carrier density" survives these changes. Otherwise Eq. 1 should be presented as purely empirical.

7. **The 3D (bulk) character of the effect is assumed from thickness rather than shown for the samples carrying the claim (Discussion p. 6; Methods "Film growth"; Supplementary Note 6).** The paper distinguishes its result from 2D QHE and Weyl-orbit physics in thinner Cd3As2 by stating that all films are "well within the bulk-like thickness regime (>100 nm)". At n ≈ 3–6×10^16 cm^-3, however, EF ≈ 20–28 meV (Fig. 2c), while the confinement subband spacing πħv/d with v ~ 10^6 m/s is about 10 meV for Sample B (210 nm) and about 6 meV for 350 nm films (Samples D, F). Only a few kz subbands may be occupied, so the system may be closer to quasi-2D than the bulk k·p model assumes. Weyl-orbit and surface-related oscillations have been reported in Cd3As2 structures of comparable thickness (refs. 31–35), and surface states, which the paper notes accompany 3D QQHE, are excluded only by argument. The 3D Fermi-surface evidence comes from a film "of a slightly higher carrier density", not from Samples A–E. The authors should measure the field-angle dependence of the ρyx kink and ρxx lump (or oscillation frequencies) in at least one low-density sample (A–C): a 3D QQHE follows the 3D Fermi surface and kF,z along the field, whereas 2D subbands or surface channels follow the perpendicular field component. They should also estimate the subband spacing against EF and Landau-band broadening for each film, and explain why thickness (210–560 nm) has no visible effect in the series. Otherwise the 3D claim should be narrowed.

8. **The regime assignment contradicts the use of the strong-disorder expression (Figs. 2b, 4m; Eq. 1).** Fig. 2b and Fig. 4m place only Sample A near eV0 ≈ EF; Samples B–D lie below the eV0 = EF line (eV0/EF ≈ 0.6–0.9). 9. **Symmetrization and contact-misalignment admixture are not described (Methods; Figs. 3d, 4a–j).** The Methods do not say whether ρxx was symmetrized and ρyx antisymmetrized in ±H. Fig. 4a–e shows raw ρxx for H < 0 and fits for H > 0, which hides whether the data are field-symmetric, and the FMR curves in Fig. 3d appear slightly asymmetric. With 6-contact Hall bars (2 mm × 0.56 mm), FMR ≈ 900% and ρxx and ρyx comparable or ρxx ≫ ρyx, a small longitudinal offset of the Hall contacts mixes ρxx into ρyx and vice versa. This can create or distort both the ρxx "lumps" that Eq. 1 attributes to H∂Hρyx and the coincident ρyx kinks. The authors should state the symmetrization procedure, show ρxx(+H) and ρxx(−H) together for each sample, show that the lump and kink persist in symmetrized data, and explain any residual asymmetry in Fig. 3d.

10. **The Hall-derived density, EF and kF,z assume single-band transport and may be biased (Methods; Fig. 2c).** n is taken from the ρyx slope below 1 T and EF from n via the DOS. For Samples A–C the Hall response is visibly nonlinear, and the low-field slope depends on the field window if a second carrier is present. In the strong-disorder regime the paper invokes (eV0 ≳ EF), charge puddles, possibly including hole puddles near the Dirac point, or a parallel channel make the low-field Hall slope an unreliable measure of the electron density. The error propagates into EF, kF,z, ρyx^FP and the regime map of Figs. 2b and 4m. The authors should give uncertainties on n and EF, cross-check n against SdH frequencies where oscillations are visible (Supplementary Note 6), quantify how a plausible parallel channel or puddling would shift the Fig. 2c values and the 155 mΩ cm prediction, and propagate these into eV0/EF.
