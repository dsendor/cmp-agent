A. Must address

1. The dielectric tensor components come from an isotropic inversion that does not hold for a uniaxial crystal, and every headline number depends on it. Methods (p. 9), Supplementary Note 2 and the Fig. 2 caption say that ε_ab and ε_c were obtained "without any model fitting" from the two-phase isotropic formula ε = sin²θ[1 + tan²θ((1−ρ)/(1+ρ))²] at a single angle of incidence (70°). The justification given is that the result is "dominantly governed by the dielectric function along the s-polarization". That is not generally true. In the side-face geometry of Fig. S3 (c along s), r_s depends on ε_c, but r_p depends only on ε_ab, because the in-plane p direction and the surface normal both lie in the ab-plane. Ψ = |r_p/r_s| and Δ therefore mix both components. The well-known result for pseudo-dielectric functions (Aspnes, JOSA 70, 1275 (1980)) is that ⟨ε⟩ is weighted mainly toward the component along the intersection of the plane of incidence with the surface, not toward the s direction. On the top face, r_p also contains ε_c through the normal component; the effect is weaker there but not negligible near 3.8 eV, where |ε_ab| is very large. The mixing matters most exactly where the claims are made: near 3.78 eV, ε_ab1 swings from about +25 to about −20 and ε_ab2 reaches 47, so even a small admixture distorts n_c, k_c, Δn and Δk. The c-axis Ψ and Δ in Fig. S4 show features near 4.0–4.2 eV, right at the ab exciton, which suggests that ε_ab is leaking into the "c-axis" spectrum. Because |Δn|max = 3.67, |Δk|max = 5.21, n_ab,max = 5.66 and the |Δn| > 0.5 visible claim all come directly from these pseudo-dielectric functions, the systematic error could be large. The azimuthal alignment accuracy of c relative to s and the c-axis misorientation of the measured side face are also not given; a few degrees of miscut would mix ε_ab into ε_c. The phrase "without any model fitting" overstates the method, since the isotropic, semi-infinite, roughness-free inversion is itself a model, and here an inappropriate one. The authors should re-analyse both faces with the exact uniaxial Fresnel equations (Berreman/4×4), fitted jointly to both faces with a common ε_ab and ε_c (a point-by-point inversion is fine and needs no oscillator model); add several angles of incidence (e.g. 60°, 65°, 70°, 75°) and, on the side face, two azimuths (c along s and c in the plane of incidence); use generalized or Mueller-matrix ellipsometry to check that off-diagonal conversion is absent; verify the side-face orientation and miscut, for example by Laue on that face; show that the recovered ε_c no longer contains the 4 eV feature; and replace Fig. 2 with the corrected Δn(ω) and Δk(ω), reporting how |Δn|max, |Δk|max and n_ab,max change, with uncertainties.

2. The claims of "transparency across the entire visible spectrum" and of anisotropy "without optical loss across a broad spectral range" contradict the paper's own data. Fig. S6 gives ln α ≈ 10.5–11.8 between 2 and 3.3 eV, i.e. α ≈ 4×10⁴ to 1.3×10⁵ cm⁻¹ and an absorption length of roughly 0.1–0.3 µm. Fig. 2d gives k_ab ≈ 0.1–0.3 across the visible, and the crystals are black (Fig. 1b, Fig. S1). A material with these constants is opaque at any thickness relevant to a waveplate or polarizer. The paper handles this by defining the "transparent region" as the Urbach tail below the gap (p. 4; Fig. 3 caption), but an Urbach tail is absorption, not transparency. Moreover, the linear ln α from about 1 to 3.35 eV has a slope of order 1 eV⁻¹, i.e. a characteristic energy of order 1 eV, far from a genuine band-tail value (tens of meV); this points to broad extrinsic or defect-band absorption. The attribution to about 1.4% Cu²⁺ is taken from Ref. 18, not measured on these crystals, and the same defects may also change n in the visible. The definition also differs from how transparency is understood for the comparison materials in Fig. 3: rutile, calcite and LiNbO3 have α of order 10⁻² to 10⁰ cm⁻¹ there. Finally, ellipsometry on thick crystals has poor sensitivity to k ≲ 0.05 and cannot establish k at the 10⁻³–10⁻⁴ level relevant to transparency, so the true sub-gap loss is not determined in any case. The authors should measure polarized transmission (and ideally retardance) on thin polished, oriented a-cut and c-cut plates of known thickness to obtain α_ab(λ), α_c(λ) and Δn directly in the visible; measure the Cu²⁺ content of the measured crystals (EPR or XPS); show that the visible-range n_ab and n_c are intrinsic, for example by comparing crystals with different Cu²⁺ content, lighter-coloured or annealed crystals, and the thin-film/powder index of Ref. 16; and otherwise restate the claim, either by restricting it to "below the direct gap", by stating that these crystals absorb in the visible and intrinsic transparency is inferred, or by reframing it with a loss figure of merit such as Δn/k or Δn per absorption length. The abstract and the Fig. 3 caption do not follow from the data as they stand.

3. The binding energy of 1.34 eV and the "2D signature" Eb/Eg ≈ 1/4 rest on equating a single-oscillator Penn-gap parameter with the quasiparticle gap. In Supplementary Note 4 (Eq. 1, Fig. S7), Eg = 5.12 eV is the ω_p parameter of a one-oscillator (Penn/Wemple/Sellmeier) fit to ε_ab1 between 1.0 and 2.25 eV. That quantity is an average oscillator energy weighted over all transitions, here dominated by the 3.78 eV exciton itself; it is not the continuum onset and lies above the gap in essentially all semiconductors. The fit window is narrow and noisy, ε∞ and ω_p are strongly correlated, the window sits where the defect absorption of concern 2 is present, and it includes visibly noisy points near 1.1–1.25 eV, probably the detector-switching region of the M-2000. No uncertainty is quoted. Eb = ω_p − E1s and the placement of CuAlO2 on the 1/4 line in Fig. 4a then follow directly from this choice. Agreement with sc-GW (5.1 eV) and with one BSE value (1.2 eV, Ref. 13) is suggestive but not a measurement: the other BSE study (Ref. 12) gives 0.47 eV, and GW gaps for delafossites vary by more than 1 eV between references. The 1/4 relation is an empirical correlation for 2D materials and is not a test of dimensionality for a single compound; Fig. 4a itself places bulk MoTe2 and bulk CrSBr on the line, and many bulk materials with strong excitons would fall near it once Eg is defined this way. The authors should determine Eg independently, for example from the continuum onset in the 7 K spectra (Elliott-type fit of ε2 including the continuum, or second-derivative spectra), photoemission plus inverse photoemission or STS, or published GW+BSE; exclude or explain the noisy points in Fig. S7 and give error bars on ω_p, Eg and Eb; and drop or heavily qualify the inference from Fig. 4a.

4. The 2D-versus-3D hydrogenic comparison (Fig. 4c,d; Supplementary Notes 6–7) and the assignment of the broad 4.47 eV feature to 2s and higher Rydberg states do not establish that the exciton is two-dimensional. First, the models do not apply: with Eb ≈ 1.34 eV and ε ≈ 5, the Wannier radius is about 1 Å to a few Å, comparable to or smaller than the Cu–Cu spacing (2.85 Å), and the paper itself describes the transition as "intratom-like" Cu dz2 → Cu px/py, i.e. Frenkel or charge-transfer-like. Neither hydrogenic series is then valid without central-cell corrections. Second, the result comes from the screening construction, not from dimensionality: the pure 2D series of Eq. 3 would put 2s at Eg − Eb/9 ≈ 4.97 eV, closer to the gap than the 3D value (≈4.8 eV), so the 4.40 eV obtained in Fig. 4c comes entirely from the n-dependent effective screening ε_n of the EMA construction (taken from Ref. 44). That construction has ad hoc inputs: a Cu-layer thickness of 2.038 Å called "half the dumbbell", whereas Cu–O is about 1.86 Å; an Al2O3 reference ε of 2.7 at 0.74 eV, whereas sapphire has ε∞ ≈ 3.1 (n ≈ 1.75), and the reported 2.34 does not match 2.7 either; a choice between two EMA roots made by analogy; layer dielectric constants fitted at 0.74 eV that already contain the exciton's own oscillator strength; an unspecified reduced mass μ; and equal linewidths. The 3D comparison model is isotropic, with no screening and no anisotropic mass, so it is a straw man; the fair competitor is an anisotropic 3D, fractional-dimension (Ref. 41) or Frenkel-like model with the same number of free parameters. Third, the 4.47 eV hump is a single broad Lorentzian of uncertain origin: it could be the band-to-band continuum onset, an interband transition at another k-point (Fig. 5a shows several flat valence bands), a phonon or vibronic sideband, or Cu²⁺/defect-related absorption. Its integrated weight relative to 1s is not compared with the ideal 2D prediction (f2s/f1s = 1/27). In Fig. S8 it does not sharpen on cooling while the 1s narrows strongly, and no resolved 2s peak appears at 7 K, which argues against a Rydberg state. The authors should state μ and all fixed and free parameters of both models; show how the 2s position depends on the layer thickness, the EMA root and the ε inputs, and give the resulting ε_n used in Eq. (5); compare oscillator strengths quantitatively; show the 7 K versions of Fig. 4b–d and low-temperature second-derivative spectra resolving any Rydberg structure; test against an anisotropic 3D model; rely on a BSE calculation (concern 7) rather than hydrogenic models; and soften "conclusively demonstrate" (p. 6) to "is consistent with".

5. Spot footprint, facet geometry, surface condition and depolarization are not characterized, although they directly affect the extracted peak values. The crystals are 1–2 mm hexagonal pillars, and the side facets in Fig. 1b and Fig. S1 are narrower than that and look visibly stepped and textured rather than optically flat ("smooth and shiny" is not quantified). At 70° incidence the beam footprint is elongated about threefold. The paper does not state the M-2000 spot size, whether focusing probes were used, whether the footprint lies within each facet, or how light from the edges, the silver epoxy and the copper cone was excluded. Surface roughness, a Cu²⁺-rich or oxidized overlayer and spill-over all reduce and distort the apparent ε2 peak and n at high energy, and so change n_ab,max and Δn_max; no roughness or overlayer layer is included. The authors should report the spot size and footprint relative to each facet, AFM roughness of both faces, and the measured depolarization spectra (which the M-2000 provides) as an indicator of surface quality and overfilling; include a roughness/overlayer layer constrained by AFM, or show that one changes the results negligibly; and show reproducibility on a second crystal and a second side face.

6. The "highest reported to date" claims compare unlike quantities, and Fig. 3 partly contradicts the text. |Δn|max = 3.67 and |Δk|max = 5.21 occur at the exciton resonance (333 and 322 nm), where k_ab ≈ 5 and ε_ab1 changes sign. 7. The theory does not test the anisotropy or the mechanism quantitatively. Fig. 5 and the Discussion (pp. 6–7) show only GGA band projections and single-particle Bloch functions at one k-point (L); no momentum matrix elements and no computed ε_ab(ω) or ε_c(ω), even at the RPA level, are given. The statement that c-polarized transitions are "forbidden" is argued from atomic-orbital parity, not from the little group at L. An on-site d → p dipole transition is parity-allowed for both polarizations (dz2 → pz is allowed for E ∥ c), so the c-axis suppression depends on the energy ordering of pz versus px/py, which GGA may misplace; and with O p and Cu s admixture the z-polarized matrix element is generically non-zero. Fig. 5c shows only faint Cu px/py weight in the lowest conduction band along L–F, Fig. 5d shows sizable O px/py weight there, and Cu 4s, commonly reported to contribute to the delafossite conduction-band minimum, is not projected. GGA without +U may misplace the Cu 3d states, and the cited GW evidence concerns other works' band ordering, not this calculation's matrix elements. The conduction band in Fig. 5a also disperses appreciably along Z–Γ, which is not obviously compatible with a 2D-confined state. The Methods give the Cu 3p⁶3d¹⁰4s¹ valence configuration but state no spin-orbit or U settings. The primitive cell has 4 atoms, so GW+BSE is not "beyond the scope"; Refs. 12, 13 and 48 have already done it. The authors should compute polarization-resolved matrix elements |⟨v|p_x,y,z|c⟩|² along L–F and the ε_ab(ω), ε_c(ω) tensor (RPA/IPA or GGA+scissor at minimum, GW+BSE preferably); plot the exciton wavefunction and its extent along c and in-plane; report band masses and k_z dispersion of the relevant bands (which also gives μ for concern 4); compare peak positions and intensity ratios with Fig. 2 and with the published BSE spectra; and check whether +U or a hybrid functional changes the dz2/4s/O pz composition of the top valence band and the conduction-band character at L, given the pseudopotential sensitivity noted in Note 7 (Ref. 13).

8. The link between "two-dimensional confinement" and the anisotropy is asserted, not argued. A dipole selection rule (dz2 → px/py allowed for E ∥ ab, with pz pushed up by the dumbbell) produces polarization selectivity whatever the exciton's dimensionality; conversely, a 2D-confined exciton need not be polarization-selective. The title-level message ("enabled by ... two-dimensionally confined excitons") should make clear which ingredient carries the anisotropy. Ideally the authors would show, for example with a 3D anisotropic model having the same selection rule, that 2D confinement is needed to reach the measured Δn.
