Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper reports the principal optical constants of bulk delafossite CuAlO2 along the ab-plane and the c-axis, measured by spectroscopic ellipsometry on two facets of mm-sized single crystals. A sharp ab-polarized transition at 3.78 eV gives a peak birefringence |Δn| = 3.67 (333 nm), |Δn| > 0.5 across the visible, and a negative-ε_ab1 window between two ENZ points (about 3.8 and 6.2 eV) that the authors call a natural Type-II hyperbolic medium in the UV. GGA calculations assign the transition to a Cu dz²→px/y excitation, allowed only for in-plane polarization, in the linear O–Cu–O dumbbell. A three-dimensionally bonded oxide with this much anisotropy in the near-UV would be useful for UV polarization optics and UV hyperbolic photonics. The result is of broad interest if the anisotropic constants are quantitatively right, but the extraction method is the weak point of the paper.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- It is a direct, polarization-resolved measurement of both principal dielectric components of a wide-gap oxide on as-grown crystallographic facets, a dataset not previously available for CuAlO2.
- The anisotropy is very large and has a clear microscopic picture (orbital-projected bands and Bloch functions at L, together with the dipole selection rule).
- Normal-incidence reflectance on the (001) face, which depends on ε_ab alone, agrees with the ellipsometry-derived ε_ab up to about 4.1 eV. This is an independent consistency check.
- The benchmarking against other birefringent and hyperbolic materials (Fig. 2e, Fig. 3e,f) gives useful context, and the source data are deposited.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The principal components come from an isotropic inversion of a strongly anisotropic crystal. Methods, Eq. (1), and p. 2 ("obtained directly from the direction-selective ellipsometric parameter through a model-free transformation") describe ε_ab and ε_c as obtained by inserting Ψ and Δ, measured on each facet at a single 70° angle, into the isotropic two-phase pseudo-dielectric formula. For a uniaxial crystal, the p-polarized reflection coefficient on either facet depends on two tensor components:
   - on the (001) face, ε_ab in the plane and ε_c along the surface normal;
   - on the (100) face with c parallel to s, ε_ab in the plane and ε_ab along the normal.
   Ψ and Δ come from the ratio r_p/r_s. The pseudo-dielectric function from Eq. (1) is therefore not ε_c on the side facet, and not ε_ab on the top facet. Vanishing cross-polarization only removes off-diagonal Jones elements; it does not make r_p independent of the other component. The text says the measurement is "only weakly sensitive" to the second component and cites Ref. 8. Here the two components differ by up to Δn ≈ 3.7 and Δk ≈ 5.2, so that approximation must be checked quantitatively. It directly controls the headline values: |Δn|max, the n_ab peak, ε_c near 5.5 eV, and the ENZ positions.
   What the authors should do:
   - Refit both facets simultaneously with a proper uniaxial model (Berreman/4×4 or a standard anisotropic-substrate model), ideally at several angles of incidence (for example 55°, 65°, 75°) and with the side facet measured at two azimuths (c parallel and perpendicular to the plane of incidence).
   - Report the difference between the uniaxial result and the Eq. (1) pseudo-dielectric functions, and propagate it into the quoted Δn, Δk, ENZ energies and FOM.
   - Show that the point-by-point result is Kramers–Kronig consistent, or fit a KK-consistent oscillator model.

2. Sample geometry, spot size and reproducibility on the side facet are not documented. The side facet in Fig. 1b is of order a few hundred µm across (scale bar 100 µm), and the crystals are 1–2 mm long hexagonal pillars. A standard M-2000 beam at 70° incidence has an elongated footprint of millimetre order unless focusing probes are used. The paper does not give the spot size, whether focusing optics were used, the facet dimensions, or how overfilling, edge scattering and depolarization were excluded. If the beam overfills the side facet, light from adjacent facets of different orientation, and from the silver epoxy, would enter the c-axis data, which carry the whole anisotropy claim. The paper also does not say how many crystals and facets were measured, or whether ε_ab and ε_c (and hence Δn) reproduce from crystal to crystal. The authors should:
   - state the beam footprint relative to each facet, and report depolarization or Mueller-matrix depolarization index for the (100) facet;
   - show ε_ab and ε_c from at least two crystals, or several facets, with the spread.
   The Mueller-matrix validation in the Supplementary uses the (001) face and a facet of undetermined Miller index. The facet that most needs validating, the (100) side facet with c in-plane, should be included, with azimuthal rotation, so that the off-diagonal elements are shown to vanish only when c is aligned with s or p.

3. The "low-loss" description conflicts with the measured extinction coefficient and with where the headline birefringence occurs. The text on p. 3 gives k_ab ≈ k_c ≈ 0.07 at 1 eV. At λ = 1.24 µm this is an absorption coefficient α = 4πk/λ ≈ 7×10³ cm⁻¹, an attenuation length of about 1.4 µm. That is far from "low-loss" for the waveplates and retarders proposed on p. 5. Across the visible k is presumably similar or larger (Fig. 2b), and the crystals look black (Fig. 1b).
   The headline |Δn|max = 3.67 at 3.72 eV lies above the quoted 3.71 eV direct gap and on the flank of a resonance where k_ab reaches about 5. Its large size is the usual dispersive consequence of a strong absorption line, not birefringence in a transparent window. The comparison in Fig. 2e, and the claim that 1.66 at 370 nm is the largest among "lower-loss" materials, are made without plotting k or α for any material. Ellipsometry on thick absorbing crystals also cannot resolve k well below about 0.01, so the sub-gap loss is poorly constrained either way.
   The authors should:
   - quantify sub-gap loss with a method that has the sensitivity, such as polarized transmission through a thinned or polished plate of known thickness;
   - plot k (or α, or a birefringence figure of merit such as Δn/k) next to Δn in Fig. 2c,e;
   - define "low-loss" numerically;
   - separate the transparent-region claim (Δn at sub-gap wavelengths with known loss) from the resonant peak value. Abstract and title wording such as "low-loss birefringence" and "colossal visible birefringence" should be scoped to what that analysis supports.

B. Should address

4. The evidence for the hyperbolic window is weaker than presented.
   - The reflectance check in Fig. 3c covers only up to about 4.1 eV (300 nm cutoff). It confirms the onset of the high-reflectance band but not the band itself, and not the second ENZ point near 6.2 eV.
   - The loss function in Fig. 3d is computed from the same ellipsometric ε_ab, so it is not independent corroboration of the ENZ points.
   - The upper ENZ point at about 6.1–6.2 eV lies close to the 6.46 eV limit of the data, where ellipsometric accuracy is lowest and where Eq. (1)'s errors (concern 1) also act.
   - In Fig. 3a, ε_c1 has a pronounced dip near 5.5–5.7 eV that appears to approach, or cross, zero. If ε_c1 < 0 there, both components are negative and that sub-band is not hyperbolic. The authors should state the minimum ε_c1 with uncertainty and mark any such sub-interval.
   - ε_ab2 stays large across the window (FOM ≤ 1.91), so the window is strongly lossy. The authors should say so, and compare with hBN's FOM at its own band.
   Extending reflectance or transmission into the deep UV (VUV reflectance or synchrotron ellipsometry) would make the second ENZ point robust.

5. The Tauc analysis is applied to an excitonic line. The direct gap of 3.71 eV (Fig. 2d inset) is obtained by extrapolating (αħω)² over a narrow window that lies only about 70 meV below a sharp peak which the paper itself attributes to a strong exciton (p. 2, Refs. 17, 18). A parabolic-band Tauc law does not describe absorption dominated by an exciton plus a Cu²⁺-related Urbach tail, so the extracted value depends on the fit window. The 3.71 eV value is used to argue that the gap is wider than in earlier reports and that |Δn| = 1.66 at 370 nm is "below the gap". The authors should report the sensitivity of the gap to the fit window, compare with an Elliott-type fit (exciton plus continuum), and reconcile this with the indirect gap of 3.45 eV, which lies between 370 nm (3.35 eV) and the direct gap.

6. Surface roughness and overlayers are not included in the inversion. The facets are as-grown and the crystal is described as dark from Cu²⁺ defects. Eq. (1) assumes an ideal abrupt interface. Even a few nm of roughness or an oxidized or Cu-rich surface layer noticeably reduces the peak ε₂ and the peak n and k at a sharp resonance. That bears directly on the quoted n_ab,max = 5.66, |Δk|max = 5.22 and ε_ab2 ≈ 47. The authors should give AFM roughness for both facets and include a roughness (Bruggeman EMA) layer in the uniaxial fit, reporting how the peak values change. The agreement with reflectance in Fig. 3c below 4 eV is reassuring, but it does not reach the peak region.

7. The theory comparison is qualitative and the exciton is invoked but not calculated. The paper uses GGA and Wannier interpolation and states that it is not attempting to reproduce the spectrum quantitatively (p. 5). Two quantitative points are still needed to support "the dipole selection rule provides a microscopic origin":
   - the calculated ε_ab/ε_c anisotropy (ratio of oscillator strengths, or Δn below the edge) compared with experiment, shown in the main text;
   - a check of whether the intra-atomic dz²→px/y matrix element actually dominates the L-point momentum matrix element, as opposed to inter-site Cu–O contributions, for example by decomposing the momentum matrix elements in the Wannier basis.
   Because the main-text argument rests on a strong exciton (Refs. 17–19), the authors should either show a BSE spectrum for both polarizations or explain why the c-axis excitonic weight is suppressed by the same symmetry. A temperature-dependent measurement of the 3.78 eV peak would also help establish its excitonic character.

8. The benchmark comparison in Fig. 2e is not like-for-like. The comparison materials are selected by band gap above 1.55 eV and plotted only over each one's "low-loss" range, but CuAlO2 is plotted up to and through its absorption peak (star symbols mark sub-gap points, while the full curve runs to 200 nm). Every material's birefringence rises near its own absorption edge. A fair comparison would plot Δn against wavelength normalized to each material's gap, or Δn at a fixed absorption level. Claims such as "largest value among the UV–visible lower-loss anisotropic materials surveyed" (p. 3) and "largest Δε1 among the natural materials surveyed" (p. 4) should be restated with the comparison criterion made explicit.

9. The defect state of the measured crystals is assumed, not measured. The 1.4% Cu²⁺ concentration and the sub-gap absorption are attributed to Ref. 15 (p. 3, Fig. 1b caption). No compositional, XPS/EPR or Hall data are given for these crystals. Since the sub-gap k sets the practical loss, and free carriers or polaronic absorption from Cu vacancies could also add Drude-like or mid-gap contributions that differ between ab and c, the authors should characterize the defect density of the measured crystals and say whether the sub-gap k is anisotropic.

C. Minor

10. Reflectance normalization (Methods, p. 7): R is obtained as the ratio of sample to aluminium-mirror intensity. The authors should state whether this ratio was multiplied by the known reflectance of Al (about 0.9, and wavelength dependent). Otherwise the absolute R in Fig. 3c is biased, and the agreement in that figure depends on the choice.

11. The phrase "model-free transformation" (p. 2) is inaccurate: Eq. (1) is an isotropic semi-infinite two-phase model. The wording should be revised, which follows from concern 1.

12. The n_ab and k curves below about 1.5 eV (Fig. 2a,b) show oscillatory noise. Its origin should be explained (detector change-over, residual backside reflection, or low signal), and the quoted "|Δn| > 0.5 throughout the visible" should be checked against the 1.6–1.7 eV end of the visible range, where Δn appears close to 0.5.

13. The text (p. 2) states that |Δn| exceeds 0.5 "except for a narrow region at 3.86–3.9 eV", but Fig. 2c shows |Δn| approaching zero there, where n_ab crosses n_c. Giving that crossing energy explicitly would help device design (an isotropic point).

14. Raman measurements are described in the Methods and the author contributions but are not presented or discussed in the main text. Either show them (for example as a structural and orientation check of the facets) or remove them.

15. The Mueller-matrix comparison uses a facet "whose exact Miller index was not determined". Determining its orientation (by Laue diffraction) would turn that measurement into a quantitative test of the extracted tensor rather than a qualitative one.

16. Fig. 2d: the inset's vertical-axis units and the definition of α (ab-polarized only?) should be stated. Fig. 3f should state whether the FOM uses ε_ab only.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Re-derive the dielectric tensor with a full uniaxial multi-angle, multi-azimuth fit of both facets, including roughness, and propagate the resulting uncertainties into every headline number (Δn, Δk, ENZ energies, FOM).
2. Add a direct sub-gap loss measurement (polarized transmission through a thinned plate) and present birefringence together with loss, so that the "low-loss" claim has a number attached.
3. Extend an independent measurement (VUV reflectance or synchrotron ellipsometry) across 4–6.5 eV to confirm both ENZ points and the sign of ε_c1 near 5.6 eV.
4. Report results from more than one crystal and put the calculated ε_ab, ε_c anisotropy (ideally with BSE) next to the experimental spectra in the main text.
