Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The paper reports principal-axis optical constants of bulk delafossite CuAlO2 single crystals from spectroscopic ellipsometry on the (001) top face and a side facet. It claims a large birefringence (|Δn|max = 3.67 at 333 nm, |Δn| > 0.5 across the visible, 1.66 at 370 nm), a direct gap of 3.71 eV, and a Type-II hyperbolic window between ENZ points near 3.8 and 6.2 eV. A GGA/Wannier analysis assigns the anisotropy to an on-site Cu dz2 → px/y transition allowed only for in-plane polarization. If the principal-axis constants are right, this is a useful result: a three-dimensionally bonded, non-van der Waals oxide with UV-visible anisotropy comparable to the best layered materials, plus a natural UV hyperbolic band between those of MoOCl2 and hBN. The result depends almost entirely on how accurately a uniaxial tensor was extracted from small mm-scale facets. As presented, that extraction is the weakest part of the paper.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- Independent access to the ab-plane and c-axis responses on bulk single crystals, rather than on thin films or flakes, is valuable. Data for this compound in this range are scarce.
- The physical picture is simple and testable. A sharp in-plane-only transition at 3.78 eV gives a large Kramers-Kronig enhancement of n_ab below the transition, and a negative ε_ab1 above it. The D3d site-symmetry argument (a1g → eu allowed for E ⊥ c, forbidden for E ∥ c) is sound.
- Normal-incidence reflectance (Fig. 3c) gives a partly independent check of ε_ab up to about 4.1 eV.
- The paper benchmarks against a broad set of birefringent and hyperbolic materials (Figs. 2e, 3e, 3f) and reports a hyperbolic figure of merit, not just the sign of ε1.
- Source data are deposited.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. The principal components are extracted with an isotropic two-phase inversion (Eq. 1), which is not valid for this uniaxial crystal at 70° incidence. Methods and the Results paragraph on Fig. 2 state that "the measured ellipsometric response is dominated by the dielectric-function component parallel to the s-polarized electric field" and that the orthogonal component "can be neglected". Ellipsometry measures ρ = rp/rs, and rp at oblique incidence is not insensitive to the anisotropy.
   - On the (001) face, rp depends on both ε_ab and ε_c (the uniaxial Fresnel coefficient contains √(ε_ab ε_c) and ε_ab/ε_c terms).
   - On the side facet with c ∥ s, rs depends on ε_c, but rp depends on ε_ab, in-plane and along the surface normal.
   - Eq. 1 therefore returns pseudo-dielectric functions ⟨ε⟩, not principal components. The error is largest exactly where the claims sit: ε_ab is large (|ε| ~ 20–50) and ε_c small near 3.7–4 eV, where Δn_max, Δk_max and the lower ENZ point are reported, and again near the upper ENZ.
   - Δn, Δk, Δε1, the ENZ energies and the FOM all inherit this error. The Mueller-matrix comparison with a facet "whose exact Miller index was not determined" (Methods, SI) shows only that off-diagonal mixing is small. It does not show that the pseudo-dielectric equals the principal component.

   The authors should refit Ψ/Δ (or the full Mueller matrix) with a proper uniaxial bulk model, including a surface roughness/overlayer layer. They should fit both facets jointly, at several angles of incidence (for example 60°, 65°, 70°, 75°) and at two azimuths on the side facet (c ∥ s and c ∥ p). Then:
   - report the fit residuals;
   - report how much each headline number (Δn_max, Δn at 370 nm, Δk_max, both ENZ energies, FOM at 4.01 eV) changes relative to the Eq. 1 values;
   - show that ε_ab from the side facet (c ∥ p geometry) agrees with ε_ab from the (001) face. That is the most direct internal test.

2. The measurement geometry relative to crystal size is not documented, so the data may include light reflected outside the facet or depolarized light. Fig. 1b shows hexagonal pillars 1–2 mm long whose faces are a few hundred μm across (100 μm scale bar). A standard M-2000 beam at 70° projects to a spot of several mm unless focusing probes are used. The Methods give no spot size, focusing optics, alignment procedure or depolarization measurement. Several features fit partial overfilling, a tilted facet or depolarization:
   - the visibly noisy n_ab and ε_ab1 below about 2 eV in Figs. 2a and 3a;
   - the low n_c (about 1.6–1.9 in the visible, below that of Al2O3 for a Cu-bearing oxide; see concern 8);
   - the nonzero sub-gap k of about 0.07 for both polarizations.

   The authors should state the spot size and how it compares with each facet, whether focusing probes were used, and the measured depolarization versus photon energy for each facet. They should also show that the extracted ε does not change when the spot is moved or reduced on the facet.

B. Should address

3. The "low-loss" and "broadband low-loss birefringence" claims (abstract, Fig. 2e, conclusions) rest only on ellipsometric k, which is neither sensitive nor reliable at this level.
   - The paper reports k_ab ≈ k_c ≈ 0.07 at 1 eV. That corresponds to α ≈ 7×10³ cm⁻¹, and in the visible (about 500 nm) roughly 2×10⁴ cm⁻¹, an absorption length of about 0.5 μm. The crystals also look black (Fig. 1b).
   - With |Δn| ≈ 0.6–0.8, a visible half-wave retarder needs about 0.3–0.4 μm thickness and would absorb an appreciable fraction of the light. This is not low-loss in the sense used for LiNbO3 or calcite in Fig. 2e.
   - Reflection ellipsometry on a bulk crystal typically cannot resolve k below about 0.01–0.05, and a surface overlayer can produce spurious k.

   The authors should:
   - measure polarized transmission on thinned (001) and (100) slabs, or on oriented films, to give quantitative k_ab and k_c in the visible;
   - report k at the wavelengths highlighted in Fig. 2e, in particular at 370 nm, where |Δn| = 1.66 is claimed as the largest "lower-loss" value. 370 nm (3.35 eV) is only 0.1 eV below the stated 3.45 eV indirect gap and on the Urbach tail;
   - give a figure of merit such as Δn/max(k_ab, k_c), so the comparison in Fig. 2e is like for like.

4. The reflectance cross-check (Fig. 3c) covers only the lower edge of the hyperbolic window, and its absolute calibration is incomplete.
   - Coverage: the microspectrometer spans 300–1050 nm (1.18–4.13 eV), but the hyperbolic window is claimed to extend to 6.2 eV. The measured points therefore test only the rise of the reflectance band near 3.8–4.1 eV, not the band itself or the upper ENZ edge.
   - Calibration: Methods say R is the ratio to an aluminium mirror. Unless this ratio was multiplied by the known Al reflectance, R is underestimated by about 8–15%. The "Al mirror artifact" near 1.5 eV is Al's interband absorption, which suggests the correction was not applied.
   - Interpretation: normal-incidence R depends only on ε_ab. Agreement in Fig. 3c therefore says nothing about ε_c, or about whether the anisotropy was treated correctly.

   The authors should correct R for the reference reflectivity, and extend reflectance (or a second ellipsometer configuration) toward 6 eV if possible. If not, they should state that the upper part of the window is supported only by the ellipsometric inversion.

5. The upper ENZ point and the sign of ε_c1 near 5.5 eV need closer support.
   - Upper ENZ: ε_ab1 crosses zero at about 6.2 eV, within about 0.25 eV of the 6.46 eV limit of the measurement, where the source intensity and the signal-to-noise are lowest. The loss-function peak (Fig. 3d) also sits at this edge.
   - Sign of ε_c1: in Fig. 3a the ε_c1 curve dips sharply near the c-axis absorption at about 5.5 eV (Fig. 3b) and appears to approach or cross zero. If ε_c1 < 0 there, both components are negative and the window is not Type-II hyperbolic over that sub-range. The text says ε_c1 "remains positive" but gives no minimum value.

   The authors should report min ε_c1 with an uncertainty that includes the model error from concern 1. They should also give the uncertainty on the upper ENZ energy and state whether the window is continuous.

6. The 3.71 eV "direct band gap" from a Tauc analysis is hard to reconcile with an excitonic peak at 3.78 eV.
   - The text attributes the 3.78 eV feature to a strong exciton (citing BSE calculations), and the Tauc fit uses the steepest part of α at about 10⁶ cm⁻¹ (Fig. 2d inset), i.e. the rising edge of that excitonic peak.
   - A parabolic-band Tauc form does not describe an excitonic line. The "gap" then sits only 70 meV below the exciton maximum, which would imply a binding energy or broadening that the authors do not discuss.
   - The abstract and comparison figures nevertheless use 3.71 eV as the band gap.

   The authors should either fit the edge with an Elliott-type model (exciton plus continuum) or call 3.71 eV an optical absorption onset. They should reconcile it with the 3.45 eV indirect value in the SI and with literature values.

7. The theory supports the selection rule only qualitatively; it is not compared quantitatively with the measured anisotropy.
   - The GGA band structure (Fig. 4) underestimates the gap; the size of the underestimate is not stated.
   - The calculated ε_ab and ε_c are relegated to the SI, so the reader cannot see whether the predicted ratio of in-plane to out-of-plane oscillator strength, and the absence of a c-axis feature, match Fig. 2b.
   - Cu 4s character, which hybridizes with dz2 in delafossites and can carry z-polarized weight, is not shown in the orbital projections. Only 4p is shown (Fig. 4c), and the conduction-band projections are faint.

   The authors should:
   - show the calculated ε_ab(ω) and ε_c(ω) in the main text, with a stated scissor shift, alongside the measured spectra;
   - quantify the ratio of in-plane to out-of-plane spectral weight for the lowest transition;
   - include the Cu s projection;
   - say which parts of the agreement depend on excitonic effects that GGA omits.

8. The absolute values of n_c and n_ab have not been checked for plausibility or internal consistency.
   - n_c ≈ 1.6–1.9 below 3 eV (Fig. 2a) is surprisingly low for this compound. This matters because Δn in the visible is mainly n_ab − n_c.
   - The point-by-point inversion is not shown to satisfy Kramers-Kronig consistency.

   The authors should fit a Kramers-Kronig-consistent oscillator model to each component. They should compare n_ab with published CuAlO2 film or powder refractive indices, and with the √ε∞ implied by the calculations, and discuss any discrepancy.

9. Sample statistics and defect sensitivity are not reported.
   - The paper does not say how many crystals were measured, or whether the headline numbers were reproduced on a second crystal or facet.
   - The sub-gap absorption is attributed to about 1.4% Cu²⁺ (citing earlier work), but Cu²⁺ content was not measured on these crystals.
   - Raman spectra are mentioned in Methods and the author contributions but are not shown in the main text.

   The authors should report results from at least two crystals, with the spread in Δn at 370 and 333 nm and in the ENZ energies. They should characterize Cu²⁺ or oxygen off-stoichiometry on the measured crystals (for example by XPS, EPR or magnetization), or soften the attribution.

10. The comparisons in Fig. 2e and the text mix resonant and transparent regimes.
    - |Δn|max = 3.67 occurs at 3.72 eV, on the steep flank of a transition where k_ab is several units. Describing it as "among the largest reported for natural anisotropic optical materials" therefore compares a lossy resonant value with transparent-window values for the reference materials.
    - The visible-range claim is better supported and is the useful one.

    The authors should restrict the benchmark claim to the region below the gap, at the k values from concern 3, and mark the in-gap or resonant part of the CuAlO2 curve in Fig. 2e as such.

C. Minor

11. In Fig. 2d the ab-plane α peak appears to reach about 2.5×10⁶ cm⁻¹. From k_ab ≈ 5.5 at about 3.83 eV (Fig. 2b), α = 4πk/λ ≈ 2.1×10⁶ cm⁻¹. Please check that α was computed consistently from the plotted k, and that the axis label is correct.

12. Eq. 1 is typeset ambiguously as "(1 − ρ/1 + ρ)²". It should read [(1 − ρ)/(1 + ρ)]².

13. Please state how the side-facet orientation was determined (by single-crystal XRD on the same crystal, or from habit alone), and the misorientation uncertainty. A miscut of a few degrees on the side facet would mix ε_ab into the "c-axis" response near the strong 3.78 eV resonance.

14. The phrase "Type-II hyperbolic" should be defined once in the text (ε_ab < 0, ε_c > 0), because naming conventions differ between communities.

15. In Fig. 3e, Δε1 is defined as |ε_ab1 − ε_c1|. Please state whether the comparison curves use the same definition and come from ellipsometry on bulk crystals, so the comparison is consistent.

16. The application paragraph (KrF 248 nm hyperbolic photolithography, sub-diffraction imaging) should note that ε_ab2 is large across the window (FOM of about 1–2). Hyperbolic polariton propagation lengths will be short, so these applications are speculative until near-field measurements exist.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Replace Eq. 1 with a uniaxial multi-angle, multi-azimuth (Mueller-matrix) model fitted jointly to both facets, including roughness. Show the cross-check of ε_ab from the side facet against ε_ab from the (001) face, and propagate the model uncertainty into every headline number.
2. Add polarized transmission on a thinned or oriented slab to put the visible "low-loss" claim on quantitative ground. Then replot Fig. 2e with an Δn/k-type figure of merit.
3. Show the calculated ε_ab(ω) and ε_c(ω) (with scissor shift, and ideally with BSE or a literature BSE result) overlaid on the data in the main text. This would make the selection-rule explanation quantitative.
4. Report the number of crystals, the spot size and depolarization, and the reproducibility of Δn, the ENZ energies and min ε_c1 across samples.
