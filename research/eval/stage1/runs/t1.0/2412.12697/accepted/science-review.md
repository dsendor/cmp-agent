A. Must address

1. The principal components are extracted with an isotropic two-phase inversion (Eq. 1), which is not valid for this uniaxial crystal at 70° incidence, so the reported ε_ab and ε_c, and hence every headline number, are pseudo-quantities of unknown accuracy. Methods, Eq. 1 and the Results paragraph on Fig. 2 (p. 2, p. 7) state that ε_ab and ε_c are "obtained directly from the direction-selective ellipsometric parameter through a model-free transformation", that the response is "dominated by the dielectric-function component parallel to the s-polarized electric field", and that the orthogonal component "can be neglected" or is "only weakly sensitive" (citing Ref. 8). Ellipsometry measures ρ = r_p/r_s, and r_p at oblique incidence depends on two tensor components. On the (001) face, r_p depends on ε_ab in the plane and on ε_c through the surface-normal field component (the uniaxial Fresnel coefficient contains √(ε_ab ε_c) and ε_ab/ε_c terms). On the side facet with c along s, r_s depends on ε_c, but r_p depends on ε_ab both in-plane and along the surface normal. Eq. 1 therefore returns a genuine mixture of ε_ab and ε_c on each facet, not a principal component. Vanishing cross-polarization only removes off-diagonal Jones elements; it does not make r_p independent of the other component, and the Mueller-matrix comparison with a facet "whose exact Miller index was not determined" (Methods, SI) shows only that off-diagonal mixing is small. Here the two components differ by up to Δn ≈ 3.7 and Δk ≈ 5.2, with |ε_ab| ~ 20–50 and ε_c small near 3.6–4.0 eV, so the error is largest exactly where |Δn|max = 3.67, the n_ab peak of 5.66, |Δk|max = 5.22, the lower ENZ point and the start of the hyperbolic window are claimed, and again near the upper ENZ and ε_c near 5.5 eV. Δn, Δk, Δε1, the ENZ energies and the FOM all inherit this error. The authors should refit Ψ/Δ (or the full Mueller matrix) with a proper uniaxial bulk model (Berreman/4×4 or a standard anisotropic-substrate model, e.g. a CompleteEASE/WVASE uniaxial layer) including a surface roughness/overlayer layer, fitting both facets jointly at several angles of incidence (for example 55°, 60°, 65°, 70°, 75°) and at two azimuths on the side facet (c parallel and perpendicular to the plane of incidence). They should report the fit quality (MSE/residuals), parameter correlations and uncertainty bands on ε_ab and ε_c; report how much each headline number (|Δn|max, Δn at 370 nm, |Δk|max, both ENZ energies, FOM at 4.01 eV) changes relative to the Eq. 1 values; and show that ε_ab from the side facet (c ∥ p geometry) agrees with ε_ab from the (001) face, which is the most direct internal test. Without this, the headline numbers cannot be accepted at the quoted precision.

2. The orientation and azimuthal alignment of the side facet, which carries the entire c-axis response, have not been verified, and the cross-polarization check was done on the wrong surface. The text says Mueller-matrix measurements on the (001) surface and on a facet of undetermined Miller index validate the absence of cross-polarization mixing (p. 7; SI). On a (001) face the optic axis lies along the surface normal, so the off-diagonal Jones elements vanish by symmetry at every azimuth; that measurement says nothing about the side-facet geometry, where c must lie accurately along s. Any in-plane rotation of c away from s, or a tilt or miscut of a few degrees of the facet away from (100)/(110), mixes ε_ab into the "c-axis" spectrum, most strongly near the 3.78 eV resonance. The side-facet orientation is only asserted from the crystal habit in Fig. 1b; no Laue, single-crystal XRD or rocking data are shown for it. The authors should state how the side-facet orientation was determined and on which crystal, quantify its misorientation by X-ray, show Mueller-matrix data (off-diagonal blocks) with azimuthal rotation on the side facet itself so that the off-diagonal elements are shown to vanish only when c is aligned with s or p, and propagate the alignment uncertainty into ε_c and |Δn|.

3. The beam footprint relative to the facet size, and depolarization, are not documented, so the data, especially the c-axis data, may include light from outside the facet. Fig. 1b shows hexagonal pillars 1–2 mm long whose faces are a few hundred µm across (100 µm scale bar). A standard M-2000 beam at 70° incidence is stretched by about 3× and projects to a millimetre-order footprint unless focusing probes are used. The Methods give no spot size, focusing optics, facet dimensions, alignment procedure or depolarization spectra, and do not say how overfilling, edge scattering and depolarization were excluded. If the beam overfills the side facet, light from adjacent facets of different orientation, from facet edges, or from the silver epoxy would enter the c-axis data (silver-epoxy mounting suppresses backside reflection but not edge scattering). Several features fit partial overfilling, a tilted facet or depolarization: the noisy n_ab and ε_ab1 below about 2 eV (Figs. 2a, 3a), the low n_c in the visible, and the nonzero sub-gap k of about 0.07 for both polarizations. The authors should state the spot size and focusing optics and how the footprint compares with each facet, show the measured depolarization (or Mueller-matrix depolarization index) versus photon energy for both geometries, and show that the extracted ε does not change when the spot is moved or reduced on the facet, or when a second angle of incidence is used.

4. The "low-loss" and "wide-bandgap transparent" framing conflicts with the measured extinction and with where the headline birefringence occurs. The paper reports k_ab ≈ k_c ≈ 0.07 at 1 eV (p. 3); at 1.24 µm this is α = 4πk/λ ≈ 7×10³ cm⁻¹, an absorption length of about 1.4 µm. Fig. 2b suggests k_ab is still larger (of order 0.2–0.5) across the visible, i.e. α ~ 2×10⁴ cm⁻¹ and an absorption length of about 0.5 µm near 500 nm, and the crystals look black (Fig. 1b). With |Δn| ≈ 0.6–0.8, a visible half-wave retarder needs about 0.3–0.4 µm thickness and would absorb an appreciable fraction of the light, which is not low-loss in the sense used for LiNbO3 or calcite in Fig. 2e, nor for the waveplates and retarders proposed on p. 5. The headline |Δn|max = 3.67 at 3.72 eV lies at or above the quoted 3.71 eV direct gap, on the flank of a resonance where k_ab is several units (about 5); it is the usual dispersive consequence of a strong absorption line, not birefringence in a transparent window. The highlighted |Δn| = 1.66 at 370 nm (3.35 eV), described as the largest among "lower-loss" materials, lies only 0.1 eV below the 3.45 eV indirect gap on the Urbach tail, and its k is not given. Reflection ellipsometry on a thick absorbing crystal also cannot resolve k below about 0.01–0.05, and a surface overlayer, roughness or depolarization can produce spurious k, so the sub-gap loss is poorly constrained either way. The authors should: measure sub-gap loss with a method that has the sensitivity, such as polarized transmission through thinned (001) and (100) slabs of known thickness or oriented films, giving quantitative k_ab and k_c in the visible; report k_ab and k_c (or α) alongside Δn at every quoted wavelength (333 nm, 370 nm, 400–780 nm) and plot k next to Δn in Fig. 2c,e; give a loss-aware figure of merit such as Δn/max(k_ab, k_c), or the retardance achievable per absorption length; define "low-loss" numerically; determine whether the sub-gap k is intrinsic (Cu²⁺ defects) or an artifact of the inversion; and separate the transparent-region claim from the resonant peak value, scoping abstract, title and conclusion wording ("broadband low-loss birefringence", "colossal visible birefringence") to what that analysis supports.

B. Should address

5. There is no independent check of Δn, or of n_c, in the transparent region. All birefringence numbers come from reflection ellipsometry on as-grown, mechanically separated facets with no surface model. The only cross-check, normal-incidence reflectance (Fig. 3c), is unpolarized, probes ε_ab alone, and covers only ≤ 4.13 eV, so it says nothing about ε_c or about whether the anisotropy was treated correctly. n_c is the less certain component and sets Δn in the visible, and its fairly low below-gap value (about 1.6–2.0, Fig. 2a; below that of Al2O3 for a Cu-bearing oxide) is what one would expect if roughness, overlayers or overfilling depress the pseudo-index. The authors should provide at least one independent measurement of Δn, for example polarized reflectance on the side facet with E ∥ c and E ⊥ c checked against the ellipsometric ε_c and ε_ab, transmission retardance through a thinned, polished side-cut plate at e.g. 532 or 633 nm, or prism-coupling or interference-fringe data, ideally from at least two crystals. Agreement at the 10% level in Δn at one or two visible wavelengths would substantially strengthen the central claim.

6. The sign of ε_c1 near 5.5 eV and the upper ENZ point need quantitative support, because they decide whether the Type-II window is continuous. In Fig. 3a, ε_c1 dips sharply near the c-axis absorption at about 5.4–5.7 eV (Fig. 3b) and appears to approach or cross zero. If ε_c1 < 0 anywhere there, both components are negative and that sub-band is doubly negative (metallic), not Type-II hyperbolic; the text says ε_c1 "remains positive" but gives no minimum value. The upper ENZ crossing of ε_ab1 at about 6.1–6.2 eV lies within about 0.25 eV of the 6.46 eV limit of the data, where source intensity, signal-to-noise and ellipsometric accuracy are lowest and where the Eq. 1 error (concern 1) also acts; the loss-function peak (Fig. 3d) also sits at this edge. The authors should show ε_c1 on an expanded scale with error bands over 5–6.5 eV, report min ε_c1 with an uncertainty that includes the model error from concern 1, mark any sub-interval where ε_c1 < 0, give the uncertainty on the upper ENZ energy, and state whether the window is continuous.

7. The hyperbolic window between about 3.8 and 6.2 eV is supported only by the ellipsometric inversion itself. The reflectance check in Fig. 3c spans 300–1050 nm (1.18–4.13 eV), so it covers only the first ~0.3 eV of the 2.4 eV window: it confirms the onset of the high-reflectance band but not the band itself or the upper ENZ edge. The loss function in Fig. 3d is computed from the same ellipsometric ε_ab and is therefore not independent corroboration of the ENZ points. The authors should extend an independent measurement across 4–6.5 eV (VUV reflectance, polarized on both facets, synchrotron ellipsometry, or a second ellipsometer configuration) to confirm both ENZ points and the sign of ε_c1; if this is not possible, they should state explicitly that the upper part of the window rests only on the ellipsometric inversion and that Fig. 3d is not independent evidence.

8. The hyperbolic response is strongly lossy, and this is not stated quantitatively. The lower "ENZ" crossing of ε_ab1 near 3.8 eV coincides with ε_ab2 ≈ 40–47 (Fig. 3a,b), so |ε_ab| there is far from near-zero and calling it an ENZ point is nominal. Across the window ε_ab1 is modest and negative while ε_ab2 is comparable (FOM ≤ 1.91, about 1–2), a lossy, excitonic Reststrahlen-like response that supports evanescent rather than well-propagating high-k modes; hyperbolic polariton propagation lengths will be short. The application paragraph (KrF 248 nm hyperbolic photolithography, sub-diffraction imaging) does not acknowledge this. The authors should show |ε| and ε2 at both ENZ points, compare the FOM with that of hBN in its own band, describe the hyperbolic regime as lossy in quantitative terms in the abstract, and present the proposed applications as speculative until near-field measurements exist.

9. The benchmark comparisons in Fig. 2e and the text mix resonant and transparent regimes and are not like-for-like. The comparison materials are selected by band gap above 1.55 eV and plotted only over their low-loss ranges, but the CuAlO2 curve runs to 200 nm, through its own absorption peak and into the 340–370 nm region where its absorption is significant (stars mark sub-gap points). Every material's birefringence rises near its own absorption edge. The claims that |Δn|max = 3.67 and |Δk|max = 5.22 are "among the largest reported for natural anisotropic optical materials" (p. 3), that 1.66 at 370 nm is the "largest value among the UV–visible lower-loss anisotropic materials surveyed", and that CuAlO2 has the "largest Δε1 among the natural materials surveyed" (p. 4) therefore compare at-resonance values with off-resonance values of other materials. The 1.55 eV cutoff also excludes several known large-Δn crystals while including MoS2, whose gap is close to the cutoff. The visible-range claim is better supported and is the useful one. The authors should use the same loss criterion for all materials (e.g. |Δn| only where max(k) < 0.1, Δn at a fixed absorption level, or Δn against wavelength normalized to each material's gap, or a loss-normalized FOM using the k values from concern 4), mark the in-gap or resonant part of the CuAlO2 curve as such, state the selection rule explicitly, and qualify the "largest" statements as at-resonance values or restrict them to the region below the gap.

10. Surface roughness and overlayers are not included in the inversion. The facets are as-grown, the crystal is dark from Cu²⁺ defects, and Eq. 1 assumes an ideal abrupt interface. Even a few nm of roughness or an oxidized or Cu-rich surface layer noticeably reduces the peak ε2 and the peak n and k at a sharp resonance, and can depress the pseudo-index below the gap and add spurious sub-gap k. This bears directly on the quoted n_ab,max = 5.66, |Δk|max = 5.22, ε_ab2 ≈ 47 and the low n_c. The agreement with reflectance in Fig. 3c below 4 eV is reassuring but does not reach the peak region. The authors should give AFM roughness for both facets, include a roughness (Bruggeman EMA) or overlayer layer in the uniaxial fit, and report how the peak values change.

12. The absolute values of n_ab and n_c have not been checked for plausibility or internal consistency. n_c ≈ 1.6–1.9 below 3 eV (Fig. 2a) is surprisingly low for this compound, and this matters because Δn in the visible is mainly n_ab − n_c. 13. The 3.71 eV "direct band gap" from a Tauc analysis is applied to an excitonic line and has no clear meaning as a band gap. The (αħω)² extrapolation (Fig. 2d inset) uses a narrow window on the steepest rising edge (α ~ 10⁶ cm⁻¹) of the sharp 3.78 eV peak, which the paper itself attributes to a strong exciton (p. 2, Refs. 17, 18, BSE calculations). A parabolic-band Tauc law does not describe absorption dominated by an exciton plus a Cu²⁺-related Urbach tail, so the extracted value depends on the fit window, and a "gap" 70 meV below the exciton maximum implies a binding energy or broadening that is not discussed; the continuum onset would lie above the exciton. The 3.71 eV value is nevertheless used in the abstract and comparison figures, to argue that the gap is wider than in earlier reports, and to call |Δn| = 1.66 at 370 nm "below the gap", although the 3.45 eV indirect gap (SI) lies between 370 nm (3.35 eV) and 3.71 eV. The authors should report the sensitivity of the gap to the fit window, fit the edge with an Elliott-type model (exciton plus continuum) to obtain the gap and exciton binding energy, or else call 3.71 eV an optical absorption onset; and reconcile it with the 3.45 eV indirect value and with earlier, lower experimental values, making the "wide bandgap" statements in the abstract consistent.

14. The theory supports the selection rule only qualitatively and omits the excitonic physics that sets the magnitude of the anisotropy. The GGA/Wannier calculation (Fig. 4) identifies orbital characters and a selection rule, which is reasonable, and the paper says it does not attempt to reproduce the spectrum quantitatively (p. 5). But the size of the effect (ε_ab2 ≈ 47, a sharp line) is excitonic, and GGA without electron–hole interaction cannot capture it. The GGA gap underestimate is not stated; the calculated ε_ab and ε_c are relegated to the SI, so the reader cannot see whether the predicted ratio of in-plane to out-of-plane oscillator strength and the absence of a c-axis feature match Fig. 2b; spin–orbit coupling and any Hubbard U on Cu 3d are not stated; Cu 4s character, which hybridizes with d_z² in delafossites and can carry z-polarized weight, is not shown in the orbital projections (only 4p, Fig. 4c, and the conduction-band projections are faint); and the "intra-atomic-like" d→p claim rests only on Bloch-function plots at a single k-point (L). The authors should overlay the scissor-shifted calculated ε_ab(ω) and ε_c(ω) on the measured spectra in the main text with the scissor shift stated; quantify the computed in-plane to out-of-plane spectral weight for the lowest transition; include the Cu s projection; quantify the d_z²/p_x,y weights along the whole contributing L–F segment; decompose the momentum matrix elements in the Wannier basis to check whether the on-site d_z²→p_x,y term dominates over inter-site Cu–O contributions; show a BSE spectrum for both polarizations (or compare directly with existing BSE ε_ab, ε_c) or explain why c-axis excitonic weight is suppressed by the same symmetry; say which parts of the agreement depend on excitonic effects GGA omits; and temper "providing a microscopic basis" accordingly. A temperature-dependent measurement of the 3.78 eV peak would help establish its excitonic character.
