A. Must address

1. **There is no temperature dependence, no normal-state reference at the same location and no null control for the field dependence, so the dissipation contrast cannot yet be attributed to superconductivity.** Every dissipation dataset was taken at T = 10 mK, and the case that the signal tracks superconductivity rests on the A/B comparison of Fig. 4b and the magnetic-field sweep at one location near point A (Fig. 4c–e; text pp. 8–9, 13). The A/B comparison mixes "conducting vs insulating" with "superconducting vs normal": point A lies in a conducting 2DES and point B on insulating, unwritten interface, so their dissipation would differ even in the normal state. In the Joule/electronic-friction picture the authors cite (Volokitin–Persson, ref. 32; Kisiel, refs. 17, 31), the loss depends non-monotonically on sheet resistance and screening length, so a well-screening normal 2DES can show much less loss than a poorly screened insulating region without any superconductivity. Kisiel et al. (ref. 31), on which the ΔV⁴ interpretation relies, compared spectra above and below Tc at the same location; no equivalent is shown here. No field map is shown at point B, on the bare-STO reference area that exists on Device B (Supp. Fig. 3), or with the tip retracted. Non-superconducting mechanisms that could produce field-dependent dissipation of this size include spin- and charge-related transitions in oxygen-deficient STO surfaces (which ref. 17 detected by nc-AFM dissipation), field dependence of the qPlus sensor's intrinsic damping Γ0 or of the excitation electronics, and normal-state magnetoresistance of the 2DES altering the ohmic loss. The authors should: (i) measure Γ(V_bias) at points A and B, and in the 200 nm strip of Fig. 5, from base temperature to well above Tc (e.g. 50, 150, 250, 400, 600 mK) at zero field, and at 10 mK with B ≫ Bc (e.g. 0.5–1 T), all with the same tip, height, amplitude and fit window, reporting how α and β change; (ii) repeat the field map of Fig. 4c at point B and over bare STO under identical setpoints; (iii) record Γ0(B) with the tip retracted; (iv) show that the A/B contrast, the low baseline and the ΔV⁴ term vanish at T > Tc and B > Bc while the controls are unchanged. If the ΔV⁴ term and low baseline survive above Tc, they are not superconducting signatures. Without this, the title's "local probing of superconductivity" and the abstract's "local diagnostic of superconductivity" are not established.

2. **The ΔV⁴ "marker" is also present at the non-superconducting reference point, and the fit as presented does not discriminate.** The text (p. 9) describes point B as showing "a clear quadratic dependence" and point A as having an emergent quartic component, yet the Fig. 4b caption gives β/α = 1.84 ± 0.01 V⁻² for B against 3.85 ± 0.03 V⁻² for A: a factor of two, not presence versus absence. The two curves are fitted over different bias windows relative to their CPDs (A: |ΔV| up to ≈0.7 V around a CPD of ≈−0.5 V; B: ≈0.4–0.5 V around ≈0.1 V). For B the quartic term equals the quadratic one only at |ΔV| ≈ 0.74 V, outside the measured range, so β for B is poorly constrained, and the fitted β/α of a truncated polynomial depends strongly on the window. The written fit form αΔV² + βΔV⁴ has no constant, yet both curves have a baseline of 18–23 × 10⁻⁸ kg/s; it is not stated whether an offset was free. Curve A is visibly asymmetric about its minimum. The quoted ±0.01–0.03 V⁻² uncertainties are least-squares only and ignore the α–β correlation and systematics (fit window, offset treatment, CPD and height drift), and say nothing about reproducibility. The authors should state the full fit function and free/fixed parameters; fit both points over the same |ΔV| window with a constant term and report α, β and the offset separately with units; show how β/α varies with the window; show residuals for the pure-quadratic, quadratic-plus-quartic and, if needed, ΔV⁶ or asymmetric models with an F-test or information criterion; and give the distribution of β/α over many pixels inside and outside the pattern (a histogram or spatial map correlated with CPD, in both devices), at zero field and above Bc and Tc. Without this, "ΔV⁴ emerges in superconducting regions" is not supported.

3. **The field scale of the dissipation does not match the transport critical field offered as its "direct connection", the key low-field data are compromised, and the transport Bc comes from a different channel.** The text (pp. 10–11, 13) says dissipation at the CPD "reach[es] a maximum as it approaches the critical magnetic field" (~200 mT) and that the curvature crossover "occurs as the field approaches 200 mT". In Fig. 4e the maxima are instead at about −70 to −100 mT and +80 to +100 mT, Γ has already fallen substantially by ±200 mT, and the text itself later refers to "enhanced dissipation around ≈±100 mT". In Fig. 4d the 42 mT (orange) curve already departs from the convex shape (double-humped) and the 102 mT curve is concave; the "green curve in Fig. 4d" described as near 200 mT is the 102 mT cut according to the arrowheads. The crossover is thus at roughly half Device A's Bc and closer to Device B's ≈55 mT (Supp. Fig. 4), which the main text never states. The red "−18 mT" curve in Fig. 4d, the main evidence for convex superconducting behaviour in the map, lies where the Fig. 4e caption reports an AFM feedback glitch and where Fig. 4c shows a narrow anomalous dark band next to a high-dissipation sliver near 0 mT; the zero-field minimum in Fig. 4e spans only ≈±20 mT and its lowest points come from this region. The map is asymmetric (the low-dissipation band is offset toward ≈−10 to −30 mT; the negative-field band extends to ≈−130 mT and the positive one to ≈+100 mT), which suggests remanent field or trapped flux in the superconducting magnet, or sweep-direction hysteresis. The zero-field spectrum at A (Fig. 4b; minimum ≈18 × 10⁻⁸ kg/s, strong convex curvature) is called "consistent" with the −18 mT cut (≈9 × 10⁻⁸ kg/s, nearly flat), but they were taken at different setpoints (Δf = −1.0 Hz vs the unstated setpoint in 4b) and are not comparable. Alternatives for a non-monotonic Γ(B) include vortex entry and tip-driven vortex motion in a 1 µm wide film in perpendicular field, normal-state magnetoresistance, and, specifically, the Ir tips (Methods, pp. 26–27): Ir is a superconductor with Tc ≈ 0.1 K and a critical field of a few mT, so the sharp ≈±20 mT minimum could originate in the tip. Finally, the transport Bc of Device A comes from contacts 4–5 (Fig. 1e; Supp. Fig. 5a), a path through the upper V-shaped/Y/1D-channel region, not the bottom of the 2D channel where Figs. 4–5 were taken (Fig. 2c orange box), so local and global critical fields refer to different structures. The authors should re-measure the low-field region without the glitch, sweeping in both directions and reporting sweep direction and hysteresis; calibrate and correct the magnet's remanent field (e.g. from the transport diamond of Fig. 1e taken in the same sweep); discuss tip superconductivity explicitly; measure transport across the 2D channel actually probed (e.g. contacts 3–7, 7–4 or 7–8); state which field scale (≈20–30 mT, ≈100 mT, 200 mT) they identify with which physical process, discuss the width of the zero-field minimum as a characteristic field, and correct the text to describe Fig. 4e accurately.

4. **The headline "~200 nm superconducting edge channel" rests on one line cut, at one field above the transport Bc, at one edge, and the data partly contradict its interpretation.** The ≈200 nm dissipation minimum (Fig. 5h) comes from one Y-line at X = 125 nm of a 250 nm-wide grid, recorded only at B = −235 mT, above the ≈200 mT transport Bc that the paper otherwise uses as the threshold for losing superconductivity; the Discussion's resolution that the edge "likely has a higher critical field" (pp. 13–14) is an assumption, not a measurement. No zero-field or low-field map of the same area is shown, only one boundary of the 1 µm 2D channel is mapped, and the channel interior, the opposite edge, the 1D wire and the waveguide, which motivate the paper, are not studied by dissipation. Edge localization, the edge/interior contrast and the width independence are therefore not demonstrated. In Fig. 5b–e the strip at Y ≈ 400–600 nm is essentially independent of bias from −900 mV to +300 mV; since the authors argue elsewhere (pp. 11, 13) that tip bias locally dopes the interface and shifts it along the dome, an optimally doped strip within a density gradient should move in Y with bias. Its bias independence fits a geometric or electrostatic feature tied to the boundary at least as well. The strip coincides with the steepest CPD gradient (0.65 V across the Fig. 5 area; Fig. 5a,b,h), where lateral electrostatic forces, dC/dz and constant-Δf height adjustments all vary rapidly, and normal-state electronic friction, which depends non-monotonically on carrier density/sheet conductance (ref. 32), would give a Γ minimum at intermediate Δn across a density gradient without superconductivity. The authors should map Γ(x, y, V) across both edges and the interior of the 2D channel, at several X positions, at zero field, at several fields from 0 to 300 mT and beyond (e.g. 1 T), and above Tc; show that the strip exists only in the superconducting state and disappears at a field higher than the interior's; show that a comparable conductor–insulator boundary above Tc gives no such dip; show the z(x, y) images recorded simultaneously with Fig. 5; extend to channels of different widths and the second device; and give the strip width with an uncertainty and the definition used for "~200 nm".

5. **The tip–sample distance and feedback conditions during spectroscopy are not specified, and height variations under constant-Δf feedback can produce both the A/B and the strip contrast.** Topography contrast is explicitly electrostatic: up to 8 nm apparent height in Fig. 4a, produced by Δf feedback on CPD differences (p. 6; Methods p. 27). Points A and B, and the pixels across the Fig. 5 boundary, are therefore at different tip heights, and dissipation and tip-induced gating depend steeply on distance. The Fig. 4e caption's mention of an "AFM feedback" glitch suggests spectra are taken under constant-Δf feedback; if so, the tip height changes with bias as the electrostatic force varies roughly as ΔV², and the distance dependence of Γ alone would generate higher-order (ΔV⁴) bias terms, differently in regions of different CPD and dC/dz. It is not stated whether Γ(V) sweeps were taken at constant height, with feedback opened at a fixed V_Bias setpoint, or with the setpoint bias at the local CPD. Different figures use different setpoints (Δf = −3.0, −1.0, −0.8 Hz; amplitudes 3.2–5.0 nm) and the tip–sample distance is never stated, so absolute Γ cannot be compared across figures. The authors should state the protocol and the absolute tip height at A, B and along Fig. 5h; acquire constant-height Γ(V) spectra, or record and show z(V) for every spectrum and correct using measured Γ(z) curves; and show Γ(V) at matched distance or Γ(z) at fixed ΔV at both locations, demonstrating that the A/B contrast and the Fig. 5h minimum are not distance effects.

B. Should address

6. **The CPD-to-density conversion (Eq. 1) is not reliable enough to place regions on the superconducting dome, and the paper's own numbers contradict the "overdoped interior" assignment.** Eq. (1) treats the LAO as an ideal parallel-plate capacitor (~1.5 nm, ε_r = 25). It ignores the tip-induced field, the very large and field-dependent permittivity of STO at mK, the extent of the 2DES into STO, quantum capacitance (invoked elsewhere, ref. 34), and the surface charge, dipoles and protonation/adsorbates that the Supplement (p. 30) calls "essential for generating measurable contrast" and that underlie the ULV-EBL and c-AFM writing mechanism (ref. 4). These enter at leading order; the sign of the dipole correction is not established, so calling Eq. (1) "an upper bound" is unjustified. Δn is a change relative to an arbitrarily chosen "non-patterned" pixel whose absolute density is unknown (CPD = 1.024 V in Fig. 3, 0.07 V in Fig. 5h), and the CPD in Fig. 2e varies smoothly across the 1.5 µm frame, including outside the pattern, so the reference is not flat; yet Δn is compared with an absolute Hall-density dome range. Absolute CPDs shift by ≈1 V between areas of the same device (Fig. 2e: 0.1–1.5 V; Fig. 5a: −0.5 to +0.15 V), suggesting a tip work-function change, which also undermines the constant-φ_tip assumption. The numbers disagree: Fig. 3b peaks at ≈8 × 10¹³ cm⁻² (text: ≈1 × 10¹⁴, i.e. overdoped); in Fig. 5h the whole patterned side is ≲5 × 10¹³ cm⁻² (≈4.5–5 × 10¹³ in the interior) and the "optimal" strip ≈2–3.5 × 10¹³, so the entire Δn range of Fig. 5h (≈0.5–5 × 10¹³) lies inside the "optimal range" of 1.5–6.5 × 10¹³ cm⁻² quoted on p. 7, and the "overdoped" region is by the paper's own numbers optimal. Device B's ≈0.3 V contrast at 6 uc (Supp. Fig. 3c) implies Δn ≈ 2 × 10¹³ cm⁻², ≈5× lower than Device A, yet both show Ic ≈ 30 nA; this is not discussed. In Fig. 3b the density outside the "estimated pattern boundary" is already ≈6–8 × 10¹³ cm⁻², indicating either strong broadening or misregistration. The sign conventions are inconsistent: Eq. (1) defines Δφ = φ_non-patterned − φ_patterned > 0 for added carriers (lower work function in patterned regions), while Supp. pp. 29–30 (CPD = φ_tip − φ_sample) says a lower CPD in the patterned region means a higher work function and more carriers. The authors should calibrate Eq. (1) independently (Hall density on a co-written Hall bar on the same chip, CPD versus side- or back-gate voltage alongside transport, or electrostatic simulation including STO's permittivity); express doping relative to the measured transport dome of the same device; use one fixed reference and keep the tip state fixed between Figs. 3 and 5; explain how pattern boundaries were registered to the AFM images; resolve the sign convention and state the expected sign for added interface electrons; give uncertainties on Δn; and, absent calibration, present the under/optimal/overdoped labels of Fig. 5g,h as qualitative.

7. **The lateral resolution of the CPD and dissipation maps is not established, yet ~200 nm spatial conclusions are drawn.** The CPD and apparent-height features are far broader than the written patterns: the 10–20 nm 1D wire appears as a several-hundred-nm stripe (Fig. 2c), the density maximum in Fig. 2e/3a lies largely outside the dashed 1D-line boundaries over a ≈600 nm–1 µm profile, and the density rise in Fig. 3b extends ≈0.6 µm beyond the dashed boundary. 8. **FM-AFM apparent-dissipation artifacts are not ruled out.** The excitation signal in FM-AFM contains "apparent dissipation" from frequency-dependent transfer functions of the piezo/excitation path and from PLL or amplitude-controller crosstalk whenever Δf changes. Here Δf varies parabolically with bias by construction (Supp. Fig. 1b), so a bias-dependent apparent dissipation would naturally produce even-order (ΔV², ΔV⁴) terms. The authors should report k, f₀, Q, V_exc,0 and the controller bandwidths; show that Γ(V) does not simply track Δf(V), e.g. by comparing spectra at different heights with the same Δf range or verifying the phase stays at resonance throughout; and show the dissipation at the CPD as a function of height.

11. **The mechanism by which superconductivity would suppress the measured dissipation is not established for this geometry.** The ohmic and phonon arguments come from Nb (refs. 31, 32), a bulk metal directly under the tip with a well-defined normal-state ohmic V² law. Here the 2DES is buried under ≈1.5 nm of LAO on a quantum paraelectric with a huge, lossy dielectric response at 10 mK. Above the transport critical field the patterned region does not recover the convex ohmic ΔV² parabola the model predicts for the normal state; the curves become concave (Fig. 4d, 162 and 222 mT), so the paper's normal-state model does not describe the normal state at A. The insulating point B is described as showing "capacitive energy losses typically observed on metallic surfaces due to ohmic dissipation" without saying what carries ohmic current there. A simpler explanation for lower Γ at A is that the 2DES screens the tip field from the lossy STO below, which would hold in the normal state too. The stated mechanism, "suppression of low-energy excitations due to the quasiparticle gap opening" (p. 9), involves a gap of ≈40–60 µeV, while biases are ~1 V and the 18 kHz oscillation corresponds to ≈75 neV; how the gap enters the bias dependence is unexplained. The authors should give an order-of-magnitude estimate (e.g. Volokitin–Persson) of the expected Γ for a 2DES with the measured sheet resistance (≈20 kΩ normal state, Fig. 1f) compared with STO dielectric loss, and of the expected change on entering the superconducting state, explain the gap-scale issue or soften the statement, and account for the concave normal-state curves.

12. **Tip-induced gating is invoked but not quantified or separated from the claimed superconducting signature.** Biases up to ≈1.2–1.4 V across a nanometre gap plus ≈1.5 nm of LAO gate the 2DES under the tip; Eq. (1) gives ≈1 V per 10¹⁴ cm⁻², comparable to the Δn attributed to patterning.
