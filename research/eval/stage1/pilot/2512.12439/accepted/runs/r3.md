Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors make few-layer Ta2NiSe5 flakes by evaporating a substrate (Au or ALD Al2O3) onto a cleaved crystal and then transferring by Au cold welding. They use polarized (ac-channel) Raman spectroscopy to follow the orthorhombic-to-monoclinic transition and the electronic transition (Fano asymmetry of B2g(o1), quasi-elastic intensity at 10 cm^-1) as a function of thickness and substrate. The central observations are these. 4L on Al2O3 has a sharp transition about 40 K below bulk. 6L and 4L on Au have broadened transitions centred near 250 and 220 K. The authors read the Au result as a vertical Tc gradient set by screening and charge transfer at the interface, and they use it to argue that excitonic interactions account for up to about 150 K of the 326 K Tc. Charge-tuning Ta2NiSe5 through a metallic interface is a new and useful perturbation, and so is the fabrication route. The microscopic interpretation, however, currently rests on one flake per condition and on several assumptions the paper does not test.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The evaporation-plus-cold-welding transfer is dry and general. It gives 10-100 um few-layer flakes of a rippled, poorly exfoliating compound (Fig. 1), which is a useful technical contribution.
- The substrate background is handled carefully. Bare Au/Cr and Al2O3 are measured (Fig. 2f, g), and the ac channel is shown to be free of substrate signal, so the analysis rests on a clean channel.
- Two independent probes of the transition are tracked: lattice (onset of Ag(m4)/Ag(m5), Fig. 3) and electronic (Fano -1/q and quasi-elastic weight, Fig. 4). Where both are available they agree reasonably well (Fig. 5).
- The thick-flake controls (18L on Au, 7L on Al2O3) recover the bulk Tc. This anchors the method.
- The data are deposited on Zenodo.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. One flake per condition; no reproducibility or device-to-device spread. Each of the five conditions (7L/Al2O3, 4L/Al2O3, 18L/Au, 6L/Au, 4L/Au) appears to be a single flake measured at a single spot. The headline contrast is 4L/Al2O3 (sharp, 280-287 K) against 4L/Au (broad, about 220 ± 40 K). It therefore rests on two flakes made by different processes. Flake-to-flake variation in strain, wrinkles, tears, defect density or thickness could produce shifts and broadenings of tens of kelvin, so the substrate assignment cannot be separated from sample variation. Ta2NiSe5 Tc is strongly sensitive to uniaxial stress (the authors' own ref. 16), and these flakes are mechanically bonded to evaporated films. The authors should measure at least two or three further flakes of 4L (and ideally 5-6L) on each substrate, show the resulting Tc and width spread in Fig. 5, and state how many flakes of each kind were prepared and measured.

2. Screening and charge transfer are not separated from other differences between the two substrates. The two interfaces differ in much more than conductivity. On the Au side, Au atoms are thermally evaporated directly onto the Se-terminated surface, with possible intermixing, Au-Se bonding or local reaction, and the film conforms to the surface ripples. On the Al2O3 side, ALD with TMA/H2O at 100-150 °C precedes the Au. The two stacks also impose different interfacial strain and different thermal-expansion mismatch on cooling. Any of these could suppress Tc in the interfacial layer and leave a gradient through the flake. The paper itself shows the substrate is not neutral: 4L/Al2O3 is already 40 K below bulk, and this is not explained. The 4L/Al2O3 sharp transition rules out bulk oxidation, but it does not rule out an Au-specific chemical or strain effect. No direct evidence of charge transfer is presented: no XPS/UPS core-level or valence-band shift, no work-function measurement, no doping-sensitive phonon shift. The authors should add the following:
   - a control that keeps the metal but blocks charge transfer and shortens nothing else, for example Au evaporated on top of 1-2 nm of ALD Al2O3 or on a monolayer hBN spacer, or a high-work-function metal such as Pt, which the authors themselves propose;
   - quantification of strain, using the high-temperature (orthorhombic) frequencies of Ag(o) modes in the aa channel compared across 4L/Al2O3, 4L/Au and the thick flakes;
   - an interface characterization (cross-sectional TEM/EDX or XPS of Au-deposited Ta2NiSe5) showing whether the interfacial layer stays chemically intact.
   Without these steps, "charge transfer and electrostatic screening ... are the likely culprit" (p. 15) is one possibility among several.

3. The vertical Tc-gradient picture is not shown to be the unique explanation of the broadening, and it ignores depth-dependent optical weighting on a metal. The mixed-phase model (Fig. 5b inset, p. 13) assumes the Raman signal is an even average over all layers ("averages over all layers"). On an optically thick Au mirror, the 632.8 nm standing-wave field is strongly reduced at the metal surface. For a 2.5-4 nm flake, the layer next to Au would then be weighted far less than the top layer, in both excitation and collection. The ×25 and ×50 weaker signals for 6L and 4L on Au, compared with flakes on Al2O3 (Figs. 3, 4), are consistent with this.

   Two consequences follow. (i) The interfacial layer that supposedly has Tc suppressed by about 150 K may contribute little to the spectrum, so the low-temperature end of the transition (about 180 K) is poorly constrained. (ii) The dominant top layer of 4L/Au begins its transition at about 220-250 K (first kink, Fig. 3t), well below 4L/Al2O3 (280 K). This contradicts the statement that surface layers "have a local Tc closer to room temperature" and suggests a suppression across the whole flake rather than a gradient.

   Lateral inhomogeneity within the laser spot would also produce a broadened, averaged transition. Possible sources are patchy contact with polycrystalline/amorphous Au, Au grain roughness, and bubbles or wrinkles. The spot size is not given, and no spatial map is shown. The authors should:
   - compute the depth-resolved Raman weighting (transfer-matrix) for each stack and include it in the toy model of Supplementary Note 4;
   - show Raman maps or several spots across each Au-supported flake at temperatures inside the broadened range;
   - present the 5L or 3L dependence, or at least discuss why a gradient model fits better than uniform suppression plus lateral spread.

B. Should address

4. The Tc and width extraction for the Au flakes is heuristic and partly circular. Two points:
   - The ±40 K width (p. 9-10) comes from assuming that the second kink in Ag(m4) lies a fixed 60 K below Tc in a sharp transition. Any extra separation (100 K on Au) is then attributed to broadening. Nothing physical ties the second kink to Tc by a fixed offset, and the offset could itself depend on doping or strain.
   - For 4L/Au, the electronic Tc^avg = 220 ± 46 K (p. 12) is obtained by shifting the 6L/Au -1/q curve by about 30 K and borrowing the 6L width. It is therefore not independent of the structural estimate, yet Fig. 5b presents it as a second, independent determination.
   The authors should define the broadened transition with a single model-based fit applied the same way to all five flakes, for example fitting the integrated Ag(m5) intensity or -1/q(T) to a Gaussian-distributed-Tc model. They should report the fitted centre and width with uncertainties, and they should mark the 4L/Au electronic point in Fig. 5 as derived rather than measured.

5. Temperature smoothing can create or exaggerate the broadening. The 2D maps are resampled on a 5 K grid and smoothed with a Gaussian of 10-15 K standard deviation in temperature (Methods). The peak positions in Fig. 3p-t are then taken from the second derivative of these smoothed maps. This smoothing is comparable to the transition widths claimed for the sharp flakes and to the ±10 K temperature spacing. For the noisy 4L/Au data, smoothing combined with the second derivative can turn a weak, noisy onset into an apparently gradual one. The authors should extract peak positions from the raw spectra at each measured temperature (fits with uncertainties), show these alongside Fig. 3p-t, and state whether the same smoothing was used for every sample.

6. The excitonic versus electron-lattice attribution is over-interpreted. Three inferences need qualification:
   - Absence of the quasi-elastic peak in 4L/Au (Fig. 4t) is taken to "affirm" its excitonic nature. This signal is ×50 weaker, the Rayleigh leakage at 15 cm^-1 was removed by interpolation in exactly the 6L and 4L Au datasets, and the reading is taken at 10 cm^-1, close to the filter edge. Loss of the peak below the noise floor or distortion by the leakage removal has not been excluded. The authors should show raw, uncorrected low-energy spectra and a noise estimate for the 4L and 6L Au data.
   - The near-constant maximum of -1/q (0.38 against 0.45) is read as unchanged electron-lattice coupling. But q depends on the continuum's density of states and on its coupling, and both change with doping and screening. The Fano parameter does not separately measure electron-lattice coupling strength.
   - The statement that excitonic mechanisms account for about 150 K and "the remaining ~180 K" comes from the lattice (p. 16) assumes the contributions to Tc add linearly. That is not justified, and doping also weakens a purely hybridization- or phonon-driven instability, as in electron-doped CDW systems.
   The authors should either soften these statements to "consistent with" or support them with a model calculation showing that screening and doping suppress the two mechanisms differently.

7. Laser heating and the effective sample temperature are not documented per sample. The laser power is about 1 mW, and a Stokes/anti-Stokes check is mentioned, but no values are given. The two stacks differ in absorption (metal mirror against 50-100 nm Al2O3 over Au) and in thermal anchoring (direct Au contact against an ALD oxide layer). Heating by even 10-30 K would shift the apparent Tc of individual samples by an amount comparable to the 40 K Al2O3 suppression. The authors should report the Stokes/anti-Stokes temperature against nominal temperature for each flake, a power dependence at one temperature near Tc, and the spot size. They should also state that the Bose correction (Eq. 2) uses the calibrated temperature.

8. The 40 K suppression in 4L/Al2O3 is left unexplained, and the thickness series is not matched. The baseline for "no interface effect" is itself 40 K below bulk. The comparison set (7L and 4L on Al2O3; 18L, 6L and 4L on Au) leaves no matched pair except 4L. The authors should add 6L on Al2O3 so that Al2O3 and Au can be compared at the second thickness where broadening is claimed. They should also discuss whether the 4L/Al2O3 suppression reflects dimensionality, strain or the oxide interface, and how this baseline enters the 150 K estimate (relative to 4L/Al2O3 the Au-induced suppression is about 100 K, not 150 K).

9. Hysteresis and thermal cycling are not reported. The structural transition is first-order-like in some reports, and flakes bonded to evaporated films may strain differently on successive cycles. The authors should state whether the data were taken on cooling or warming, and whether Tc and the broadening are reproduced after a second thermal cycle on the Au flakes.

C. Minor

10. On p. 12, "see Fig. 3o" should read Fig. 4o (the -1/q comparison).
11. Thickness assignment by AFM on evaporated Au (which conforms to the surface ripples) and on Al2O3 deserves a stated uncertainty in layer number in the main text, because the 4L/6L distinction is central. Optical contrast or the thickness dependence of low-frequency interlayer modes, if any are observed, could corroborate it.
12. For Fig. 2f, the "bare Al2O3" reference is 50-100 nm Al2O3 on Au/Cr/wafer. The Methods should state this explicitly, because the stack affects both the background and the optical interference.
13. The text asymmetry should be made explicit. The structural Tc of 18L/Au (320 K) and its electronic Tc (306 ± 14 K) differ by 14 K, while the 7L/Al2O3 values (320 K and 330 K) bracket bulk. Comment on whether the 15%-of-maximum criterion is biased by the asymmetric peak shape.
14. Fig. 3 and Fig. 4 colour maps use different arbitrary scales per panel. Add a line to the captions stating that intensity comparisons between substrates are not meaningful because of the different optical stacks. Alternatively, normalize to a substrate-independent reference.
15. The flakes are exfoliated in Ar or vacuum but measured in a cryostat. Say whether they were exposed to air, and for how long, before measurement, because top-surface degradation would also produce a depth-dependent Tc.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Build an interface series that separates the mechanisms. Use Au with and without an ultrathin insulating spacer, plus a high-work-function metal (Pt), on 4L flakes from the same crystal, measured with identical optics and smoothing. This would turn the qualitative screening/charge-transfer argument into a test.
2. Add an independent probe of doping and the gap on the Au-supported flakes. Options include XPS/ARPES through the evaporated-Au route (the transfer can end in UHV, as the authors note), or low-temperature optical reflectance of the gap edge.
3. Replace the kink heuristics with a quantitative distributed-Tc model that includes the transfer-matrix depth weighting. Fit it jointly to the Ag(m5) intensity, the peak shifts and -1/q for all flakes, so that "vertical gradient" and "uniform suppression plus lateral spread" can be compared directly.
4. Use the transfer method to make a laterally patterned Au/Al2O3 substrate under one flake. Raman line-scans across the boundary would test the interface effect on a single flake and remove sample-to-sample variation entirely. This would also be a first demonstration of the junctions proposed in the Conclusions.
