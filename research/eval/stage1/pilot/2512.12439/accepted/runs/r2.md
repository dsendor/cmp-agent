Review outline:

1. Significance and novelty

The authors use evaporation-assisted exfoliation and Au cold-welding transfer to make few-layer Ta2NiSe5 flakes on Al2O3 and on Au. With polarized Raman spectroscopy they track the structural transition (onset of the monoclinic Ag(m4)/Ag(m5) modes in the ac channel) and the electronic transition (the Fano asymmetry of B2g(o1) and the quasi-elastic response at 10 cm^-1) as a function of thickness and substrate. The main result is that 4L and 6L flakes on Au show a transition that is lowered and broadened, with an average Tc of about 220–250 K, while 4L on Al2O3 stays sharp at about 280 K. The authors attribute this to interfacial screening and charge transfer from Au. From it they infer that excitonic physics accounts for up to about 150 K of the 326 K transition. The fabrication method and the idea of substrate-controlled Tc in an excitonic insulator candidate are timely. However, the step from "Au substrate broadens the transition" to "excitonic mechanisms account for about 150 K of Tc" rests on a small data set and on interpretive assumptions that are not yet tested.

2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- A practical all-dry transfer method (Fig. 1c) that gives few-layer Ta2NiSe5 flakes with lateral sizes of 10–100 µm, a regime that is hard to reach by tape exfoliation.
- Raman spectra of few-layer flakes are cleanly symmetry-resolved, with bare-substrate controls in both channels (Fig. 2f, g). These controls justify using the ac channel.
- Structural and electronic signatures are tracked separately and agree in the cases where both can be extracted (Fig. 5a, b).
- The 18L-on-Au flake reproduces the bulk Tc, an internal check that Au does not perturb thick flakes.
- Data are deposited on Zenodo.

3. Concerns

A. Must address

1. Each substrate/thickness combination is a single flake, and the decisive comparison rests on two flakes. The whole conclusion rests on 4L/Au versus 4L/Al2O3 (Figs. 3, 4, 5), and each is one flake. The data on Al2O3 also show that Tc depends steeply on thickness: 7L gives 320 K and 4L gives 280 K. So a one-layer error in thickness, or a flake that is locally thinner in the laser spot, could move Tc by tens of kelvin. Lateral thickness variation inside the spot could also broaden the transition. The paper shows no second 4L/Au or 4L/Al2O3 flake, no spread between flakes, and no spatial map of Tc across a flake. The authors should measure at least two or three additional flakes for each of the key combinations (4L/Au, 4L/Al2O3, and ideally 6L/Al2O3, which is missing and needed to separate the thickness effect from the substrate effect at 6L). They should also show the AFM thickness of the measured spot (not only the flake average) relative to the laser spot size.

2. Strain and interface chemistry are not excluded as the cause of the reduced and broadened Tc on Au. The two substrate stacks differ in much more than conductivity:
   - In the Au case, Au is thermally evaporated directly onto the Ta2NiSe5 surface. The authors themselves say this makes the Au conform to the surface ripples and bind more strongly.
   - In the Al2O3 case, the flake sees 50–100 nm of ALD Al2O3 grown from TMA/H2O at 100–150 °C, with Au further below.

   Ta2NiSe5 is known to be very sensitive to uniaxial pressure and strain (Refs. 14–17). A rippled, strongly bonded Au layer with a thermal expansion coefficient very different from that of the flake and the ALD oxide could impose strain that is inhomogeneous and depends on temperature. That alone could lower and broaden Tc. Hot Au adatoms could also react with or diffuse into the first Se layer. The paper offers no direct evidence for either charge transfer or screening: no phonon frequency shifts attributable to doping, no measurement of the doping level, and no metal with a different work function. The authors should:
   - compare the high-temperature (orthorhombic) phonon frequencies and linewidths of 4L/Au with those of 4L/Al2O3 and 18L/Au, as a gauge of strain;
   - add a control with a thin insulating spacer (for example a few nm of Al2O3 or hBN) between the flake and Au, which keeps the Au and its mechanical environment but removes charge transfer;
   - if possible, include XPS or cross-sectional TEM of the Ta2NiSe5/Au interface.

   Without these, "charge transfer and electrostatic screening are the likely culprit" (p. 15) is one hypothesis among several.

3. The optical field profile and intensity on Au conflict with the layer-gradient interpretation and with the claim that the quasi-elastic peak is missing. On a metallic mirror, the 633 nm standing wave has a near-node at the Au surface. Raman scattering from the layers closest to Au should therefore be suppressed relative to the top layers, yet the discussion (p. 13) relies on the interfacial layers being "detectable in the overall signal". The ac signal of 4L/Au is also about 50–100 times weaker than that of 4L/Al2O3 (scale factors ×50 in Figs. 3e and 4e; colour bars 0.57 against 52.6). The authors should:
   - compute the depth-resolved Raman sensitivity (a transfer-matrix calculation of the incident and scattered fields) for both stacks, including the Al2O3/Au stack below the Al2O3 flakes, and use it as the layer weights in the toy model of Supplementary Note 4;
   - show that a quasi-elastic peak of the expected relative size would have been detectable in 4L/Au at the achieved signal-to-noise. "Undetectable" (p. 12) is used on p. 15 as positive evidence that excitonic fluctuations are destroyed. This only holds if the absence is not a sensitivity limit. The intensity at 10 cm^-1 should be normalized to an internal reference such as the integrated Ag(m2)/B2g(o1) intensity.

4. Laser heating differs between substrates and is not quantified. The laser power is about 1 mW, and the effective temperature was "checked" by the Stokes/anti-Stokes ratio (p. 18), but no values are given. A few-layer flake on 50–100 nm of amorphous Al2O3 is much more thermally isolated than one in direct contact with Au. This difference could shift the apparent Tc of the Al2O3 flakes (the 40 K reduction of 4L/Al2O3) by an amount that differs from the Au flakes. It is also not stated how the measurements above 300 K (up to 400 K) were made, or whether they used the same cryostat. The authors should:
   - report the Stokes/anti-Stokes temperature against nominal temperature for each flake;
   - show a power dependence of a Tc marker (for example the Ag(m5) intensity near Tc) for 4L/Al2O3 and 4L/Au;
   - use the corrected temperature in the Bose factor of Eq. (2).

B. Should address

5. The extracted transition width depends on an ad hoc construction and on smoothing. The ±40 K broadening on Au (p. 9–10) is inferred by assuming that the "second kink" of Ag(m4) lies at a fixed 60 K below Tc. That spacing comes from only three flakes and has no physical justification. In addition, the 2D maps are Gaussian-smoothed with σ = 10–15 K in temperature (p. 19), comparable to the 10 K measurement steps near Tc. Fig. 3k–o are second derivatives of these smoothed maps. If the peak positions in Fig. 3p–t were taken from the smoothed second-derivative maps, the kinks are artificially broadened. The authors should:
   - state whether Fig. 3p–t uses raw or smoothed spectra;
   - extract the onset width directly from the integrated intensity of Ag(m4)/Ag(m5) against T, fitted with an order-parameter form plus a distribution of Tc, for all five flakes;
   - justify or drop the "second kink" argument.

6. The electronic Tc of 4L/Au is assumed, not measured. On p. 12 the value Tc^avg ≈ 220 ± 46 K is obtained by shifting the 6L −1/q curve by 30 K and borrowing the 6L width. Fig. 5b then plots it alongside the structural value as if it were independent, and the statement that the electronic and structural values "agree" is partly circular for this flake. The authors should mark this point as an estimate in Fig. 5b or remove it. Alternatively, they could extract a width from a fit of −1/q(T), for example its inflection point and width, applied uniformly to all flakes. (The text also cites "Fig. 3o" for the −1/q comparison; this should be Fig. 4o.)

7. The decomposition of Tc into roughly 150 K excitonic plus roughly 180 K lattice is not supported. The argument (p. 15–16) rests on two observations: the quasi-elastic peak is suppressed while the maximum of −1/q is reduced by only 16% (0.38 against 0.45). Several problems weaken it:
   - Tc contributions from different mechanisms are not additive in general.
   - The quasi-elastic suppression may be a sensitivity issue (concern 3).
   - Even in the mixed-phase picture, the interfacial layers do not reach a monoclinic ground state above the lowest measured temperature, so "suppression by up to 150 K" is a lower bound on the perturbation of those layers, not a measure of the excitonic share.
   - Strain or chemistry (concern 2) would also reduce Tc without being specifically excitonic.

   The authors should present the 150 K figure as a hypothesis consistent with the data, or support it with a model, for example a Landau or mean-field model with exciton-lattice coupling in which screening reduces only the excitonic coupling, so that the size of the predicted Tc shift can be compared.

8. Details of the Fano fit and its robustness are missing. Eq. (1) has no explicit background term. In 6L/4L on Au, the broad Au electronic background and the Rayleigh leakage at 15 cm^-1 (removed by interpolation, p. 19) fall inside the 20–80 cm^-1 fitting window. The authors should state which parameters are free, whether a background is included, and how sensitive −1/q is to the fitting window and to the interpolated Rayleigh region. For the 6L/4L flakes on Au, where Fig. 4n, o carries the electronic argument, they should show residuals. The same applies to the 10 cm^-1 cut in Fig. 4p–t, which lies close to the removed 15 cm^-1 spurious peak.

9. Thickness assignment for the flakes on Al2O3 and Au. The layer counts (4L, 6L, 7L, 18L) are central, and the AFM procedure is deferred to Supplementary Notes 5–6, which are not in the submitted PDF. Two points need explicit treatment in the main text:
   - On cold-welded Au and on Al2O3/Au, the step height can include an interfacial gap or an Au roughness contribution. How were these accounted for?
   - Was the uncertainty in layer number (±1L) carried into the Tc-versus-thickness plot in Fig. 5?

10. The surface-layer Tc in the mixed-phase picture is unclear. The model assumes that the layers far from Au have Tc "closer to room temperature" (p. 13). But 4L/Al2O3, with no claimed interface effect, already has Tc ≈ 280 K. The authors should state which Tc they assign to the top layers of 4L/Au: bulk, the 4L/Al2O3 value, or a free parameter. They should show that the toy model (Supplementary Note 4) reproduces Fig. 3t and Fig. 4o quantitatively with physically motivated weights (see concern 3). The claim that the interface effect is "confined to one or two layers" (p. 13–14) should also be reconciled with the 6L/Au flake. If only 1–2 of 6 layers were affected, one would expect a clearly weaker broadening than in 4L, yet the two show similar widths (±40 K).

11. Leakage-mode reliance. The structural Tc of the sharp samples is defined by a kink in the position of Ag(o3)*, a leakage of an aa mode caused by polarizer misalignment (p. 7, 9). The degree of leakage, and hence the relative weight of this feature against the intrinsic ac modes, can differ between flakes and cooldowns. The authors should confirm the Tc values with a criterion based only on intrinsic ac modes, for example the onset temperature of Ag(m5) intensity, for all five flakes.

C. Minor

12. p. 12: "see Fig. 3o" should read "Fig. 4o".
13. Fig. 4 caption: "(k–o)" uses inconsistent formatting. It is also unclear whether the overlaid curves in Fig. 4n, o are data or smoothed guides; the caption says "dashed/dotted lines".
14. Fig. 5b: indicate which points are directly measured and which are estimated (see concern 6), and add the 4L/Al2O3 electronic Tc error convention to the legend.
15. Methods: "Au pellets (3N5%)" appears to be a typo for 3N5 (99.95%).
16. The 7L/Al2O3 electronic Tc of 330 ± 10 K is above the bulk value. Comment on whether this reflects the 15% criterion or the temperature calibration.
17. Report the laser spot size and give the penetration depth used in the statement "the penetration depth of photons exceeds the thickness" (p. 7).
18. The supplementary information is referenced repeatedly (Notes 1–6) but was not part of the submitted file. Make sure it accompanies the manuscript, since several key steps (azimuthal symmetry assignment, AFM, the toy model) are only there.

4. Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a spacer control (Ta2NiSe5/thin hBN or Al2O3/Au) and a second metal with a different work function. These would separate screening and charge transfer from strain and chemistry, and would test the doping direction the authors propose for hole doping.
2. Measure several flakes per key condition and show spatial Raman maps of the Ag(m5) onset across a 4L/Au flake. This would establish reproducibility and distinguish a vertical Tc gradient from lateral inhomogeneity.
3. Include a transfer-matrix calculation of depth-resolved Raman sensitivity for each stack, feed it into the layer-resolved toy model, and normalize the low-energy response to an internal phonon reference before drawing conclusions about the quasi-elastic peak.
4. Reframe the "150 K excitonic, 180 K lattice" split as a qualitative bound unless it can be supported by a coupled exciton-lattice model calculation.
