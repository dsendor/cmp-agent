A. Must address

1. **The dispersion law used to extract J^orb is the next-nearest-neighbour form, so it cannot support the nearest-neighbour conclusion for PrNiO2, and the fitted J^orb does not reproduce the bandwidths marked in Fig. 5.** Eq. (1), ε_k = 2J^orb cos(k_x)cos(k_y), is taken from Ref. 43, where it describes diagonal (NNN) orbiton hopping in CaCuO2 (cos(k_x+k_y) + cos(k_x−k_y) = 2cos k_x cos k_y). The same function is fitted to PrNiO2 to obtain J^orb = +14 ± 4 meV, and that positive coefficient is then read as nearest-neighbour orbital superexchange (pp. 6–7) and set against the calculated J^orb_NN = 14.5 meV (Table III). NN hopping on a square lattice gives a dispersion ∝ (cos k_x + cos k_y), with the extremum at M opposite to Γ; flipping the sign of the cos k_x cos k_y coefficient does not turn an NNN process into an NN one. The two forms differ in the shape of the dispersion along [H,H] relative to [H,0], not in sign: the NN form gives equal bandwidths of 4J from Γ to (0.5,0) and from Γ to (0.25,0.25), with the energy still changing towards (0.34,0.34), while the NNN form gives 4J along [H,0] but only about 1.4J from Γ to (0.34,0.34). The quoted numbers also do not match the fit. With J^orb = 14 meV, Eq. (1) predicts about 56 meV from Γ to (0.475,0) and about 20 meV from Γ to (0.34,0.34), whereas Fig. 5(a) marks 90 meV and 62 meV; the [H,H] branch is off by a factor of about 3. For CaCuO2, J^orb = −11 meV gives 44 and 22 meV, against 56 and 34 meV marked in Fig. 5(c). Eq. (1) also has no constant on-site term, although the data sit at about 1.3 and 1.65 eV, and the Fig. 5(a) caption calls the continuous line a "charge transfer model fitting" while the text says it is the Eq. (1) fit. As it stands the reported J^orb, and its agreement with theory in Table III, are not meaningful. The authors should write the full dispersion, ε0 + 2J_NN(cos k_x + cos k_y) + 4J_NNN cos k_x cos k_y; fit the [H,0] and [H,H] branches of both compounds jointly with NN-only, NNN-only and NN+NNN forms; report parameters, uncertainties, the J_NN–J_NNN correlation, χ² and the [H,0]/[H,H] bandwidth ratios for each; overlay all fitted curves on the data along both cuts; state which form is used for which compound; reconcile the marked bandwidths (62/90 meV and 34/56 meV) with the fit, stating whether they are differences between fitted points or of the fitted curve, with uncertainties; and correct the caption. The central claim, NN propagation in the nickelate against NNN in the cuprate, should rest on that comparison.

2. **The PrNiO2 d_xy dispersion has not been shown to be robust against fitting and geometry artefacts.** The claimed dispersion (60–90 meV) is extracted from a Gaussian roughly 0.5 eV wide (Fig. 2(c)) that overlaps the 0.7 eV "RE peak", the d_xz/yz peak at 1.93 eV (about 0.6 eV higher), the d_z² component and an unpolarized charge continuum or broad tail (Fig. 4(a–c)). At fixed 2θ = 149.5° the incidence angle changes continuously along the cut, from grazing incidence on one side to grazing emission on the other, and L changes with it. Fig. 4 shows that the relative cross-sections of d_xy, d_xz/yz and the continuum depend strongly on geometry, on the sign of H and on polarization, and the polarization character of the 1.29 eV peak itself changes with H (p. 6). Self-absorption through the STO cap and the film, which can distort Ni L3 line shapes, also varies with geometry. With free widths and intensities, a centroid shift of tens of meV can therefore arise purely from momentum-dependent component weights, and the sign of the effect relative to CaCuO2, where the d_xy peak is narrow and well separated (Fig. 2(d), Fig. 5(d)), could differ for instrumental rather than physical reasons. It is also notable that the maximum of the d_xy energy in Fig. 5(a) coincides with the specular point, where the elastic and low-energy intensity is largest (Fig. 1(e)). Fig. 5(b) shows the stack only with fixed vertical guide lines, against which no shift is visible by eye. The authors should: state which fit parameters (widths, positions and amplitudes of neighbouring peaks, the RE-peak position and weight, the continuum shape) are free, fixed or shared across Q; refit with fixed versus free widths, with and without the 0.7 eV component and the continuum, with a different continuum model, with constrained versus free d_xz/yz positions, and with a model-independent first moment over a fixed window; compare +H and −H branches at equal |H|, which are equivalent by symmetry but measured in different geometries, so an intrinsic dispersion must be symmetric; show σ- versus π-incidence cuts as an independent check; give the d_xy linewidth and its Q dependence; estimate, from the single-ion polarization and geometry factors, how large an apparent shift overlapping peaks of these widths can produce; discuss geometry-dependent self-absorption; and overlay raw, normalized spectra at Γ and at the zone boundary so the shift can be seen without a fit.

3. **J⊥ and the claim of "three-dimensional antiferromagnetic order" rest on a gap near Γ measured at an uncontrolled, varying L, and order itself is not demonstrated.** The abstract and p. 4 conclude that both materials "support a three-dimensional antiferromagnetic order", and J⊥ = 5.9 ± 1.1 meV for PrNiO2 (Fig. 3(a)) is justified only by the magnon energy staying at 60–80 meV near the in-plane zone centre "at non-integer L". The Methods state that L is not fixed during H scans and that "this is not an issue, since the in-plane Js are still dominating", which contradicts using the same data to extract J⊥. With 2θ = 149.5° at 852.4 eV and c = 3.28 Å, L varies from roughly 0.44 r.l.u. at Γ to roughly 0.18 r.l.u. at H = 0.475 (similarly for CaCuO2), so J⊥ is constrained essentially by a handful of points near Γ. Those points lie within about 1.5–2 resolution widths (40 meV) of a very strong specular elastic line (Fig. 1(e), Fig. 2(a)) and close to the phonon, and carry the largest error bars in Fig. 3(a). The LSW fit does not exclude other explanations for a finite energy at Γ: an exchange or single-ion anisotropy gap (easy-plane/XY, raised in Ref. 59, which the text acknowledges is hard to separate and then sets aside), an artefact of the elastic/phonon subtraction, finite film thickness (15–20 unit cells), the paramagnon character of a system without long-range order, or Pr 4f–Ni coupling. The LSW model presupposes a specific 3D Néel order whose stacking vector is not stated, while no magnetic Bragg peak or temperature dependence is shown and the introduction itself notes that bulk infinite-layer nickelates show no long-range order and show spin-glass behaviour (Refs. 13, 19, 20); a dispersion consistent with LSW does not establish long-range 3D order. The authors should tabulate L for every point in Fig. 3(a) and include it explicitly in the SpinW fit; show the Γ-point spectra, their decomposition and the sensitivity of J⊥ to them; compare fits with J⊥ = 0 plus an anisotropy gap against fits with J⊥ ≠ 0 and no anisotropy, with goodness of fit; ideally measure the magnon at fixed in-plane momentum near Γ (and at (0.5,0)) while varying L, by changing 2θ or the incident energy within the resonance, to show whether the energy follows the LSW J⊥ form or an L-independent gap; and, short of direct evidence of order, change "support a 3D antiferromagnetic order" to "show three-dimensional spin correlations" or "interlayer spin correlations".

4. **The orbital argument relies on a magnetic string effect that is asserted, not computed, and that contradicts the paper's own magnetic conclusions.** The charge-transfer model in Table III contains no magnon coupling. For CaCuO2 it gives a positive |J_NN| = 22 meV that exceeds |J_NNN| = 15 meV, yet the experiment is attributed to NNN propagation by invoking suppression of NN hopping by magnon dressing (the string effect) outside the model. For PrNiO2 the same suppression is argued to be "absent or much weaker" because "the nickelate … does not present evidence of long-range AFM order" (p. 6). But the spin section reports a sharp, weakly damped magnon with J1 ≈ 46 meV, a sizeable fraction of the CaCuO2 value, and argues for 3D AFM order (concern 3); the two statements cannot both support the conclusions. The string effect depends on antiferromagnetic correlations over a few lattice spacings on the timescale of orbiton hopping, not on static long-range order. The authors should give a quantitative argument, at least an estimate based on the measured J1 and the spin correlation length (for example the ratio J^orb_NN/J1 that controls the string effect in the Wohlfeld et al. framework, or the orbiton–magnon coupling relative to J1), for why NN hopping is suppressed in CaCuO2 but not in PrNiO2; or present the NN/NNN interpretation as a hypothesis and make the abstract consistent with it.

5. **The charge-transfer model parameters are partly tuned, so the agreement between theory (14.5 meV) and experiment (14 ± 4 meV) is not independent support.** In Table II the PrNiO2 hoppings are set to 80% of the CaCuO2 values, and Δ = 8.00 eV was "chosen sufficiently larger than U also providing reasonable results" (p. 7), which is effectively tuning against the outcome; literature electronic-structure estimates of Δ for infinite-layer nickelates are commonly a few eV, substantially below 8 eV. U = 3.2 eV for Ni (from Refs. 5 and 25) is far below the 8 eV used for Cu and the 5–7 eV usually assumed for nickelates, and those references give effective one-band U values rather than the on-site multiplet Hubbard U a multiband charge-transfer model requires. It is also not checked that the on-site parameters (ε_xy = 0.70 eV, ε_πσ = −1.00 eV) are consistent with the measured dd energies in Table I. The authors should list all parameters with their sources and justify the PrNiO2 set against literature estimates; show J_NN and J_NNN as functions of Δ (for example 3 or 4 to 10 eV), U (for example 3 to 7 eV), t_pd and the hopping scaling; state whether the sign of the NN–NNN competition survives across that range; check the on-site energies against Table I; and give the model equations and the definition of orbital superexchange in the Supplement rather than only by reference to Ref. 43.

6. **The magnon damping comparison does not isolate self-doping.** The claim (p. 4, Fig. 3(b)) that the ~100 meV damping in PrNiO2, smaller than the ≳200 meV in UD15K Bi2201, "demonstrates that self-doping has a much milder impact on spin order" than chemical doping does not follow. The self-doped hole count in PrNiO2 is never estimated, so the materials are compared at unknown relative doping. Absolute damping is compared between a system with J1 ≈ 46 meV and Bi2201 with J of order 150 meV; in units of the magnon energy, PrNiO2's damping (about 100 meV against 100–200 meV magnon energies) is not obviously smaller than underdoped Bi2201's (about 250 meV against roughly 300 meV). The Bi2201 values come from a different study (Ref. 49) with a different resolution and possibly a different line-shape and background model. The PrNiO2 value itself depends on the fit model: no continuum is included for PrNiO2 (Fig. 2(a); phonon and magnon overtones are omitted), the magnon overlaps the phonon near Γ, and the authors note that a single-peak fit overestimates the CaCuO2 damping near (0.5,0) because of the multimagnon continuum, so the flat ~100 meV may partly reflect the fit. Damping in a 5 nm topotactically reduced film can also come from inhomogeneous broadening (partial reduction, residual or excess oxygen, stacking faults or c-axis defects, strain relaxation) absent in single crystals. The authors should normalize the damping (γ/ω(q) or γ/J1); refit the Bi2201 raw data, or a representative subset, with the same procedure and resolution convolution, or demonstrate equivalence; show the sensitivity of the PrNiO2 damping to adding a continuum; estimate the self-doped hole density from Ni L3 or O K pre-edge XAS or Hall data on the measured film and place it on the Bi2201 damping-versus-doping plot; compare with Sr-doped infinite-layer nickelates (Ref. 12, Ref. 46) or literature NdNiO2/LaNiO2 damping; or otherwise rephrase the conclusion as a hypothesis.

7. **The LSW exchange constants are weakly constrained, their uncertainties are not credible, and the model adequacy and literature agreement are not shown.** Five parameters (J1–J4, J⊥) are fitted to a single dispersion per compound along one path that does not reach X (it stops at H = 0.475), yet the table in Fig. 3(a) quotes J1 = 45.8 ± 0.3 meV and similarly tight values for J2 to J⊥, implausibly small given the error bars in Fig. 3(a). J1, J2 and J3 are expected to be strongly correlated along M–Γ–X–M, and systematic errors (L variation, elastic/phonon subtraction, and the quantum renormalization factor Z_c, whose use is not stated) are not included. The PrNiO2 [H,H] dispersion maximum lies at (0.2,0.2) rather than (0.25,0.25), with rising elastic intensity beyond (p. 5, Fig. 1(e)); if this reflects stripe-like or incommensurate physics, a Néel-based J1–J4 model may absorb it into J2–J4 and bias them, and it is unclear whether the fit was forced through these points. "In agreement with the literature [12, 24, 46]" needs numbers: Ref. 12 reported about 64 meV for NdNiO2 using a different (2D) parameter set, and the difference from 46 meV should be stated and explained (rare-earth or strain dependence, inclusion of J⊥, different longer-range terms). The authors should report the fit covariance or correlation matrix, uncertainties including systematic contributions, and whether Z_c was applied; test reduced models (J1, J2, J⊥; or J1, J2, J3 with J3 = J2/2 as in a t–t′–U mapping, plus J⊥); show how J1–J4 change when the [H,H] points beyond (0.2,0.2) are excluded; tabulate literature values; and compare model-independent quantities, namely the zone-boundary energies at (0.5,0) and (0.25,0.25), across compounds and references.

9. **The two films sit on different substrates and their strain state is not given, although J1 and the d_xy energies are compared across them.** PrNiO2 is on STO and CaCuO2 on NGO, with in-plane lattice constants of 3.94 versus 3.86 Å; the PrNiO2 value of 3.94 Å is larger than that of SrTiO3 (3.905 Å). The d_xy energy difference is partly attributed to this, but strain could also change the hoppings and thus J1 and the orbiton hopping; part of the factor of about two in J1 may reflect lattice parameter and strain, as Ref. 57 suggests.
