Review outline:

**1. Significance and novelty**

- **Topic.** The manuscript uses angle-resolved surface Brillouin–Mandelstam scattering (BMS) to track the Rayleigh surface acoustic wave (RW) in a ~220 nm exfoliated NiPS₃ flake on SiO₂(300 nm)/Si between 80 and 300 K.
- **Central claim.** The RW phase velocity softens by ~5.5% (≈1920 → ≈1815 m s⁻¹) on cooling through T_N ≈ 155 K. The authors attribute this to a "giant" magnetoelastic renormalization of the long-wavelength elastic tensor caused by zigzag AFM order.
- **Supporting work.** The claim is backed by:
  - temperature-dependent Raman spectroscopy, showing weak optical-phonon anomalies;
  - DFT (PBE-D3) elastic tensors for AFM and non-magnetic (NM) states;
  - COMSOL eigenmode modelling of the heterostructure.
- **Why it matters.** Acoustic and elastic responses of exfoliated vdW magnets are largely unexplored, because ultrasound and inelastic neutron scattering are ill-suited to micron-scale flakes. A spectroscopic route to magnetoelastic constants in such samples would interest the 2D-magnetism, magnetoacoustics and phononics communities.
- **Conceptual framing.** The contrast between bond-scale (optical phonon, ⟨S_i·S_j⟩) and continuum-scale (strain, elastic tensor) spin–lattice coupling is well articulated and pedagogically useful.
- **Novelty is moderate rather than transformative.**
  - BMS of acoustic phonons in MPS₃ crystals has been reported before (incl. Ref. 36 by the same group).
  - Elastic anomalies at magnetic transitions are textbook physics (Landau order-parameter–strain coupling).
  - The genuinely new element is the claimed size of the RW anomaly in a supported thin film. Its robustness therefore determines the paper's impact.
- **Scope of the "2D" framing.** A 220 nm flake (~350 layers) is bulk-like magnetically. The title and abstract framing ("2D antiferromagnetic semiconductor", "strict 2D limit") overstate what is actually probed.

**2. Potential reasons for acceptance**

- **Timely, broadly relevant question.** The paper asks how long-range magnetic order renormalizes macroscopic elasticity in vdW magnets. This bears on strain-mediated spintronics, magnon–phonon hybridization and magnetoacoustic devices.
- **Experimentally accessible methodology.**
  - Surface BMS with in-plane wavevector selection (q = 4π sinθ/λ) is well suited to exfoliated flakes.
  - The dispersion measurement (θ = 30–50°) and slow-on-fast interpretation are physically sensible.
- **Multi-pronged approach.** Combining Raman, BMS, DFT elastic constants and finite-element modelling gives a reasonably complete picture of how the mode is identified.
- **Credible mode assignment.**
  - The bare-substrate BMS control (Supp. Fig. 5) supports assigning the peak to the NiPS₃ film.
  - The FEM displacement field and the decreasing-velocity trend qualitatively support the Rayleigh-wave identification.
- **Useful data for the community.** The tabulated 13-component elastic tensor (AFM vs NM) and the c_ij sensitivity analysis (Supp. Table 2) are valuable for others modelling MPS₃ mechanics and heterostructures.
- **Clear contrast with Raman.** The observation that optical-phonon anomalies are weak, while an acoustic probe shows a percent-level effect, is potentially interesting. If substantiated, it motivates acoustic probes of magnetoelasticity in other vdW magnets.

**3. Potential reasons for rejection**

**Reason 1 — The central experimental result rests on sparse, low-quality temperature data that do not unambiguously establish a transition-linked anomaly.**

- **Sparse sampling where it matters most.**
  - Fig. 4(b) contains only seven temperatures (≈90, 120, 130, 150, 200, 250, 300 K).
  - There are no data between 150 and 200 K, exactly where the claimed anomaly occurs.
  - The "drop at T_N" is therefore a difference between two isolated points 50 K apart. Its onset, width and shape (step vs cusp vs gradual crossover) cannot be determined, and its pinning to T_N ≈ 155 K is not demonstrated.
- **The 5.5% figure depends on the chosen reference point.**
  - It is computed from the maximum value at 200 K (~1925 m s⁻¹), not from a fitted paramagnetic baseline.
  - Relative to 300 K (~1865 m s⁻¹), the low-temperature value (~1805–1835 m s⁻¹) is only ~2–3% lower.
  - The paramagnetic phase itself shows a ~3% stiffening between 300 and 200 K. That is a dv/dT several times larger than typical anharmonic behaviour in layered crystals, and it is unexplained. It may indicate that short-range correlations, substrate effects, or experimental drift already act well above T_N.
  - A quantitative anomaly should be defined against an extrapolated anharmonic baseline (e.g., a Varshni/Einstein-type fit), with uncertainty propagated.
- **Unreliable spectra at low temperature.**
  - In Fig. 4(a), the RW peak intensity collapses on cooling; the 80–130 K spectra are barely above background on a sloping central-peak tail.
  - The dashed "guide to the eye" shifts by only ~0.1 GHz, whereas the quoted velocities imply f ≈ 4.15 GHz at 200 K and ≈ 3.90 GHz at 150 K (f = vq/2π, q = 0.01355 nm⁻¹). This inconsistency between spectra and extracted velocities must be resolved.
  - Error bars at 90 and 120 K (±25 m s⁻¹) are comparable to the intra-AFM scatter. Their derivation (fit covariance? repeated runs?) is not stated.
  - Linewidth and intensity versus temperature are not reported. Acoustic attenuation peaks near T_N would be a much stronger, classic signature of critical magnetoelastic coupling.
- **Internal inconsistency at room temperature.**
  - At q ≈ 0.0135 nm⁻¹, Fig. 3(b) gives v ≈ 1945 m s⁻¹ (f ≈ 4.2 GHz).
  - Fig. 4(b) gives ≈1865 m s⁻¹ at 300 K for nominally the same geometry.
  - This ~4% spread in a nominally identical measurement is comparable to the claimed magnetic effect. It points to systematic errors (angle setting, focus, sample position in the cryostat, laser heating) that are not addressed.
- **No reproducibility evidence.**
  - Only one flake, one thickness, one wavevector, one propagation direction and apparently one cooling run are presented.
  - There is no heating/cooling cycle and no hysteresis check.

**Reason 2 — Non-magnetic and extrinsic origins of the velocity change are not excluded.**

- **Substrate contribution is uncontrolled.**
  - At q = 0.0135 nm⁻¹ the acoustic wavelength (~465 nm) exceeds the film thickness. The authors themselves show (Supp. Fig. 7) that the RW penetrates substantially into the 300 nm SiO₂ and into Si.
  - Amorphous SiO₂ has anomalous elasticity: its moduli decrease on cooling over this temperature range. Any temperature-dependent change in the substrate therefore enters the RW velocity directly.
  - A temperature-dependent BMS measurement of the bare SiO₂/Si substrate (and ideally a non-magnetic film on the same substrate) is indispensable but absent.
- **Thermal-mismatch strain is ignored.**
  - On cooling, the differential thermal contraction between NiPS₃, SiO₂ and Si imposes temperature-dependent biaxial strain and possibly partial interfacial slip.
  - Either could alter the effective boundary condition and the RW velocity independently of magnetism.
  - Strain also shifts T_N; Raman/PL could be used to quantify it.
- **Missing non-magnetic reference sample.**
  - No isostructural non-magnetic analogue (e.g., ZnPS₃ or CdPS₃) or other MPS₃ with a different T_N (FePS₃ ≈ 118 K, MnPS₃ ≈ 78 K) is measured.
  - Showing that the anomaly tracks T_N across compounds would be the most convincing proof of magnetic origin.
- **Sample temperature in the BMS measurement is unverified.**
  - BMS laser power and the in-situ temperature calibration are not reported. Laser heating in a semiconducting flake (532 nm is above the gap) could offset the true temperature by tens of kelvin.
  - T_N is not independently verified in the BMS-measured flake, e.g., via the 1.47 eV spin-entangled exciton PL, two-magnon Raman, or Raman 176 cm⁻¹ mode behaviour.
- **Cryostat geometry and density effects.**
  - Thermal contraction of the cold-finger mount can tilt the sample and change θ, and hence q. A 1° error at 35° changes q by ~2.5%, the same order as the effect.
  - Density changes from magnetostriction across T_N (known in MPS₃) are not considered in v ∝ √(c/ρ).

**Reason 3 — Theory does not support, and partly contradicts, the quantitative interpretation.**

- **Order-of-magnitude mismatch between DFT/FEM and experiment.**
  - DFT/FEM predict a 0.54% RW frequency decrease, versus ~5.5% measured (stated inconsistently as "~6%").
  - Invoking "finite-temperature effects" without calculation is not an explanation. The theory thus confirms only the sign, which is weak corroboration given that DFT uncertainties in c_ij are of the same order.
  - The absolute agreement is also limited: 4.6 vs 4.2 GHz, and simulated velocities ~1985–2210 m s⁻¹ vs measured ~1700–1950 m s⁻¹. These ~10% offsets exceed the effect being explained.
- **The authors' own sensitivity analysis contradicts their conclusion.**
  - The RW velocity is most sensitive to c₃₃ and c₄₄.
  - In DFT, c₄₄ is unchanged (9.7 → 9.7 GPa) and c₃₃ drops ~8%.
  - The largest DFT change (c₆₆, +17%) is a shear-horizontal constant that barely affects a sagittal Rayleigh wave propagating along b.
  - The conclusion that the data "imply a sizable reduction in the shear- and out-of-plane-related elastic coefficients" is therefore an inference not supported by the presented calculations. It requires quantification: what Δc₄₄ and Δc₃₃ are needed to reproduce 5.5%? Note that the stated 11% modulus reduction from Δc/c ≈ 2Δv/v neglects substrate participation, so the film modulus change required would be even larger.
- **Questionable DFT methodology.**
  - **No Hubbard U.** The calculations use plain PBE-D3 for Ni²⁺ d⁸ without U. Standard practice for NiPS₃ is DFT+U to recover the insulating gap and correct exchange constants and magnetic moments.
  - **NM is a poor paramagnetic proxy.** Quenching the S = 1 local moments changes bonding, Ni–S distances and possibly the metallicity. The NM–AFM difference thus conflates moment formation with magnetic ordering. A paramagnet retains local moments; better proxies are spin-disordered/SQS configurations, averages over several collinear configurations, or comparison of AFM with FM/other AFM orderings.
  - **Missing technical details.** Required are lattice parameters for both states, the role of the c-axis D3 correction in c₃₃/c₄₄, convergence of c_ij with k-mesh, Gaussian smearing in an insulator, the strain amplitude (1.5% is large for soft interlayer constants), and the treatment of ionic relaxation.
- **The phenomenological model is heuristic and partly incorrect.**
  - The expression c_eff = c⁽⁰⁾ − λ²χ_m is asserted without derivation, and χ_m in an AFM does not produce a step-like softening below T_N.
  - Standard Landau treatment of a strain–order-parameter coupling (λεL²) predicts a discontinuity Δc ∝ λ²/b at a second-order T_N plus fluctuation contributions. This should be used and fitted, ideally linked to measured magnetostriction or thermal-expansion anomalies of NiPS₃.
  - The "giant" label lacks a quantitative benchmark: no magnetoelastic coupling constants are extracted or compared with values from bulk ultrasound or dilatometry in MPS₃, Cr trihalides or oxides.

**Reason 4 — Supporting characterization and claims are insufficiently rigorous; several statements are overstated or inaccurate.**

- **Crystallographic orientation is ambiguous.**
  - Cleavage edges in MPS₃ form 60°/120° angles in a pseudo-hexagonal lattice. Distinguishing the monoclinic a and b axes from edges alone is ambiguous: the b-direction and the directions 60° from it are nearly equivalent in-plane for the stiffness, but not for the magnetic zigzag order.
  - Angle-resolved polarized Raman or SHG/linear dichroism (which tracks the zigzag axis below T_N) should confirm the orientation.
  - Measuring only along b forgoes a key test: anisotropy of the anomaly along the ferromagnetic-chain axis (a) versus the AFM-coupled axis (b).
- **The Raman section is weakly integrated and partly contradicts literature without resolution.**
  - The authors report no splitting of the ~176 cm⁻¹ mode, contrary to Ref. 25, but offer no explanation (thickness, strain, spectral resolution, 0.024 mW power, temperature step).
  - Concluding that "optical phonons do not provide a sufficiently sensitive probe" compares relative shifts of fundamentally different observables. A percent change in an acoustic velocity and a 0.3 cm⁻¹ linewidth deviation are not comparable measures of coupling strength.
  - Known magnetic Raman signatures of NiPS₃ (two-magnon continuum, Fano asymmetry evolution, low-energy magnons near 10 cm⁻¹) are not exploited to verify T_N in this specific flake.
- **Inaccurate or imprecise statements.**
  - **Loudon's rule is misstated.** Ripple scattering requires an out-of-plane (surface-normal) displacement at the surface, not "displacement perpendicular to propagation". For a partially transparent film, the elasto-optic contribution must also be considered.
  - **Theoretical claim of Ref. 49 is uncritically adopted.** The statement that NiPS₃ has "an order of magnitude higher" spin–phonon coupling than FePS₃ or CrBr₃ rests on a single theoretical work, and different spin–phonon coefficients are not directly comparable.
- **Presentation and typographical errors.**
  - Fig. 2(a) axes are mislabeled (y = "Raman Shift", x = "Temperature").
  - Fig. 4(b) reads "Temperaure".
  - The methods refer to a "linear electrodynamic equation" (should be elastodynamic).
  - The quoted effect is variously "~5.5%" and "~6%".
  - The FEM boundary condition (fixed bottom of finite Si) and domain size are not shown to be converged for low-q modes that penetrate deeply.

**4. Suggestions for improvement**

1. **Strengthen and densify the core BMS dataset.**
   - Measure with ≤5 K steps through 120–220 K, on both cooling and heating, on at least 2–3 flakes of different thickness (e.g., 50–500 nm).
   - Repeat at several wavevectors (θ = 30–50°) at each temperature, so the anomaly is shown in the dispersion and not only at a single q.
   - Report linewidth and integrated intensity versus T (looking for attenuation peaks at T_N), the fit procedure and error-bar derivation, the BMS laser power, and the in-situ temperature calibration.
   - Fit the paramagnetic data to an anharmonic baseline and define Δv/v against it rather than against the 200 K maximum.
   - Resolve the room-temperature discrepancy between Figs. 3(b) and 4(b).
2. **Add decisive control experiments to exclude extrinsic origins.**
   - Temperature-dependent BMS on bare SiO₂/Si under identical geometry.
   - A non-magnetic isostructural film (ZnPS₃ or CdPS₃) and ideally FePS₃ and/or MnPS₃ films of similar thickness on the same substrate, to show that the anomaly tracks each compound's T_N.
   - Measurements along both a and b, to test magnetic anisotropy of the effect.
   - Independent verification of T_N and of the zigzag axis in the measured flake, e.g., the 1.47 eV exciton PL, linear dichroism/SHG, or two-magnon Raman.
   - Consider suspended or hBN-encapsulated flakes, or a stiffer substrate (sapphire, diamond), to reduce substrate participation and thermal-mismatch strain.
3. **Upgrade the theoretical analysis so it quantitatively addresses the measured magnitude.**
   - Use DFT+U (with a U-sensitivity check) and a physically meaningful paramagnetic reference (SQS/spin-disordered supercells or configuration averaging) instead of the NM state.
   - Report lattice parameters and c_ij convergence for each state.
   - Use the FEM model inversely: determine which combinations of Δc₃₃, Δc₄₄ (and Δρ from magnetostriction) reproduce the 5.5% shift, and assess whether these are plausible.
   - Replace the heuristic χ_m formula with a Landau-type strain–order-parameter model. Fit the temperature dependence to extract magnetoelastic coupling constants, and compare them with known thermal-expansion or magnetostriction anomalies of NiPS₃ and with values for other magnets, to justify or retract the "giant" label.
4. **Tighten the framing, Raman discussion and presentation.**
   - Moderate "2D" and "giant/anomalous" claims to match a ~220 nm bulk-like film, or add thickness-dependent data that actually approach the few-layer limit.
   - Either integrate the Raman data meaningfully (use magnetic Raman signatures to calibrate T_N; discuss the discrepancy with Ref. 25 regarding the 176 cm⁻¹ splitting) or condense it into the SI.
   - Correct the statement of Loudon's ripple-scattering selection rule and discuss the elasto-optic contribution.
   - Fix figure labels and typos, make the quoted magnitude (5.5% vs 6%) consistent, and document FEM convergence with respect to Si thickness, mesh and bottom boundary condition.
   - Provide raw spectra with fits for every temperature in the SI so readers can judge peak-extraction reliability at low temperature.
