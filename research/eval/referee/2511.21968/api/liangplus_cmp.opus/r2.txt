**Review outline:**

### 1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript uses angle-resolved surface Brillouin scattering on a ~220 nm exfoliated NiPS3 flake on SiO2/Si to track a Rayleigh-type surface acoustic wave through T_N. It reports a ~5.5% drop in phase velocity, from ~1920 to ~1815 m s⁻¹. DFT elastic tensors (AFM vs. non-magnetic) and COMSOL modelling are used to support the mode assignment and the sign of the shift.

Probing long-wavelength elastic renormalization in supported micron-scale vdW magnets is a worthwhile goal, and BMS is well suited to it. However:
- the headline anomaly currently rests on a sparse temperature series from what appears to be a single flake and a single wavevector, with no in-situ reference;
- the authors' own first-principles calculation yields an effect ten times smaller than measured.

So the claim of a "giant" magnetoelastic renormalization is not yet established.

### 2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- The experimental approach is appropriate and relatively rare: surface BMS on exfoliated flakes, with in-plane orientation selection and full room-temperature dispersion (Fig. 3a,b).
- The room-temperature mode assignment is supported by several lines of evidence:
  - bare-substrate spectra (Suppl. Fig. 5);
  - FEM displacement fields (Fig. 3c);
  - the slow-on-fast trend of decreasing phase velocity in both experiment and simulation (Fig. 3b,d).
- The internal arithmetic is consistent. The plotted q values match q = (4π/λ)sinθ for θ = 30–50°, and the velocities in Fig. 3b match 2πf/q.
- A full 13-constant monoclinic elastic tensor is given for two magnetic configurations (Table 1), together with a sensitivity analysis (Suppl. Table 2). These are useful to the community.
- If the anomaly is confirmed, a magnetically induced change of several percent in a long-wavelength acoustic velocity of a supported vdW antiferromagnet would be of broad interest for magnetoacoustic and straintronic concepts.

### 3. Concerns

#### A. Must address

**1. The magnitude and location of the anomaly rest on very limited, partly inconsistent data (Fig. 4).**

- *Sparse sampling.* Fig. 4b has seven points: 90, 120, 130, 150, 200, 250 and 300 K. There is no point between 150 and 200 K. The "5.5% drop" is defined from a single PM maximum at 200 K (1925 m s⁻¹) to the AFM values. The transition temperature and the shape of the anomaly (step, cusp, or broad crossover) are therefore unresolved.
- *Room-temperature mismatch.* The RT value in Fig. 4b at θ = 35° is ~1865 m s⁻¹ (f ≈ 4.0 GHz in Fig. 4a). Fig. 3b at the same q = 0.0135 nm⁻¹ gives ~1945 m s⁻¹ (f ≈ 4.2 GHz). This ~4% discrepancy under nominally identical conditions is comparable to the claimed magnetic effect. It is unexplained: different flake, cryostat window, angle calibration, or spot position?
- *Low-temperature spectra.* The 80–90 K spectra in Fig. 4a are very weak and broad (FWHM ~1 GHz) on a steep elastic background. The fitted shift (~0.2 GHz) is a fraction of the linewidth. The background model and the origin of the error bars are not described.
- *Missing point.* Fig. 4a shows an 80 K spectrum that does not appear in Fig. 4b.

**Requested:**
- dense temperature steps, at most 5 K, between ~120 and ~200 K;
- both cooling and warming sweeps;
- at least two additional flakes, ideally of different thickness, with flake-to-flake spread shown;
- an explanation of the Fig. 3/Fig. 4 RT discrepancy;
- a description of the fitting procedure (background, free parameters, Stokes and anti-Stokes averaging).

**2. No in-situ reference or null control rules out apparatus artifacts that can produce percent-level velocity changes.**

The velocity is computed as v = 2πf/q with q assumed fixed, so any change in incidence angle maps directly onto v. Several artifacts can each produce velocity changes of a few percent and are not excluded:

- *Angle drift.* Since Δq/q = cotθ·Δθ, a 1° change at θ = 35° gives ~2.5%. Thermal contraction of the cold finger or sample mount can easily tilt the sample by this much.
- *Adsorbate or ice loading.* Mass loading of the surface softens Rayleigh waves. The pronounced loss of scattered intensity on cooling (Fig. 4a) is consistent with deposition on the sample or window.
- *Laser heating.* NiPS3 absorbs at 532 nm. The BMS laser power and spot size are not given, whereas Raman used 0.024 mW. The effective sample temperature, and hence the apparent T_N, is unknown.

**Requested:**
- Track a SiO2/Si substrate mode (Rayleigh or Sezawa, from an adjacent bare region or simultaneously) at every temperature as an internal angle and temperature reference.
- Measure the temperature dependence at two or more incidence angles. Angle drift and a film-elastic change produce different q-dependences of Δv/v.
- Report a laser-power dependence at, for example, 100 K and 200 K.
- Ideally, measure an isostructural non-magnetic reference flake (ZnPS3 or CdPS3, or a non-magnetic vdW film of similar thickness and velocity) under matched conditions.

**3. The calculations do not quantitatively support the claim, and in one respect they contradict the interpretation (pp. 15–17, Table 1).**

- *Size of the computed effect.* The AFM-vs-NM FEM shift is 0.54%, ten times smaller than measured. Moreover, NM (zero local moment) vs. AFM should overestimate the elastic effect of ordering, because local moments persist in the paramagnet. The appeal to "finite-temperature effects" therefore does not explain the shortfall.
- *Absolute agreement.* The calculated RT frequency (4.6 GHz) differs from experiment (4.2 GHz) by ~10%. This exceeds the effect being explained, so "close agreement" is overstated.
- *Which constant changes.* The sensitivity analysis identifies c33 and c44 as dominant. A simple estimate of the sagittal shear velocity along b, √(c44/ρ) ≈ 1.75 km s⁻¹ for ρ ≈ 3.2 g cm⁻³, is close to the measured high-q asymptote. Yet DFT gives c44 identical in both phases (9.7 GPa). The experiment therefore implies a ~10% softening of the interlayer shear stiffness, or a large change in c33. Neither is reproduced by theory or obviously linked to intralayer zigzag exchange.

**Requested:**
- Quantitatively invert the data: state which reductions of c44 and/or c33 (with uncertainties) reproduce the 5.5% shift at q = 0.0135 nm⁻¹. Ideally constrain them from the full dispersion measured above and below T_N.
- Discuss explicitly why interlayer shear would be strongly renormalized.
- Temper "giant" and "reproduce … its magnetic-order–induced shift" (Abstract) unless the theory can be brought into agreement.

#### B. Should address

**4. Alternative, non-dynamical origins of the velocity change are not considered.**

- *Clamped magnetostriction.* NiPS3 shows spontaneous anisotropic magnetostriction at T_N. For a film clamped to SiO2, this strain cannot relax in-plane, so a built-in stress develops below T_N. Through third-order elastic constants and possible interfacial slip, this changes the acoustic velocity independently of the dynamic renormalization c_eff = c0 − λ²χ.
- *Thermal mismatch.* Differential thermal contraction between NiPS3 and Si/SiO2 adds a further temperature-dependent strain.
- *Interface compliance.* Changes in interface compliance would also enter.

**Requested:** order-of-magnitude estimates of these contributions, and a discussion of whether a free-standing or differently supported flake would distinguish them.

**5. The baseline and above-T_N physics are undefined.**

- The +3% velocity rise between 300 and 200 K is attributed to anharmonic stiffening. This is large for 100 K and is not fitted to any model, so the magnitude of the "anomaly" depends on an unstated baseline.
- NiPS3 is quasi-2D with strong short-range correlations well above T_N (broad susceptibility maximum near ~260 K). Part of the 300→200 K trend may itself be magnetic.

**Requested:**
- Fit a standard phonon-anharmonic (Varshni-type) background to a non-magnetic reference or to the high-T data.
- Quote the anomaly relative to that baseline.
- Discuss fluctuation contributions above T_N.

**6. The phenomenological model is misapplied and does not predict the magnitude (p. 16).**

- The expression c_eff = c0 − λ²χ_m with the uniform susceptibility is not the standard result for an antiferromagnet. The uniform χ does not diverge at T_N. A Landau coupling λεη² gives a step ΔC ∝ λ²/b below T_N, plus fluctuation corrections.
- The sentence "Consequently, the Rayleigh-wave velocity … decreases by 5.5%" is circular: 5.5% is the input, not an output.
- Δc/c ≈ 2Δv/v assumes the Rayleigh velocity scales with a single film modulus. That is not true in a heterostructure with substrate participation at qh ≈ 3.

**Requested:**
- a correct Landau or magnetoelastic treatment;
- a microscopic estimate of the coupling, for example from strain derivatives of E_AFM − E_FM in DFT, or from measured NiPS3 magnetostriction;
- a demonstration that the predicted step is compatible with ~10% in c44 or c33.

**7. Crystallographic orientation and magnetic domains are not controlled.**

- Cleavage edges at 60° in the pseudo-hexagonal lattice do not uniquely distinguish a from the [110]-type directions.
- Zigzag order can form three domain orientations, and the BMS spot averages over several microns. The DFT AFM tensor assumes a single domain with chains along a.

**Requested:**
- independent determination of the axes and of the Néel-vector or domain orientation on the measured flake (polarization-resolved Raman of magnon or exciton features, or linear dichroism);
- the temperature dependence for q along a as well as b, which would directly test the anisotropic renormalization implied by Table 1.

**8. T_N of the measured flake is assumed rather than measured.**

The bulk value of ~155 K is used, but the 150 K point is labelled as the drop. Please determine T_N on the same flake, under the BMS illumination conditions, via:
- the Raman FWHM kink;
- magnon or exciton signatures; or
- a critical attenuation peak in the BMS linewidth itself.

The BMS linewidth and intensity versus temperature should be reported in any case. A damping anomaly at T_N would be strong corroborating evidence.

**9. Mode assignment and scattering cross-section need firmer support.**

- At qh ≈ 2.6–4 a slow-on-fast film should support Sezawa modes, and the FEM finds several branches. Why are they absent from the spectra?
- The statement of Loudon's rule ("displacement components perpendicular to the phonon propagation direction") is inaccurate. The ripple mechanism requires surface-normal displacement, and shear-horizontal modes are also perpendicular but do not ripple. For an absorbing film the elasto-optic contribution may also matter.

**Requested:** a computed surface-BMS cross-section (ripple plus elasto-optic) for the FEM branches.

**10. Computational details are insufficient or inconsistent (Methods).**

- No Hubbard U or spin–orbit coupling is mentioned. NiPS3 normally requires DFT+U for a realistic gap and moment, and the NM state is likely metallic. A disordered-local-moment or SQS paramagnet, or at least an FM comparison, would be a better PM proxy than non-spin-polarized Ni²⁺.
- The cell dimensions are inconsistent with the stated supercell. A "1×4×2 supercell" of 11.499 × 19.914 × 12.308 Å does not match the conventional C2/m cell (a ≈ 5.8, b ≈ 10.1, c ≈ 6.6 Å). Please specify the cell, the spin arrangement, and whether the relaxed AFM structure was symmetrized.
- Please compare the relaxed lattice parameters with experiment.
- Please justify convergence of the soft constants (c44 ≈ 10 GPa) with respect to strain amplitude (0.015), k-mesh (3×2×3) and smearing.
- Please compare with published NiPS3 elastic constants.
- For the FEM, give:
  - the NiPS3 density;
  - the SiO2 and Si constants;
  - the Si orientation and propagation direction;
  - how the monoclinic tensor was rotated into a frame with z normal to the layers (β ≈ 107°, which matters for c15, c25, c35, c46);
  - convergence with Si depth, given the fixed bottom boundary.

**11. Novelty and literature context.**

- Mechanical signatures of T_N in MPS3 have been reported before. Nanomechanical resonators of FePS3, MnPS3 and NiPS3 (Šiškins et al., Nat. Commun. 2020), and anisotropic magnetostriction in FePS3/NiPS3 membranes (Houmes et al., Nat. Commun. 2023), should be cited and discussed.
- Ultrasonic elastic anomalies of several percent at T_N are well known in conventional antiferromagnets. "Giant" and "rarely reported" need quantitative comparison.
- Please compare the RT velocities with the authors' own earlier MPS3 BMS data (Ref. 36).
- Ref. 49 concerns optical spin–phonon constants. Its relevance to elastic renormalization should be stated more carefully.

**12. The Raman contrast is framed loosely (pp. 7–8, Fig. 2).**

- The claim that optical phonons are insensitive is central to the narrative. However, the 255 cm⁻¹ FWHM in Fig. 2d shows a visible change near T_N.
- The anharmonic model, its fit range (it should exclude T < T_N), and the free parameters are not given.
- The absence of the reported ~176 cm⁻¹ splitting may reflect domain averaging or thickness, linking back to concern 7, rather than weak coupling.
- Comparing an absolute shift of ~0.1 cm⁻¹ with a fractional acoustic change of 5% needs care. Please compare fractional shifts and coupling constants on a common footing.

### 4. Suggestions for improvement

1. **Dispersion above and below T_N.** Measure the full dispersion (θ = 25–55°) at ~200 K and ~80 K, along both b and a. Invert for c44, c33 (and c22, c23) with uncertainties. This turns a single-point anomaly into a determination of which elastic constants change, and inherently tests for angle drift.
2. **Built-in reference plus controls.** Track a substrate SAW mode at every temperature, and add a non-magnetic isostructural reference flake and a thickness series. The magnetic contribution should scale with film confinement, which differs with qh. Add laser-power checks.
3. **Use linewidth and dense temperature data.** Analyse the BMS linewidth and intensity near T_N in dense temperature steps within a Landau or magnetoelastic framework. This gives an independent T_N and a second magnetic signature.
4. **Strengthen the theory.** Use DFT+U(+SOC), a better paramagnetic reference, and explicit strain derivatives of the exchange energies to estimate the magnetoelastic coefficients. Confront them with measured NiPS3 magnetostriction and with the inverted Δc44 and Δc33.
