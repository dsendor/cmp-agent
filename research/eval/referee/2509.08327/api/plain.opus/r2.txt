# Review: "Trans-scale spin Seebeck effect in nanostructured bulk composites based on magnetic insulator"

## Summary

The authors coat crushed single-crystal YIG powders with Pt (nominally 5, 15 and 30 nm) by dynamic powder sputtering. They then consolidate the powders into bulk pellets by pressing at 300 °C or room temperature under 350–500 MPa. The pellets have relative densities of 67–75% and percolating Pt networks. The effective electrical conductivity σ_eff is 5.6×10²–5.0×10³ S/m, and the thermal conductivity κ is 1.46–1.87 W/mK. A field-odd transverse thermoelectric signal appears in two orthogonal magnetothermal geometries (H_z–∇_xT and H_x–∇_zT), with S_SSE ≈ 7.5–17.3 nV/K. The authors attribute it to the SSE and argue that it is free of ANE contamination, citing XRD, magnetization and Hall data. The headline claims are:

1. Scalable fabrication of an FMI/NM bulk composite.
2. A 2–3 order-of-magnitude increase in σ_eff over conventional YIG/Pt devices.
3. Observation of an "isotropic", bulk-scale SSE.

The topic is timely. Extending SSE from bilayers to volumetric FMI/NM architectures is a sensible direction, and the powder-sputtering and low-temperature consolidation route is a useful processing contribution. The Pt-mediated cold consolidation of an oxide, and the observation that 800 °C sintering destroys conduction, are practically valuable. However, in its current form the manuscript does not demonstrate the central premise: that trans-scaling improves SSE device performance. Several key controls needed to attribute the signal unambiguously to the SSE are also missing. There are also internal inconsistencies, notably in the magnetization data.

**Recommendation: Major revision.**

---

## Major issues

### 1. The performance advantage of "trans-scaling" is not established; σ_eff alone is the wrong metric

The paper's motivation is that bilayers are limited in output power. The quantitative evidence offered, however, is almost entirely σ_eff, and σ_eff can be raised trivially by adding more metal. Raising σ_eff is not the goal; raising output power per volume is. In a bilayer with t_Pt ≫ λ_s, σ_eff ∝ t_Pt while E_ISHE ∝ 1/t_Pt. Adding Pt therefore increases σ_eff but not the power factor.

The relevant comparison is PF_eff = σ_eff·S² (or power density at fixed ∇T) against conventional devices, evaluated with the same σ_eff definition.

- Room-temperature longitudinal SSE coefficients E/∇T for bulk YIG/Pt (~10 nm) are typically of order 10⁻⁷ V/K. That is roughly an order of magnitude larger than the 7.5–17 nV/K reported here.
- Using the authors' own σ_eff = 53 S/m for single-crystal YIG/Pt (Table S1) and S ~ 0.1 μV/K gives PF_eff ~ 5×10⁻¹³ W/mK². This is comparable to the best composite (30-RT, 5.1×10⁻¹³ W/mK²). A larger S would make the bilayer better.

The ~10× loss in S thus roughly cancels the ~10²–10³ gain in σ_eff. The authors should:

- add S_SSE and PF_eff for the reference systems to Table S1;
- ideally measure a reference YIG-slab/Pt bilayer in the same setup with the same ∇T determination, both as a calibration and as a fair comparison;
- report zT. From the authors' own numbers, zT = PF·T/κ ≈ 5×10⁻¹³ × 300 / 1.87 ≈ 8×10⁻¹¹. This should be stated so readers can judge the "scalable platform for bulk SSE-based thermoelectrics" claim in the abstract.

Even setting PF aside, the "2–3 orders of magnitude" figure is generous. The lowest composite value (5.6×10² S/m) is only about 10× the highest literature value in Table S1 (53 S/m), so the range is ~1–3 orders. The literature σ_eff values also depend directly on the arbitrarily chosen substrate/slab thickness (Eq. S3). A thinner YIG slab raises σ_eff without any architectural change, which weakens σ_eff as a figure of merit.

### 2. Missing controls needed to attribute the signal to the SSE

The exclusion of parasitic effects rests on XRD, magnetization and a linear Hall signal. These are indirect and, in places, weak (see points 3 and 4). The standard and much more decisive tests for an SSE origin are:

- **Spin-Hall-angle control:** composites made with an NM of negligible spin Hall angle (Cu, Au), and with one of opposite sign (W, Ta). Sign reversal with W/Ta would be strong evidence for ISHE detection.
- **Non-magnetic matrix control:** Pt-coated non-magnetic garnet powders (e.g., GGG or YAG) processed identically. This excludes Pt-intrinsic, processing-induced or field-dependent thermal artifacts.
- **Uncoated YIG control:** a YIG-only pellet. The authors report that one was mechanically poor, but even a fragile pellet, or YIG mixed with an inert binder, would help.
- **Field dependence vs. magnetization:** overlay normalized E(H) on normalized M(H), measured in the same geometry with demagnetization accounted for, for both configurations. Close agreement would considerably strengthen the assignment.

Given the very small signal (nV/K), a composite with many interfaces and grain boundaries, and the possibility of intermixing at the sputtered interface, these controls are needed rather than optional.

### 3. Magnetization data: units and the argument built on them appear inconsistent

- Fig. 3f and Fig. S6 plot M in "emu/g" with saturation values of ~0.02–0.027. The text quotes 37.9 emu/g for YIG. The y-axis values are about three orders of magnitude too small for mass magnetization. It is likely the raw moment (emu) is plotted.
- If the data are not mass-normalized, the key argument that "saturation magnetization decreases upon Pt deposition, so no FMM phase forms" is not valid as stated.
- 37.9 emu/g corresponds to YIG near 0 K. The room-temperature value is ~27 emu/g, which is the relevant comparison for 300 K data.
- If the data are normalized, the composites show a ~25% reduction relative to the single crystal, and this reduction is nearly the same for 15 and 30 nm Pt.
  - A simple estimate is much smaller: for ~10 μm particles, Pt volume fraction ≈ 6t/d gives roughly 4–7 wt% Pt.
  - A thickness-independent ~25% drop instead suggests degraded YIG, such as magnetically dead or damaged surface layers from crushing or high-pressure pressing.
  - Such degradation is directly relevant to the interfacial spin-mixing conductance and to the SSE magnitude.
- Please clarify the normalization, report Pt mass fractions (e.g., from ICP or weight gain), and discuss the origin of the reduction.

A uniform decrease in M also cannot exclude a minor FMM phase at the ~1 wt% level, because Pt dilution would mask it.

### 4. XRD cannot rule out Fe₃O₄ at relevant levels

- The strongest Fe₃O₄ reflection, (311) at ~35.4° (Cu Kα), overlaps almost exactly with YIG (422) at ~35.5°. Several other Fe₃O₄ lines also lie close to YIG reflections.
- Laboratory powder XRD detection limits are typically ~1–5 wt%.
- The statement that α-Fe₂O₃/YFeO₃ formation "could not be distinguished in the XRD data" means these phases are not excluded either.

The ANE exclusion should not rest primarily on XRD. More convincing evidence would be:

- high-resolution STEM-EDS/EELS line profiles across Pt/YIG interfaces in the *sintered* samples, especially the 300 °C ones, to check Fe–Pt intermixing; and
- the controls in point 2.

### 5. No physical model for the composite SSE

The paper would be much stronger with even a simple effective-medium or circuit model linking bilayer parameters (spin-mixing conductance, θ_SH, λ_s, λ_m), grain size d and Pt fraction to S_eff and PF_eff. For example:

- For a Pt-coated grain, the local ISHE field ∝ (∇T·n̂) n̂ × M. Its orientational average is non-zero and isotropic (∝ ∇T × M/3). This supports the isotropy claim and gives a geometric prefactor.
- The volumetric advantage should then scale roughly as interface area per volume (∝ 1/d). The signal per grain is in turn limited when d ≲ λ_m. Both effects depend on the particle size distribution, which is not reported, although the SEM images show sizes from sub-μm to >10 μm.

Such a model would allow the authors to:

- judge whether 10–17 nV/K is consistent with the expected magnitude, which bears on point 2;
- identify which parameter limits performance; and
- give concrete design rules, replacing the generic outlook in the Discussion.

### 6. Isotropy claim and measurement geometry

The composite is isotropic by construction, apart from possible texture from uniaxial pressing. Contrasting it with a rotated thin film is therefore not very informative. More importantly:

- The pressing axis relative to x, y and z, and the sample dimensions and electrode spacing, are not given. Uniaxially pressed powders often have flattened particles and Pt channels aligned perpendicular to the pressing axis. σ_eff and κ may then be anisotropic, and σ_eff should be measured along the voltage direction used in each configuration.
- The two configurations use very different thermal setups: sapphire plates with grease versus Cu blocks with adhesive thermal tape. Both estimate ∇T from a ΔT that includes interfacial drops, with different and unquantified interfacial resistances. The 14.8 vs 14.4 nV/K agreement may be partly fortuitous.
- ∇T should be measured on the sample itself, for example with thermocouples on the sample or lock-in/IR thermography, which this group is well placed to do. An uncertainty budget should be given.

### 7. Statistics and reproducibility

- Only one sample per condition is reported, all from a single batch.
- The trends are presented as physically meaningful but are not explained or tested for reproducibility:
  - RT pressing gives ~2× higher S than 300 °C.
  - 15 nm Pt gives slightly higher S than 30 nm.
  - σ_eff is superlinear in Pt thickness: ×6 for 300 °C, ×4 for RT.
- The thickness dependence is called "qualitatively consistent" with bilayer behavior. Yet bilayer shunting for t ≫ λ_s would predict roughly a factor of 2 between 15 and 30 nm, against the observed ~1.2.
- The 15 and 30 nm thicknesses are nominal, inferred from deposition time and the 5 nm calibration. They should be verified by TEM or by mass gain.

The authors should add replicate samples, uncertainties for σ_eff, κ and S, and a discussion of why 300 °C processing reduces S. Possible causes include Pt agglomeration, interfacial reaction, or oxidation in air.

### 8. Thermal conductivity interpretation

Attributing the low κ to "phonon scattering at the YIG/Pt interface" is not supported:

- Cold-pressed powders at 25–33% porosity with point-like inter-particle contacts typically show κ reductions far greater than linear in density. Contact thermal resistance is the likely dominant factor.
- Pt, which has high κ, would if anything raise κ.
- An uncoated-YIG reference pressed the same way, or an effective-medium estimate for the measured porosity, is needed before invoking interfacial phonon scattering.

The relative densities are computed against YIG alone (5.11 g/cm³). Including the Pt mass would raise the theoretical density and lower the reported relative densities.

## Minor issues

- p. 7: "consistent with that obtained in the H_x–∇_zT configuration (Figs. 3c and S5)" should read H_z–∇_xT.
- p. 6: "symmetrized with respect to H to remove field-even components" should read *antisymmetrized*. The same applies to the Hall data in Methods.
- SI Note S2 repeatedly refers to "Fig. S2" for XRD; it should be Fig. S3.
- The Cu Kα wavelength is given as 0.15406 nm in Methods and 0.15418 nm in SI.
- Fig. S3 assigns the same card number (PDF#43-0661) to both Y₂O₃ and FePt.
- Methods state SQUID (MPMS); the author contributions mention VSM.
- p. 4/5: thicknesses "well above λ_s … ensuring clear observation of the SSE without discontinuities" conflates two issues. Continuity is a growth question; t ≫ λ_s actually reduces E_ISHE through shunting.
- "Bad metal regime" has a specific meaning tied to the Mott–Ioffe–Regel limit in intrinsic conductors and is inappropriate for a percolating composite. Comparisons with undoped Si/Ge are not meaningful; comparisons with practical thermoelectrics (~10⁴–10⁵ S/m) or transverse thermoelectric bulk materials would be more informative.
- Table S1: the "This work" rows should be labeled by sample, and S_SSE/PF columns added (see point 1).
- Fig. S4 caption: dashed reference lines also appear in panels (d) and (e), not only (b)–(c).
- Fig. 3 caption: the sentence on subscripts x, y, z mapping to ∇T, I and H is confusing; please rephrase.
- Fig. 2c: the Pt EDS map appears concentrated in regions of fine particles, with large Pt-free areas over large grains. Please comment on the homogeneity of the Pt network and give a particle size distribution.
- Interface preparation: there is no surface cleaning of the crushed powders before sputtering, and pressing is done in air. Interfacial quality, the main determinant of g↑↓, is not characterized beyond one TEM image of the 5 nm sample. TEM of the actual 15/30 nm sintered samples would be valuable.
- p. 8: the paragraph on α-Fe₂O₃/YFeO₃ is logically awkward ("could not be distinguished … suggest that they are inert") and grammatically incomplete ("known to strongly anisotropic"). Note also that α-Fe₂O₃ is weakly ferromagnetic at room temperature.
- Prior art: the context of scalable SSE should cite spin-Seebeck coatings (Kirihara et al., Nat. Mater. 2012) and FM-oxide/metal multilayers (Ramos et al., PRB 2015). The novelty relative to Boona et al. (Nat. Commun. 2016) should be stated more precisely: an insulating FM and the absence of ANE.
- Typos and names: "It is worth nothing" → "noting"; "Galosov" → "Golosov"; check the author name order ("Hirata Keisuke").
- The "630% higher" PF should be stated as "~7× higher", which is clearer.

## Questions for the authors

1. What is the particle size distribution of the crushed YIG, and how does S_SSE compare with the expected dependence on grain size relative to λ_m?
2. What are the pressing axis and sample dimensions in each configuration? Is σ_eff anisotropic?
3. Is the sign of S_SSE consistent with a reference Pt/YIG bilayer measured in the same setup?
4. Why does 300 °C pressing reduce S_SSE by ~2× relative to RT? Is there evidence of interfacial change?
5. Are the Fig. 3f/S6 data in emu or emu/g? What is the measured Pt mass fraction?
6. What is the measured power density (or maximum power into a matched load) per unit volume and ΔT, compared with a bilayer of the same footprint?

---

## Overall assessment

This is an interesting processing demonstration: it is genuinely useful to show that Pt-coated YIG powders can be cold-consolidated into conductive, mechanically intact bulk pellets that show a field-odd transverse thermoelectric response. The manuscript, however, overstates what has been shown.

- **SSE origin:** plausible, but not yet nailed down. Controls with different spin Hall angles and with a non-magnetic matrix are missing.
- **Performance advantage:** not demonstrated, because the σ_eff gain is offset by a much smaller S and PF_eff is not compared.
- **Supporting evidence:** the magnetization data, a key part of the ANE exclusion, appear to have a units/normalization problem.

With the controls, a fair PF/power comparison, a simple model and the corrections above, the work could make a solid contribution.
