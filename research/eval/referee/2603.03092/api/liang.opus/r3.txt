# Review outline

## 1. Significance and novelty

- **Problem addressed.** The paper attempts the first momentum-resolved, surface-sensitive (ARPES) study of a cuprate side surface in the superconducting state. The target is the (110) face of overdoped La₂₋ₓSrₓCuO₄ (x = 0.22).
  - This face is predicted to host topologically protected zero-energy Andreev flat bands. These arise from the chiral winding number of the d-wave BdG Hamiltonian.
  - It is also predicted to show local suppression of the d-wave order parameter.
  - Until now, evidence for these states has come almost entirely from tunneling. Tunneling lacks momentum resolution, and its zero-bias conductance peaks have notoriously ambiguous interpretations.
- **Technical novelty.** This is the first application to a high-Tc cuprate of a recently introduced FIB "micro-notch" controlled-cleave technique (Hunter et al., 2024; previously used on normal-state Sr₂RuO₄).
  - The technique yields side surfaces with AFM rms roughness of about 1.8 Å.
  - It allows photon-energy and deflector mapping that recovers the known LSCO Fermi surface. This is a genuine enabling step for spectroscopy of layered quantum materials that do not cleave perpendicular to the planes.
- **Physics claims.** There are three:
  - (i) No superconducting gap is resolved on the (110) surface within 3–4 meV.
  - (ii) No flat-band peak or associated dip is observed.
  - (iii) Self-consistent BdG simulations argue that the AFM-measured geometric roughness cannot explain (ii), whereas Gaussian onsite disorder of about 100 meV can.
- **Assessment of significance.**
  - The methodological advance is of clear interest.
  - The physics content is essentially a null result plus a model-dependent interpretation.
  - The impact for a top-tier journal therefore hinges on whether the null result is shown to be intrinsic rather than extrinsic, and on whether the disorder explanation is quantitatively constrained.

## 2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- **Enabling technique on a hard problem.** Clean, oriented side surfaces of a cuprate are obtained and verified in several ways:
  - SEM and AFM characterization;
  - recovery of the (001) Fermi-surface cross-section via hν scans;
  - plausible nodal and antinodal dispersions.
  
  This opens a route to momentum-resolved studies of Andreev surface states, phase crystals, time-reversal-symmetry-breaking surface phases, and similar physics in many layered materials.
- **Careful conceptual framework.** Several points are well articulated:
  - the topological and trivial momentum sectors on the [11] edge (Fig. 1);
  - the non-trivial k⊥ (k_z) structure of weakly confined surface states when Δ ≪ t (Supp. VI B);
  - the observation that the surface-state signature survives order-parameter suppression (Supp. Fig. 13).
  
  Identifying an observable "dip–peak" signature at the antinodal k_z is a useful guide for future experiments.
- **Realistic, ARPES-oriented simulations.** The modeling includes:
  - real-space self-consistent BdG on a large slab;
  - experimentally derived edge roughness;
  - an inelastic-mean-free-path weighting with explicit k_z broadening.
  
  This goes beyond typical clean-limit predictions and directly targets what ARPES would see.
- **Bulk superconductivity is checked.** SQUID measurements before and after milling show unchanged Tc. A (001) reference on a comparably prepared sample is provided, along with a second sample and temperature cycling. These controls show awareness of extrinsic pitfalls.
- **Community value of a negative result.** If made robust, this result is valuable:
  - It sets expectations for future side-surface ARPES.
  - It argues that bulk disorder, not geometric roughness, is the limiting factor.
  - It points to cleaner cuprates as targets.

## 3. Potential reasons for rejection

### 3.1 The null result is not demonstrated to be intrinsic; an extrinsic, non-superconducting or degraded surface layer is not excluded

- **No positive evidence of superconductivity at the measured surface.** The measured EDCs at 6, 21 and 35 K (Fig. 4g; Supp. Fig. 11, up to 55 K) are essentially temperature-independent across Tc (about 27 K for x = 0.22).
  - No gap, coherence peak, or leading-edge shift is seen even at the nodal-to-antinodal positions where the simulations predict observable structure.
  - The simplest reading is a surface layer that is non-superconducting or strongly pair-broken over the full ARPES probing depth. Possible causes include Ga implantation or amorphization near the notch, oxygen loss, local overdoping, or cleave-induced strain.
  - Bulk SQUID data cannot address this, since the probed volume is dominated by unmilled material. The authors' own choice to measure "a thin slab at the top" still contains mostly bulk.
- **The doping estimate does not rule out a non-superconducting surface.** The surface doping estimate (p ≈ 0.227) comes from fits the authors themselves describe as distorted and imperfect, with values up to p ≈ 0.3 for alternative fits. This upper range lies beyond the superconducting dome of LSCO (about 0.27).
  - Moreover, the tight-binding chemical potential was tuned to match the nodal k_F, which the authors note is weakly doping-dependent.
  - The claim that the surface is "unlikely to undergo a reconstruction" and has bulk-like doping is therefore not secure enough to anchor the interpretation.
- **"Pristine" surface is asserted rather than established.**
  - AFM provides only rms height (1.8 Å, about half a lattice constant) with few-nm lateral resolution. No lateral correlation length, step density, or facet/miscut analysis is given.
  - There is no in situ LEED, core-level XPS, cross-sectional TEM of the cleave, or comparison of nodal quasiparticle linewidths with (001) cleaves.
  - The miscut tolerance of the Laue-based alignment is not stated. A vicinal surface would contain (100)/(010) micro-facets that carry no flat band.
  - It is unclear whether AFM was performed on the same cleave as the ARPES data, or under what conditions after removal from UHV.

### 3.2 The expected signal magnitude is overestimated relative to the experimental conditions, weakening the "surprising" absence

- **Inconsistent gap scales.** The simulations are tuned to an antinodal gap of about 24 meV, and the text expects "approximately 20 meV". However, the authors' own (001) reference on a comparably prepared x = 0.19 sample yields only about 7 meV.
  - At x = 0.22 the gap is expected to be even smaller.
  - With a gap of roughly 5–7 meV, the predicted dip–peak structure would be compressed to a scale comparable to:
    - the 3–4 meV resolution;
    - thermal broadening at 6 K;
    - the strong intrinsic antinodal lifetime broadening of overdoped LSCO (tens of meV near the van Hove singularity, close to the Lifshitz transition at this doping).
  - The simulations include only 3 meV of broadening and no self-energy. The "surprise" in the abstract is therefore not quantitatively justified.
- **No quantitative upper bound is extracted from the data.** Fig. 4g compares background-subtracted, total-intensity-normalized EDCs with gold by eye.
  - The Shirley/Matt-type background subtraction and k-integration over finite sectors could themselves wash out a weak, narrow peak.
  - There is no Fermi-function division, no leading-edge-midpoint analysis versus temperature (as was done carefully for the (001) reference), and no fit establishing the maximal flat-band spectral weight or width compatible with the data.
  - Without such a bound, the conclusion that the flat band is "suppressed beyond detectability" cannot be evaluated against the theory.
- **Inconsistent k_z broadening and kinematics.**
  - The Fig. 3 caption states Δk = 0.2 Å⁻¹ (mean free path about 5 Å), whereas the text and Methods give about 0.4 Å⁻¹ (FWHM = 2/λ). The authors also state that the simulation overestimates k_z broadening.
  - The selection of hν = 95 eV for the antinodal k_z relies on a free-electron final state with an unstated inner potential, while Supp. F reports noticeable Fermi-surface distortions.
  - Matrix-element and polarization effects in this unusual geometry are not discussed. Each of these could reduce the visibility of the targeted feature.

### 3.3 The disorder explanation is under-constrained and partly inconsistent

- **One disorder strength, not independently justified.** A single Gaussian onsite disorder strength (σ = 100 meV on every site, comparable to roughly half the nearest-neighbor hopping) is shown, apparently for a single realization.
  - There is no scan in σ, no disorder averaging, and no criterion for when the flat band becomes undetectable at the experimental resolution.
  - This disorder is not linked to independently measurable quantities of x = 0.22 LSCO, such as the residual resistivity, the normal-state scattering rate, or the ARPES nodal linewidth on (001).
  - Such strong dense potential disorder would normally be strongly pair-breaking for a d-wave superconductor (Abrikosov–Gor'kov-type Tc suppression).
  - The authors compensate by increasing the pairing interaction V from 0.153 to 0.21555. The units are given as "meV", which appears to be erroneous. This retuning makes the model internally consistent with a bulk gap only by construction.
- **Claimed agreement is qualitative.** The "visual agreement" in Fig. 4d/f is weak evidence, given that the experimental spectra are largely featureless.
  - Many mechanisms would produce featureless spectra: a non-superconducting surface, lifetime broadening, a small gap, or displacement of the states away from the topmost layer (which the authors themselves invoke).
  - The paper does not identify a discriminating prediction of the disorder scenario, such as the predicted increase of trivial-sector weight near E_F, and test it.
- **Terminology and relation to prior theory need care.**
  - The disorder Vᵢ = vᵢσ_z in the Nambu basis is an ordinary non-magnetic potential (σ_x in the chiral basis). Calling it a "mass term" is confusing.
  - The mechanism is well known: chiral-symmetric disorder couples the opposite-chirality flat-band sectors on the [11] edge, whose net index vanishes once k∥ is no longer conserved. This should be connected to the extensive quasiclassical and microscopic literature on rough/diffuse surfaces and impurity broadening of zero-bias conductance peaks (e.g., Barash, Kalenkov, Fogelström, Tanaka, Asano, Golubov, and others).
  - The novelty and robustness of the theoretical conclusion should be stated relative to that literature.
- **Geometric roughness modeling is limited.**
  - Edge sites are removed according to an AFM height histogram, at a discretization of a/√2.
  - The lateral correlation of the roughness is unknown, since it lies below AFM resolution. The authors concede that sub-nm lateral roughness could destroy the states.
  - The statement that "roughness is too weak" is therefore only a statement about roughness at scales the AFM can resolve. This caveat must appear prominently in the abstract and conclusions, not only in the body.

### 3.4 Overstated framing relative to the evidence, and incomplete reporting

- **Title and abstract overreach.**
  - "Suppression of Spectral Gap and Flat Bands" implies that an intrinsic suppression was demonstrated; what is shown is non-observation.
  - "Reveal disorder as the key factor" is a model inference not discriminated from alternatives.
  - "Pristine" surfaces is asserted from rms roughness alone.
  
  For a top-tier venue, the central physical conclusion needs either a positive observation (e.g., a gap on a control surface of the same cleave type, or any surface-state signature) or a much tighter exclusion.
- **Reproducibility and statistics are thin.**
  - The number of cleaves, success rate, and number of samples are unclear.
  - The temperature-dependent intensity changes in the main sample were not reproduced in the second sample and are dismissed as extrinsic.
  - No control measurement is shown on a side surface where no flat band is expected, such as (100). Such a control would separate topological effects from generic side-surface degradation.
- **Internal inconsistencies and missing elements undermine confidence.** Examples include:
  - Δk 0.2 versus 0.4 Å⁻¹;
  - V given in "meV";
  - the Fig. 4b axis labelled r in Å⁻¹;
  - "2 amt";
  - mixed use of x and p;
  - TPP-2M evaluated with a "band gap" equal to the superconducting gap, which is not the intended input of that formula;
  - an empty Data Availability section;
  - incomplete figure labelling of integration ranges.

## 4. Suggestions for improvement

1. **Establish the superconducting and structural integrity of the measured surface, ideally with a discriminating control.**
   - Provide in situ LEED and core-level spectra.
   - Provide cross-sectional TEM or EDX near the cleave and notch to quantify FIB damage and Ga content.
   - Report the miscut angle and the AFM lateral correlation length on the same cleave.
   - Measure, under identical preparation:
     - a (100) side surface, where no flat band but a gap is expected;
     - an (001) cleave of the same x = 0.22 crystal.
   - Most importantly, search on the (110) surface for any temperature-dependent signature of superconductivity. Possibilities include a leading-edge shift relative to gold across Tc, or changes in off-nodal spectra or the nodal kink. Use the same careful leading-edge-midpoint, space-charge and flux-extrapolation protocol applied in Supp. E.

2. **Turn the null result into a quantitative bound.**
   - Fit the k-resolved EDCs with a model containing a Fermi–Dirac function, Gaussian resolution, a realistic lifetime-broadened bulk spectral function, and an optional zero-energy surface peak. Extract upper limits on its weight and width.
   - Show results with and without background subtraction.
   - Redo the simulations with:
     - the experimentally appropriate gap (about 5–7 meV, consistent with the authors' (001) reference and doping);
     - realistic antinodal self-energy broadening;
     - a single, consistently justified k_z broadening.
   
   This will show whether the clean-limit signature would actually be resolvable under the experimental conditions.

3. **Make the disorder analysis systematic and falsifiable.**
   - Scan disorder strength and type: dense Gaussian, dilute strong (unitary) scatterers, smooth out-of-plane dopant potentials, hopping disorder, and surface-localized versus bulk disorder.
   - Average over realizations and report the flat-band bandwidth and peak visibility versus σ at the experimental resolution.
   - Map σ onto measurable quantities such as the normal-state scattering rate, residual resistivity, and Tc suppression without retuning V. Compare these with known values for x ≈ 0.22 LSCO and with the authors' own (001) nodal linewidths.
   - Identify a prediction specific to the disorder scenario, such as the enhanced trivial-sector weight or particular k-space redistribution, and test it in the data.
   - Situate the result within existing theory of surface roughness and impurity broadening of Andreev bound states.

4. **Reframe and tighten the manuscript, and outline a path to a positive observation.**
   - Revise the title and abstract to reflect non-observation and model-based interpretation, for example "absence of resolvable gap and flat-band signatures".
   - State the AFM lateral-resolution caveat explicitly in the abstract.
   - Report sample, cleave and reproducibility statistics.
   - Correct the listed inconsistencies and complete the Data Availability statement.
   - Strengthen the outlook with concrete proposals that would make the technique decisive:
     - cleaner or stoichiometric cuprates (e.g., detwinned YBCO, Tl2201, Hg1201);
     - laser-ARPES or higher resolution;
     - photon energies giving larger probing depth (soft X-ray) to test the proposed displacement of surface states below the topmost layer;
     - nano-ARPES to address the spatial-averaging argument made in the Discussion.
