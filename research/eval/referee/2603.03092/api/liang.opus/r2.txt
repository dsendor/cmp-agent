Review outline:

**1. Significance and novelty**

- **Claimed advance.**
  - The manuscript reports the first momentum-resolved ARPES study of a cuprate side surface in the superconducting state: (110)-oriented overdoped La₂₋ₓSrₓCuO₄ with x = 0.22.
  - Access to this surface is enabled by FIB-defined micro-notch cleaving, a technique previously demonstrated only on the normal state of Sr₂RuO₄.
  - This is a genuine technical milestone. (110) surfaces of d-wave superconductors are the textbook host of zero-energy Andreev/flat-band states, and the large literature on zero-bias conductance peaks (ZBCPs) has long lacked momentum resolution.
- **Scientific outcome.**
  - The main result is a null observation. Within ~3–4 meV resolution, no spectral gap and no zero-energy flat-band peak are seen at kz values corresponding to the antinodes.
  - The interpretation rests on self-consistent BdG slab simulations. These show that:
    - the AFM-measured geometric roughness (1.8 Å RMS) is insufficient to destroy the flat band;
    - Gaussian on-site disorder with a standard deviation of 100 meV can destroy it.
- **Conceptual contributions.**
  - **kz-mixing of edge states.** The paper explains why small-gap edge states acquire bulk-like kz dependence and why antinodal kz cuts are optimal for detection. This is pedagogically useful for future side-surface ARPES.
  - **Chirality-sector argument.** The paper argues that inter-chirality scattering requires large momentum transfer, which makes the flat band fragile to short-range disorder.
  - **Prior theory.** The disorder sensitivity of (110) Andreev bound states is already established in the quasiclassical and BdG literature (e.g., Kalenkov–Fogelström–Barash; Poenicke et al.; Tanaka–Kashiwaya; Asano). The theoretical novelty is therefore incremental.
- **Overall assessment.**
  - The methodological novelty is high.
  - The physics conclusion ("disorder is the key factor preventing flat bands") is currently a plausible hypothesis rather than a demonstrated result. Whether the work meets a top-tier bar depends on whether trivial explanations for the null result can be excluded.

**2. Potential reasons for acceptance**

- **Technique.**
  - This is the first successful application of FIB-controlled cleaving to a high-Tc cuprate.
  - The paper provides evidence that bulk superconductivity is unaffected by the preparation (SQUID on the milled region).
  - The cleaved surface has low AFM roughness.
  - This opens a broadly applicable route to non-natural cleavage planes of layered quantum materials.
- **Data quality.**
  - The full 3D Fermi surface is mapped from a side surface, including photon-energy maps that reproduce the (001) Fermi surface.
  - Nodal and antinodal cuts are obtained.
  - A surface doping estimate (p ≈ 0.22–0.25) is given, compatible with the bulk, which argues against gross surface reconstruction.
- **Theory–experiment integration.**
  - The modelling is fully momentum-resolved and self-consistent. It includes the ARPES probing-depth weighting, kz broadening, and an AFM-derived edge profile.
  - This goes beyond the usual qualitative comparisons and gives concrete, testable predictions: the surface-state peak, the DOS dip, and the coherence-peak recovery under roughness.
- **Relevance to a long-standing controversy.**
  - The paper offers a coherent framework that reconciles three observations:
    - spatially inhomogeneous ZBCPs in STM;
    - ZBCPs in tunnel junctions;
    - the absence of a flat-band signature in area-averaged ARPES.
  - It gives actionable guidance to the community: minimise bulk disorder, and consider near-stoichiometric compounds.
- **Analytical supplement.**
  - The analytical derivation of edge-state wavefunctions and their kz-limit behaviour is a useful reference.

**3. Potential reasons for rejection**

- **(i) The null result is not disentangled from trivial, extrinsic explanations, because a decisive control is missing.**
  - **No evidence the probed layer is superconducting.**
    - The absence of any gap and any temperature dependence across Tc (6, 21, 35 K; up to 55 K for the second sample) is equally consistent with a non-superconducting or degraded surface layer.
    - Candidate causes include:
      - oxygen loss or Sr segregation in UHV;
      - cleave-induced strain or microcracking;
      - local over-doping (the authors' own fits reach p ≈ 0.3, beyond the LSCO superconducting dome);
      - near-surface damage related to FIB milling.
    - SQUID magnetometry is bulk-sensitive and says nothing about the top ~5 Å probed by VUV ARPES.
  - **Missing side-surface control.**
    - The natural control is a FIB-cleaved (100) side surface of the same crystal. There, no flat band is expected and the d_{x²−y²} order parameter is not pair-broken, so an antinodal gap should remain visible.
    - Without this, or an equivalent control such as a (001) surface of the same x = 0.22 crystal measured in the same session, the "disorder broadens the flat band" interpretation cannot be distinguished from "the surface is simply not superconducting."
    - The (001) reference in Supplement E is from a different doping (x = 0.19). It uses a different photon energy and resolution, and it does not test the FIB/side-cleave procedure.
  - **No surface chemistry or structure characterisation.**
    - There are no XPS core-level data (Cu 2p satellite structure, O 1s, La/Sr ratio).
    - There is no LEED or cross-sectional TEM, so surface order and stoichiometry are undetermined.
    - The AFM appears to be ex situ and lateral-resolution-limited.

- **(ii) Internal inconsistency in the expected gap magnitude undermines both the experimental "gap suppression" claim and the simulations.**
  - **Expected versus measured gap.**
    - The text argues that a gap of "approximately 20 meV" is expected at x = 0.22 and is absent. Yet the authors' own (001) reference at x = 0.19 yields only ~7 meV from symmetrized EDCs.
    - At the more overdoped x = 0.22, the gap should be smaller still. It would then be comparable to the 3–4 meV resolution plus thermal and kz broadening.
    - The "absence of a gap" is therefore far less surprising, and less diagnostic, than presented.
  - **Simulated gap is ~3× too large.**
    - The BdG pairing strength was tuned to give ~24 meV at the antinode, about three times the measured value.
    - A smaller gap implies a longer coherence length and a more delocalised edge state. The edge state would then have lower weight within the ~5 Å probing depth, be more kz-smeared, and be more fragile to disorder.
    - The quantitative conclusions depend on this choice: that AFM roughness is "insufficient," and that 100 meV disorder is what is needed. Both may change qualitatively with a realistic Δ.
  - **Re-tuned pairing in the disordered model.**
    - The pairing interaction was increased from 0.153 to 0.2156 (units stated as meV, presumably eV) in the disordered calculation to restore the gap.
    - This artificially compensates for impurity pair-breaking. The bulk state being compared is therefore not the same physical system.

- **(iii) The disorder model is not independently constrained and is not "moderate"; the central conclusion is sufficiency, not necessity.**
  - **Strength of the disorder.**
    - Gaussian on-site disorder of 100 meV on every site is described as "moderate" and "comparable to the superconducting gap." It is in fact 5–14× the gap and a sizeable fraction of t.
    - A Born estimate gives a normal-state scattering rate Γ ≈ πN(0)σ² of order 10–20 meV, i.e., Γ ~ Δ.
    - For a d-wave superconductor, non-magnetic disorder at this level should strongly suppress Tc (Abrikosov–Gor'kov-type pair breaking). It should also produce large nodal and antinodal linewidths.
  - **Disorder not cross-checked against the data.**
    - The disorder level is not compared with the measured bulk momentum-distribution-curve (MDC) widths at the node, with residual resistivity, or with the observed (001) gap and coherence-peak sharpness.
    - Supplement Fig. 12, which shows a surviving bulk gap, is shown only qualitatively.
  - **Single disorder type, unclear averaging.**
    - Only one disorder type is explored: uncorrelated Gaussian on-site disorder. Realistic out-of-plane Sr/O dopant potentials in LSCO are smooth and forward-scattering.
    - By the authors' own chirality argument, such disorder should be much less effective at hybridising the flat band. The selected model may thus be the most favourable choice for the authors' conclusion.
    - It is not stated whether results are averaged over multiple realisations, or how sensitive they are to the slab size (280 × 200 sites).
  - **Overclaiming.**
    - The abstract states that disorder is "the key factor currently preventing" flat bands. The evidence only shows that one disorder model is a sufficient explanation.
    - Several alternatives are not excluded:
      - surface non-superconductivity (point i);
      - spontaneous symmetry breaking that gaps or splits the flat band (TRSB d+is, phase crystals, edge magnetism), which is the very physics motivating the study and which the self-consistent calculation excludes by restricting to a single d-wave channel;
      - sub-surface displacement of the states;
      - sub-resolution micro-faceting.

- **(iv) Data analysis and presentation do not yet support a quantitative claim of absence, and there are numerous inconsistencies.**
  - **Unquantified sensitivity.**
    - The key experimental evidence (Fig. 4g) consists of EDCs that are background-subtracted (following Ref. 77), normalised to total intensity, and integrated over k-ranges defined from simulations.
    - No raw EDCs, error bars, or detection-limit analysis are given. In particular, the paper does not state what fraction of the simulated peak weight would survive the experimental background, resolution, 6 K thermal broadening, and analyzer response.
    - Background subtraction could remove precisely a broad, weak near-EF enhancement.
  - **Missing standard gap analyses.**
    - Standard gap diagnostics are absent for the (110) data: leading-edge shift versus gold, symmetrisation or Fermi–Dirac division at the antinode, and comparison of node versus antinode.
    - These are what would turn "we do not observe a gap" into a quantitative upper bound.
  - **Inconsistencies and errors that reduce confidence.**
    - **kz broadening:** the Methods state ~0.4 Å⁻¹ (2/λ) while the Fig. 3 caption states 0.2 Å⁻¹.
    - **Units:**
      - "kF = −0.4605 Å" should be Å⁻¹;
      - pairing interaction "V = 0.153 meV" is implausibly small for a ~24 meV gap;
      - the Fig. 4b axis "r[11] (Å⁻¹)" should be Å;
      - "2 amt" appears to be a typo for atm;
      - "40˘200 eV" is garbled.
    - **Notation:** the doping is variously labelled "p" and "x."
    - **Equation:** the supplementary map is written as R \ {0} rather than R² \ {0}.
    - **Data availability:** the section is empty.
    - **Sample statistics:** it is not reported how many cleaves and samples were measured, how many succeeded, or whether the beam spot (40–50 µm on a ~150 µm cleave) included steps or other facets.

**4. Suggestions for improvement**

- **(1) Add decisive control measurements.**
  - Measure a FIB-cleaved (100)/(010) side surface of the same x = 0.22 crystal under identical conditions. A visible antinodal gap there would validate that side-cleaved surfaces are superconducting and would isolate the (110)-specific physics.
  - Measure the (001) surface of the same x = 0.22 crystal in the same beamtime.
  - Ideally, repeat at a lower doping (e.g., x ≈ 0.15) with a larger gap and Tc, or in a cleaner, near-stoichiometric cuprate.
  - Consider laser or low-photon-energy ARPES. Their longer probing depth and better resolution would better match the coherence length and could detect states displaced below the topmost layer.
- **(2) Characterise the cleaved surface beyond AFM.**
  - Provide in-situ LEED.
  - Provide XPS core levels to assess stoichiometry, oxygen content, and implanted ion species from PFIB.
  - Provide cross-sectional TEM/STEM of a cleaved notch to quantify any damaged or amorphous layer and micro-faceting.
  - Quantify the actual surface misorientation from the photon-energy maps.
  - If feasible, use higher-lateral-resolution probes (nc-AFM, or STM on larger cleaves) to constrain roughness on the sub-nm scale that the simulations identify as critical.
- **(3) Make the theory quantitatively consistent and broader in scope.**
  - **Realistic gap.** Recalibrate the BdG pairing to the measured gap (~5–10 meV) without re-tuning it when disorder is added. Report the resulting Tc and gap suppression explicitly.
  - **Disorder phase diagram.** Map flat-band peak visibility versus disorder strength, correlation length, and type: on-site Gaussian, dilute strong scatterers, smooth out-of-plane dopant potentials, and hopping disorder. Average over many realisations and check finite-size effects.
  - **Cross-check against observables.** Identify where the bulk nodal linewidth, the (001) gap, and Tc remain consistent with experiment.
  - **Subdominant orders.** Include s- or d_xy-wave subdominant channels and magnetic channels in the self-consistency to test whether spontaneous splitting or gapping of the flat band could also explain the data.
  - **Literature comparison.** Place the results explicitly in the context of the existing quasiclassical and BdG work on disorder-broadened (110) Andreev states.
- **(4) Strengthen data analysis, transparency, and framing.**
  - **Raw and analysed data.** Show raw (non-background-subtracted) EDCs with error bars. Present leading-edge-shift and symmetrised or Fermi-divided spectra for the (110) nodal and antinodal cuts at all temperatures.
  - **Detection limit.** Forward-model the simulated spectra through the experimental background, resolution, and temperature to state a detection limit, e.g., an upper bound on the flat-band spectral weight or peak height.
  - **Reproducibility.** Report the number of samples and cleaves, and the spot position relative to cleave features.
  - **Fix inconsistencies.** Correct the kz-broadening values, units, notation, and typos listed above, and populate the data availability section.
  - **Tone down claims.** Replace "reveal disorder as the key factor" with "show that bulk disorder of this type is a sufficient explanation." Drop "moderate"/"comparable to the gap" for 100 meV disorder. Explicitly discuss the competing explanations (non-superconducting surface layer, symmetry-broken splitting, sub-surface displacement) and state what future measurements would discriminate among them.
