Review outline:

**1. Significance and novelty**

- **Scope of the work.**
  - The manuscript applies focused-ion-beam (FIB)-notch-controlled cleaving, previously shown by Hunter et al. only on normal-state Sr₂RuO₄, to a cuprate superconductor for the first time.
  - The authors report what they describe as the first momentum-resolved ARPES study of a cuprate side surface: the (110) face of overdoped La₂₋ₓSrₓCuO₄ (x = 0.22), measured in the superconducting state.
- **Why the target matters.**
  - Zero-energy Andreev flat bands on (110) d-wave surfaces have been predicted for about 30 years (Hu; Ryu–Hatsugai; Sato et al.).
  - So far they have been inferred only from zero-bias conductance peaks (ZBCPs) in tunneling, whose interpretation is contested.
  - A momentum-resolved probe could in principle separate topological flat bands from trivial in-gap states. It could also open access to proposed surface symmetry-broken phases: TRSB states, phase crystals and edge magnetism.
- **Main experimental findings.**
  - Bulk-like Fermi surface and nodal/antinodal dispersions are recovered via photon-energy (kz) and deflector scans.
  - No superconducting gap is resolved within 3–4 meV.
  - No flat-band peak or dip is resolved at the antinodal kz.
- **Main theoretical findings.**
  - Self-consistent BdG slab simulations use edge roughness taken from AFM data. They indicate that the measured roughness does not destroy the flat band.
  - Adding Gaussian on-site "mass" (potential) disorder with σ = 100 meV broadens the flat band beyond detectability.
- **Assessment of novelty.**
  - The technical advance (side-surface ARPES on a cuprate) is genuinely novel and of broad methodological interest.
  - The physics content is essentially a null result plus a model-based interpretation.
  - The novelty of the physical conclusion ("disorder is the key factor preventing flat bands") is therefore much weaker than the technical novelty. Its significance depends on how convincingly trivial explanations are excluded.

**2. Potential reasons for acceptance**

- **Technical milestone.**
  - A cuprate (110) side surface is prepared reproducibly by FIB-defined cleaving, with sub-lattice-constant RMS roughness (1.8 Å by AFM).
  - Bulk Tc is preserved after milling (SQUID).
  - This is likely to be adopted for other layered quantum materials where side surfaces are inaccessible, e.g. ruthenates, nickelates and other cuprates.
- **Data quality and Brillouin-zone access.**
  - Data quality is sufficient to map the (001) Fermi surface via hν scans from a side surface, and the [001]–[1̄10] plane via deflector scans.
  - kz-selective access to nodal (79 eV) and antinodal (95 eV) regions is demonstrated. This provides a useful template for future side-surface experiments.
- **Theoretical framework.**
  - The framework is careful and clearly presented:
    - winding-number sectors;
    - analytic decay of the edge states;
    - the argument that a small Δ/t gives the surface state substantial kz (bulk-like) character, so that spectral-weight contrast must be sought at the antinodes;
    - inclusion of ARPES matrix-element-free surface weighting and kz broadening.
  - The point that a pronounced dip-plus-peak structure survives even when the surface gap is suppressed is a useful experimental guide.
- **Honest reporting of a negative result.**
  - The negative result is reported transparently, including temperature-dependent EDCs, a second sample and acknowledgement of AFM resolution limits.
  - Such results are valuable to the community, given decades of ambiguous ZBCP literature.
- **Implications for disorder.**
  - The work raises a relevant and testable point. Inter-chirality, large-momentum-transfer scattering from short-range disorder may make (110) flat bands far more fragile in area-averaged probes than STM on selected nanoscale regions suggests.

**3. Potential reasons for rejection**

- **Reason 1: The null result is not shown to arise from a superconducting, intrinsic surface. Trivial explanations are not excluded.**
  - *SQUID data do not test the probed region.*
    - The SQUID check of a milled slab tests the bulk only.
    - It says nothing about the top ~5 Å probed by VUV ARPES at the cleave plane.
  - *Possible surface-damage mechanisms are not addressed.* No surface-sensitive characterization (XPS/core levels, LEED, in-situ STM) is provided to rule out:
    - FIB/PFIB-induced damage, amorphization or ion implantation near the notch;
    - oxygen loss or Sr segregation;
    - surface polarity or reconstruction of the (110) termination;
    - a dead layer.
  - *The doping estimate cannot exclude a non-superconducting surface.*
    - The surface doping from Fermi-surface fitting ranges from 0.20 to 0.30, and the authors themselves describe the fit as poor.
    - The upper end lies beyond the LSCO superconducting dome (which ends near p ≈ 0.27).
    - The data therefore cannot exclude a non-superconducting or heavily overdoped surface layer. This alone would explain both "missing" features.
  - *Temperature evolution is uninformative.*
    - EDCs at 6, 21 and 35 K (and up to 55 K for a second sample) are essentially indistinguishable. The small changes observed are attributed to extrinsic drift.
    - This is equally consistent with a surface that is simply not superconducting.
    - No leading-edge-midpoint analysis, Fermi–Dirac-divided or symmetrized side-surface EDCs are shown.
  - *The (001) reference is weak.*
    - It uses a different doping (x = 0.19).
    - It yields only ≈7 meV, versus the 15–25 meV cited as expected.
    - It thereby undermines rather than supports the claim that this preparation route preserves surface superconductivity at the expected strength.
  - *A natural control is missing.*
    - The most decisive control is a (100) side surface, where the d-wave order parameter is not pair-broken and no flat band is expected.
    - On such a surface an antinodal gap should be visible.
    - Without it, gap suppression and flat-band absence cannot be attributed to (110)-specific physics.

- **Reason 2: The geometric-roughness argument is weaker than the abstract states.**
  - *The relevant length scale is unresolved.*
    - The authors acknowledge that suppression requires roughness at the single-unit-cell lateral scale.
    - AFM lateral resolution (a few nm) cannot resolve this scale. In addition, the AFM data cover ~1 µm², while the beam spot is ~50 µm.
    - The statement that "measured geometric roughness … is too weak to eliminate these modes" therefore applies only to the roughness components AFM can see.
    - The claimed "upper bound on the disorder autocorrelation length" is logically reversed or ill-defined: AFM can only exclude long-wavelength roughness, not bound the short-wavelength part that matters.
  - *The roughness model is oversimplified.*
    - The AFM profile is mapped onto site removal in a single-orbital 2D model with a√2/2 resolution.
    - This ignores roughness along the c axis. Each CuO₂ plane in the body-centered stack may terminate at a different position, and ARPES integrates over the full (110)–[001] surface.
  - *Facets and misorientation are not addressed.*
    - Neither facet coexistence nor macroscopic misorientation within the spot (the SEM shows non-trivial topography) is quantified.
    - Misorientation is known to redistribute and weaken flat-band weight.
  - *AFM conditions are unclear.*
    - It is not stated whether AFM was performed in situ or after air exposure, or on the same cleave as the ARPES.
    - Ex-situ topography may not represent the measured surface.

- **Reason 3: The disorder model is ad hoc, internally inconsistent and not validated against independent constraints.**
  - *Disorder magnitude.*
    - The Introduction describes the disorder as "comparable to the superconducting gap".
    - The simulations use σ = 100 meV Gaussian potential disorder on every site, which is ~4–5× the 24 meV antinodal gap.
    - The pairing interaction had to be retuned (V from 0.153 to 0.21555, with units given as "meV", which appear implausible) to restore the bulk gap.
    - This indicates that the chosen disorder is strongly pair-breaking. Why this retuning is physically justified, rather than a sign of excessive disorder, is not discussed.
  - *No cross-check against measured scattering.* The implied normal-state scattering rate and mean free path are not compared with independent observables for LSCO x = 0.22:
    - residual resistivity;
    - nodal ARPES momentum widths (MDC widths) from the authors' own data;
    - superfluid-density and Tc-suppression constraints (Abrikosov–Gor'kov-type).
  - *Discrepancy with the cited microscopic picture.*
    - The literature cited (Özdemir, Lee-Hone, Hirschfeld) argues that overdoped LSCO disorder is predominantly weak, forward-scattering out-of-plane dopant disorder.
    - This is precisely the type that would *not* efficiently couple opposite-chirality sectors at large q.
    - Uncorrelated on-site disorder is thus the least representative choice for the mechanism invoked.
  - *Missing methodological details.*
    - The number of disorder realizations, the averaging procedure and the convergence/finite-size checks (280 × 200 sites relative to ξ and the disorder correlation length) are not stated.
    - No disorder-strength scan establishes the threshold at which the peak becomes undetectable.
  - *Engagement with prior theory is limited.*
    - Prior work on disorder effects on (110) Andreev bound states is cited only briefly (Kalenkov–Fogelström–Barash's two regimes) or not at all (e.g. Asano–Tanaka et al.).
    - The predicted bandwidth "proportional to disorder strength" is not benchmarked against these results.
  - *The interpretation is not falsifiable.*
    - With no free-parameter scan and no independent constraint on disorder, the conclusion that "disorder is the key factor" amounts to a demonstration that it *can* explain the data. It does not show that it *does*.
    - Alternative mechanisms are not excluded quantitatively. These include surface splitting from TRSB or magnetic order, which would yield sub-resolution split peaks, and surface non-superconductivity.

- **Reason 4: The experiment–theory comparison is qualitative, and several methodological inconsistencies weaken the quantitative claims.**
  - *kz broadening is reported inconsistently.*
    - Methods derive Δkz ≈ 0.4 Å⁻¹ (FWHM = 2/λ, λ ≈ 4.7 Å).
    - The Fig. 3 caption states that all calculations use Δk₍₁₁₀₎ = 0.2 Å⁻¹ and λ ≈ 5 Å.
    - The text also notes that the calculations overestimate kz broadening and attributes this to aliasing and spectral leakage of the slab.
    - Since flat-band visibility at the antinodal kz depends critically on this broadening, the inconsistency must be resolved.
  - *Questionable TPP-2M input.*
    - The 20 meV superconducting gap is used as the "band gap" parameter, which is physically inappropriate (the insulating band gap enters at the eV scale).
    - The effect is minor numerically but signals imprecision.
  - *Processing could suppress weak features.*
    - The EDCs in Fig. 4g are normalized to total intensity after an empirical background subtraction (Ref. 77) and integrated over broad k windows.
    - These steps could suppress or create weak near-EF features.
    - No raw EDCs, Fermi–Dirac-divided spectra, symmetrized EDCs or error bars are shown.
  - *The undetectability claim is not quantified.*
    - The "visual agreement" between Fig. 4d (right) and Fig. 4f is not quantified, e.g. by a χ² comparison or forward-modelling with the measured background, resolution and S/N.
    - There is no estimate of the minimum flat-band spectral weight that would have been detectable. Without it, "suppressed beyond detectability" cannot be evaluated.
  - *Theory-section errors.* Several statements in the theory section are imprecise or erroneous:
    - "dk: S¹ → ℝ\{0}" should read ℝ²\{0}.
    - ℝ²\{0} is referred to as a "group".
    - The trigonometric expansion yields a circular Fermi surface, which is not representative of the antinodal LSCO van Hove regime at x = 0.22.
    - The Discussion explains tunnel-junction ZBCPs via disorder pushing states below the top layer, citing a Bi₂Se₃ sputtering study. This is speculative and not supported by the present calculations.

**4. Suggestions for improvement**

- **Suggestion 1: Establish that the probed surface is superconducting and intrinsic.** Add the following controls:
  - FIB-cleaved (100) side surfaces of the same crystal, where an antinodal gap should survive and no flat band is expected. This is the key discriminating control.
  - An (001) reference on the same x = 0.22 crystal under identical preparation.
  - Leading-edge-midpoint and symmetrized/Fermi–Dirac-divided EDC analyses on the (110) surface, with error bars, across Tc.
  - XPS/core-level checks for Ga/Xe implantation, Sr segregation and oxygen stoichiometry; LEED/RHEED for surface crystallinity and termination.
  - A tighter, error-bounded surface-doping estimate, e.g. constraining fits with the nodal kF and antinodal Lifshitz topology.
  - Ideally, optimally doped LSCO (larger gap, higher Tc) or a cleaner cuprate, to enlarge the expected spectral contrast.

- **Suggestion 2: Strengthen the roughness and spatial-averaging analysis.**
  - Perform in-situ or same-cleave topography with higher lateral resolution, e.g. STM on a larger FIB-defined facet or high-resolution AFM tips, over areas comparable to the beam spot.
  - Quantify facet misorientation with SEM/EBSD or with ARPES kz/angle offsets.
  - Extend the simulations to include interlayer (c-axis) variation of edge position and small misorientation angles.
  - Use nano-ARPES (sub-µm spot) or spatial scanning of the existing beam to test the authors' own dilution hypothesis. If flat-band weight resides on isolated segments, a smaller spot should reveal spatially varying near-EF weight.
  - Rephrase the claimed "upper bound" correctly: AFM excludes long-wavelength roughness only.

- **Suggestion 3: Make the disorder modelling systematic and physically constrained.**
  - Scan σ (e.g. 10–150 meV) and report:
    - the flat-band peak height and width;
    - the bulk gap;
    - Tc (or V-retuning).
  - Report realization averages and finite-size checks.
  - Compute the implied normal-state scattering rate and residual resistivity, and nodal MDC widths. Compare these with the measured nodal linewidths in Fig. 3d and with LSCO x = 0.22 transport and superfluid-density data.
  - Compare uncorrelated on-site disorder with:
    - spatially correlated (smooth) out-of-plane dopant potentials;
    - dilute strong scatterers;
    - hopping/pairing disorder.
  - Explicitly show whether realistic forward-scattering disorder can suppress the flat band.
  - Benchmark against existing theory of disorder and Andreev bound states (Kalenkov–Fogelström–Barash; Asano–Tanaka; Queiroz–Schnyder).
  - Fix the units of V. Reconcile the "comparable to the gap" wording with σ = 100 meV.

- **Suggestion 4: Quantify detectability and tone down the conclusions accordingly.**
  - Forward-model the clean-surface prediction with:
    - the actual background;
    - the experimental resolution;
    - a consistent kz broadening (resolve the 0.2 vs 0.4 Å⁻¹ discrepancy);
    - the measured S/N and identical integration windows.
  - From this, derive a quantitative detection threshold, e.g. the minimum flat-band weight relative to the bulk.
  - Show raw spectra before background subtraction and normalization.
  - Discuss alternative explanations explicitly: a non-superconducting surface, TRSB- or magnetism-induced splitting below resolution, and facet mixing.
  - Recast the abstract and conclusions accordingly. The central, well-supported contribution is the technique and the null result. "Bulk disorder as the key factor" should be presented as a plausible hypothesis consistent with the data, not as an established cause.
  - Correct the minor errors in the theory section and the TPP-2M input.
