Review outline:

## 1. Significance and novelty

The paper adapts focused-ion-beam (FIB) notch-defined cleaving, previously shown on normal-state Sr₂RuO₄, to a cuprate. It reports ARPES in the superconducting state on a (110) side surface of overdoped La₂₋ₓSrₓCuO₄ (LSCO, x = 0.22). The authors report no spectral gap and no zero-energy flat-band peak. Using self-consistent Bogoliubov–de Gennes (BdG) slab calculations, they argue that the AFM-measured roughness cannot remove the flat band, whereas Gaussian on-site disorder of 100 meV can.

The technique is a real step: it opens cuprate side surfaces to momentum-resolved probes. The physics conclusion, that bulk disorder is "the key factor" suppressing the flat band, rests on a null result whose alternative explanations are not yet excluded, and on a disorder model that has not been constrained independently.

## 2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- **Technique.** This is the first demonstration of FIB-controlled side-surface cleaving on a high-Tc cuprate. The AFM roughness is low (1.8 Å RMS), and the ARPES is good enough to recover the (001) Fermi surface through photon-energy dependence and to show [001] dispersion directly (Fig. 3).
- **Calculations.** The self-consistent BdG slab includes an ARPES-like surface weighting, kz broadening, and an AFM-derived edge profile. This is a useful framework for anyone pursuing side-surface ARPES, as is the sector-resolved EDC analysis (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 10).
- **Topological physics.** The supplementary treatment of the winding number, chirality sectors, and the small-Δ/t limit (spectral weight evolving into the normal-state Fermi surface, Eqs. 30–33) explains clearly why side-surface flat bands acquire kz dependence in ARPES.
- **The null result.** If made rigorous, it is informative for the long-standing debate over zero-bias conductance peaks.

## 3. Concerns

### A. Must address

**1. A non-superconducting or chemically altered surface layer is not excluded.**

- *The problem.* The simplest explanation for seeing neither a gap nor a flat band is that the top ~5 Å is not superconducting. Possible causes include oxygen loss from local heating during milling, damage or redeposition near the notch, surface overdoping or Sr segregation, or a polar or reconstructed termination.
- *Why the existing evidence does not settle it.*
  - The SQUID data (Fig. 2b) are bulk-sensitive and report only the onset. They cannot detect a nanometre-scale dead layer.
  - The surface-doping estimate (Sec. II C; Supplementary F) gives p = 0.2271 nominally, but alternative fits ranged from 0.20–0.25 up to about 0.30. The upper end lies beyond the superconducting dome of LSCO (about 0.27). The data therefore do not exclude a non-superconducting surface.
- *Why it matters.* This alternative would by itself explain both headline observations (no gap, no flat band), without invoking pair-breaking or disorder.
- *What I would ask for.* The decisive control is a geometry where no pair-breaking or flat band is expected, prepared by the identical FIB process at the same doping:
  - a FIB-cleaved (100) side surface, the "trivial" edge of Fig. 1d; and/or
  - an (001) surface of the same x = 0.22 crystal.

  A bulk-like gap on such a surface would establish that the process preserves near-surface superconductivity. Its absence would point to the surface layer.
- *Further items.*
  - State the distance between the ARPES spot and the milled faces of the notch.
  - State whether spatial maps across the ~150 µm cleave face were taken.
  - Give the cleave temperature.
  - Discuss the (110) termination and its polarity.

**2. Gap magnitude: the reference measurement, the model and the text disagree, and this changes what "absence" means.**

- *The inconsistent numbers.*
  - Introduction: 15–25 meV.
  - Section II D: 10–20 meV, then "approximately 20 meV at our doping level."
  - The BdG pairing strength V is tuned to give about 24 meV (Methods F).
  - The authors' own (001) reference (Supplementary E) is on a different doping (x = 0.19) and yields only about 7 meV. Its "same conditions" preparation is not described, so it is unclear whether it was FIB-cleaved.
- *Why it matters.*
  - A 7 meV gap measured at 3–4 meV resolution, plus kz smearing, is a much weaker test than a 24 meV gap.
  - The simulated dip, coherence peak and flat-band contrast in Fig. 4e are all computed with the larger gap.
  - The disorder-to-gap ratio of 100/24 used to call the disorder "moderate" and "comparable to the gap" (abstract, introduction) becomes about 14 against the measured gap.
- *What I would ask for.*
  - Measure the antinodal gap at x = 0.22 on a reference surface.
  - Re-run the clean and disordered simulations with V chosen to reproduce that measured gap.
  - Show that the predicted dip, peak and coherence features remain resolvable at 3–4 meV resolution and 6 K.

**3. The disorder model is not constrained independently, and the pairing strength was retuned to compensate for it.**

- *The specific problems.*
  - **Single strength, unclear averaging.** Only σ = 100 meV Gaussian, site-uncorrelated on-site potential is shown. It is not stated whether results are averaged over realizations.
  - **Pairing retuned.** V was raised by about 40% (from 0.153 to 0.21555, with units given as "meV") when disorder was added. This retuning keeps the bulk gap alive in Fig. 12 and should be stated in the main text.
  - **Pair-breaking estimate.** Short-range scattering of this size gives, in the Born limit, a scattering rate of order π N(0) σ². That is tens of meV, comparable to or larger than the gap. For d-wave pairing this is strongly pair-breaking: with fixed V it should suppress Tc substantially. The measured Tc (about 25 K, Fig. 2b) and the sharpness of the nodal bands in Fig. 3d are therefore direct constraints the model must satisfy.
  - **Wrong kind of disorder for LSCO?** The paper's own mechanism needs large-q, inter-chirality scattering (Supplementary C). Yet the dominant LSCO disorder is out-of-plane Sr dopants, generally regarded as smooth, forward-scattering potentials (Refs. 73–74). These would couple opposite chiralities only weakly.
- *What I would ask for.*
  - A scan of σ, and of the disorder correlation length, with ensemble averages.
  - The threshold at which the flat-band peak becomes undetectable at 3–4 meV resolution.
  - A comparison of the simulated nodal MDC widths and quasiparticle lifetimes with the measured 79 eV nodal widths.
  - The Tc or gap suppression at fixed V.
  - At least one calculation with realistic extended dopant potentials.

  Without these, "moderate Anderson disorder" is a fitting parameter rather than evidence.

**4. The data do not discriminate between bulk disorder and sub-AFM-resolution surface roughness.**

- *The problem.* The text concedes several points:
  - Roughness at the unit-cell scale would broaden the surface states.
  - Such roughness is below AFM lateral resolution, and AFM tip convolution means 1.8 Å RMS is only a lower bound on high-spatial-frequency roughness.
  - The "Edge 0.5" probabilistic-roughness case is shown in Fig. 4b,e but is never defined in the main text.
- *Why it matters.* If "Edge 0.5" (or a realistic atomic-scale roughness model) also removes the peak, then geometric disorder at the surface is an equally viable explanation. The claim in the abstract and conclusion that bulk inhomogeneity is "the key factor" would then be unsupported.
- *What I would ask for.* Either:
  - provide a measurement that distinguishes the two (for example, atomic-resolution topography by STM or AFM on a test cleave, or a disorder-sensitive observable that differs between them); or
  - reframe the conclusion as "disorder, either sub-nanometre surface roughness or bulk short-range disorder."

  Define GE0.5 explicitly, and state what fraction of edge sites it removes.

### B. Should address

**5. The null result needs a quantitative detection limit.**

- *Missing elements.*
  - Fig. 4g shows EDCs after background subtraction (Ref. 77) and normalization to total intensity. The raw, unsubtracted EDCs and the subtraction procedure should be shown, because a weak peak near E_F can be absorbed by the background model.
  - Provide the expected flat-band peak-to-background ratio for the clean and AFM-rough models, after convolution with the experimental Gaussian resolution (the simulation uses a 3 meV Lorentzian), the Fermi function at 6 K, and the measured noise. Compare it with the statistical noise in each sector window.
  - Provide a leading-edge-midpoint analysis against gold, with uncertainties, for the antinodal and topological-sector EDCs. This turns "no gap within resolution" into a numerical upper bound, as was done for the (001) reference.
- *Areal dilution.* Quantify the surviving surface fraction required to drop below detection. Areal dilution (Discussion) reduces intensity in proportion to that fraction; it does not eliminate it.

**6. Photoemission matrix elements and polarization.**

- The light polarization for the (110) measurements is not stated.
- With the surface normal along [110], the dipole selection rules for the d(x²−y²)-derived states, and for the electron and hole components of the surface state, differ from the familiar (001) geometry.
- Please report the polarization. Show spectra with both linear polarizations, or at least argue that the topological-sector window is not suppressed by matrix elements.
- State whether other photon energies probing equivalent kz in other zones were tried.

**7. kz broadening and surface weighting are internally inconsistent.**

- Methods D gives λ = 4.74 Å and Δkz ≈ 0.4 Å⁻¹ (2/λ). The Fig. 3 caption states Δk[110] = 0.2 Å⁻¹ from λ ≈ 5 Å. Section II C mentions "on the order of 0.4 Å⁻¹," then argues that the calculation overestimates the broadening.
- Clarify:
  - which value enters Figs. 3 and 4;
  - whether the exponential weight exp(−r/λ) is applied to amplitudes or to intensities (amplitude weighting halves the effective probing depth); and
  - how sensitive the flat-band and gap visibility are to this choice.
- Using the superconducting gap as the "band gap" input to TPP-2M is not physically meaningful. This is harmless numerically, but it should be stated as such.

**8. Surface orientation and topography at the beam-spot scale.**

- The AFM map in Fig. 2d covers about 3 µm, whereas the beam spot is 40–50 µm (FWHM).
- Please state whether the AFM was done in situ or ex situ, and before or after ARPES. Give the tip radius and the lateral resolution.
- Provide large-area topography (AFM or SEM over the actual ARPES footprint) and quantify step and facet densities. The SEM image in Fig. 2c shows macroscopic structure.
- The Fermi-surface fit needed an extra four-fold-symmetry-breaking parameter (Supplementary F). This could reflect misorientation of the cleave from (110). Misorientation shrinks the topological sector and mixes in trivial facets, so quantify the surface normal from the ARPES k-space geometry, or by EBSD or Laue on the cleaved face.

**9. The surface-doping estimate is weak as stated.**

- The Luttinger-area fit uses kF points only.
- It adds a symmetry-breaking parameter and depends on the inner potential and final-state approximations, which the authors acknowledge distort the map.
- The text alternates between p = 0.22, 0.2271, "x = 0.2–0.25," and "up to about 0.3."
- Please give:
  - an uncertainty from a defined procedure;
  - the inner potential used;
  - a cross-check from the nodal kF and velocity against Ref. 83 at x = 0.22; and
  - the antinodal band-bottom position relative to the Lifshitz transition (Ref. 54).

  Together these would make the "no reconstruction" statement defensible. This bears directly on concern 1.

**10. Sample statistics and stability.**

- State how many crystals and cleaves were measured, and on which of them the absence of the gap and peak was confirmed. Fig. 11 adds one sample.
- Show whether spectra evolved with time after cleaving.
- Show spatial reproducibility across the cleave face.
- The temperature series (6, 21, 35 K; up to 55 K in Fig. 11) spans Tc, yet no change is seen, which is consistent with any of the explanations. Report Tc for the measured crystals explicitly. Fig. 2b suggests about 25 K, which should be compared with the x = 0.22 value used.

**11. The argument about whether a gap should be visible is internally inconsistent.**

- Section II D states that "one in general does not expect the superconducting gap to be observable at a (110) surface."
- Yet the title and abstract present the gap suppression as a finding.
- Meanwhile, the clean simulation (Fig. 4b,e) retains a finite edge pairing amplitude (about 2–4 meV at r = 0) and visible gap-edge or coherence features.
- Please state clearly what the model predicts for the gap observable at 4 meV resolution, and whether the measured absence is a discriminating test.

**12. The model geometry is simplified relative to the real surface.**

- The 2D single-layer slab ignores the body-centred stacking. On a (110) face, adjacent CuO₂ layers are offset along the surface normal by a/√2, which produces intrinsic edge-height alternation along [001], within the plane probed by ARPES.
- It also ignores c-axis coupling, which matters for kz mapping in LSCO (Fig. 3c; Ref. 90) and for the [001] dispersion measured in this paper.
- At x ≈ 0.22, LSCO is close to the LTO/HTT boundary, so possible twin domains or facets should also be discussed.
- Please discuss whether these features affect the flat-band visibility.

### C. Minor

13. **Units.**
    - V = 0.153 meV is implausibly small for the pairing interaction; eV is presumably intended.
    - In Methods C, k_F = −0.4605 should carry units of Å⁻¹.
    - In Fig. 4b, the axis label "r[11] (Å⁻¹)" should be in Å.
    - Fig. 1d gives no energy units.

14. **Labels.** The Fig. 4 caption abbreviations (C, GEAFM, GE0.5, BA) do not match the legend (Clean, Edge AFM, Edge 0.5, Mixed). Unify them.

15. **Supplementary A.**
    - "R \ {0} ≃ S¹" should read R² \ {0}.
    - R² \ {0} is called a "group."
    - α in Eq. (26) is undefined.
    - Dimensional consistency of Eq. (18) should be checked.
    - The class CI assignment for the reduced Nambu block deserves a sentence of justification.

16. **Methods typos.** "2 amt" should be atm; "40˘200 eV" should be 40–200 eV; "δ = a√(2)" needs formatting; "Supplementary Fig. VI J" and similar cross-references need fixing.

17. **Fig. 2b.** Different fields (5 Oe and 2 Oe) and unnormalized susceptibilities make comparison difficult. Show normalized χ and the shielding fraction.

18. **Data availability.** The Data Availability section is empty.

19. **Discussion.** The appeal to Ref. 76 (Bi₂Se₃ sputtering) to argue that surface states are displaced from the top layer is speculative for this system. Either support it with a calculation or label it as conjecture.

20. **Priority claim.** The claim of the "first momentum-resolved measurements on a cuprate side surface" should be checked against prior ARPES on non-(001) cuprate faces and thin films, and qualified if necessary.

## 4. Suggestions for improvement

1. **Null geometry under matched conditions.** Prepare a (100) side surface with the same FIB protocol and doping, and measure it under identical ARPES conditions. Expect a bulk-like gap and no flat band. This single control would separate "pair-breaking plus disorder" from "non-superconducting surface."

2. **Recalibrate the model to the measured gap.** Calibrate V to the measured x = 0.22 gap. Then scan disorder strength and correlation length with ensemble averaging, and confront each case with the measured Tc, nodal MDC widths and antinodal linewidths. Report the minimum disorder consistent with the null result and the maximum consistent with bulk data.

3. **Make the null result quantitative.** Show raw EDCs, leading-edge-midpoint bounds, polarization-dependent spectra, and a simulated-versus-measured detection threshold in each momentum sector.

4. **Narrow the conclusions to what is excluded.** State explicitly that the AFM-resolvable roughness is ruled out. State that atomic-scale surface roughness, bulk short-range disorder, and surface chemical modification remain open unless they are excluded by the additional controls above. The technical advance stands on its own and does not need the stronger physics claim.
