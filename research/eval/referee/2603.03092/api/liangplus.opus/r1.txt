Review outline:

**1. Significance and novelty**

The manuscript reports what appears to be the first momentum-resolved (ARPES) study of a cuprate side surface in the superconducting state. A FIB-defined notch enforces a (110) cleave in overdoped La₂₋ₓSrₓCuO₄ (x = 0.22). The technique is a real advance; it extends the Sr₂RuO₄ approach of Hunter et al. to a high-Tc cuprate.

The physics results are null:
- no resolvable gap, and
- no zero-energy flat-band peak.

The authors attribute the missing flat band to bulk Anderson-type disorder, supported by self-consistent BdG slab calculations. The methodological contribution is clear. The physical interpretation, that disorder rather than surface preparation is "the key factor," is currently much less well supported than the abstract suggests.

**2. Potential reasons for acceptance**

- **New and broadly useful technique.** Controlled FIB-notch cleaving gives mm-scale-flat (110) side surfaces of a layered cuprate. AFM rms roughness is 1.8 Å (Fig. 2d–e). SQUID shows that Tc of the milled material is unaffected (Fig. 2b).
- **Good electronic-structure quality.**
  - Photon-energy mapping recovers the (001) Fermi surface from the side surface (Fig. 3b, Fig. 9).
  - Nodal and antinodal dispersions are sharp (Fig. 3c–e).
  - Results are reproduced on a second sample (Fig. 11).
- **Clear pedagogical theory.** The material on winding numbers, the kz/k⊥ distribution of the edge-state weight in the small-Δ/t limit (Supp. VI A–B), and chirality-sector scattering (VI C) is useful to the community.
- **A worthwhile negative result.** The absence of a flat-band signature on a macroscopically averaged, topographically good (110) surface is valuable, especially given decades of ambiguous ZBCP literature.

**3. Concerns**

**A. Must address**

1. **No control separates pair-breaking from a non-superconducting or altered surface layer (Sec. II D, Fig. 4g).**
   - *The problem.* Absence of a gap and absence of a flat-band peak are both what one would see if the top ~5 Å were simply not superconducting. Possible causes include:
     - an overdoped or oxygen-modified surface layer;
     - cleave-induced disorder;
     - polarity-driven charge reconstruction of the (110) termination;
     - a dead layer.
   - *Why the current evidence is insufficient.* Bulk SQUID (Fig. 2b) and a temperature series spanning Tc (6, 21, 35 K) that shows no change cannot discriminate these scenarios. The headline claim of "suppression of the superconducting spectral gap" by pair-breaking therefore rests on theory, not on the data.
   - *The decisive control.* A (100) side surface prepared identically, ideally from the same rod. There a d-wave gap should survive at the surface and no flat band is expected.
     - If a ~5–10 meV antinodal gap is seen on (100) but not on (110), the pair-breaking interpretation and the surface quality are both validated.
     - If no gap is seen on (100) either, the surface is not representative of the bulk superconductor, and the disorder interpretation of the missing flat band is moot.
   - *If (100) data cannot be obtained.* The authors should state explicitly that the gap absence cannot be attributed to pair-breaking. They should rephrase the abstract accordingly.

2. **The surface doping estimate is too uncertain to exclude a non-superconducting surface (Sec. II C; Supp. VI F, Fig. 9).**
   - *The problem.* Surface hole concentration is inferred from Fermi-pocket areas in a photon-energy map. The authors themselves note:
     - a distorted FS;
     - kz broadening of ~0.4 Å⁻¹ along exactly the mapped direction;
     - final-state and beamline effects;
     - a fit that "does not perfectly match the data";
     - estimates of 0.2–0.3 depending on constraints.
   - *Why it matters.* For LSCO the superconducting dome ends near p ≈ 0.27. A surface at p ≳ 0.27 would trivially show neither gap nor Andreev states, so the quoted range does not establish "little variation between bulk and surface."
   - *What the authors should do.*
     - Determine kF more precisely from fixed-hν deflector maps in the well-resolved k[1-10]–k[001] plane.
     - Use antinodal kF along k[1-10] and the position of the van Hove singularity relative to E_F. At x ≈ 0.2 this is near the Lifshitz transition (their Ref. 54) and is highly doping-sensitive.
     - Report the inner potential used and the kz assignments with uncertainties.
     - Report a doping value with a defensible error bar.

3. **The gap magnitude used in the simulations is inconsistent with the authors' own reference measurement.**
   - *The inconsistency.* Several different gap values are used or quoted:
     - The BdG pairing V is tuned to give a 24 meV antinodal gap (Methods III F).
     - The text quotes 15–25 meV (Intro), 10–20 meV (Sec. II D, "consistent with our (001)-surface reference") and ~20 meV (Sec. II D; Methods III D).
     - The authors' own (001) reference on an identically prepared x = 0.19 sample gives ~7 meV (Supp. VI E). The antinodal gap at x = 0.22 is expected to be smaller still.
   - *Why it matters.* The predicted visibility of the flat-band peak and the "dip–peak" signature (Fig. 4d–e) depends directly on Δ:
     - the edge-state decay length ξ ~ ħv_F/Δ sets how much weight lies within λ ≈ 5 Å;
     - the dip width is ~Δ.
     With Δ ≈ 5–7 meV, the dip becomes comparable to the 3–4 meV resolution, and surface-state weight in the probed layer is diluted.
   - *Consequences for the claims.* With a more realistic gap, the clean-case prediction that "a peak should be observable" may weaken substantially. The 100 meV disorder would then be far stronger relative to Δ.
   - *What the authors should do.* Repeat the clean, GE_AFM and GE_AFM+BA simulations with the bulk gap matched to the measured value (or bracketed, e.g. 7 and 12 meV). Reconcile the gap values quoted throughout the text.

4. **No quantitative detection limit; the theory–experiment comparison is visual only (Fig. 4d–g; Supp. VI G).**
   - *What is missing.*
     - Simulations are at 0 K with 3 meV Lorentzian broadening.
     - Experimental EDCs are background-subtracted (Ref. 77) and normalized to total intensity.
     - A zero-energy peak is half-truncated by the Fermi function. At 6 K with 3–4 meV Gaussian resolution, it would appear mostly as a sharpened or overshooting leading edge, not as a peak.
   - *What the authors should do.*
     - (i) Forward-model the clean and disordered spectral functions with the 6 K Fermi–Dirac function, the measured Gaussian resolution, and the same background and normalization as the data.
     - (ii) Show raw and gold-normalized (FD-divided) EDCs from the (110) surface in the band, topological and trivial windows.
     - (iii) Perform the same leading-edge-midpoint and symmetrization analysis applied to the (001) reference (Supp. VI E). This turns "no gap within 3–4 meV" into a quantitative bound.
     - (iv) Quote an upper bound on the surface-state spectral weight, as a fraction of the clean-model prediction, compatible with the data.
   - *Why it matters.* Without this, "suppression of the flat band" and "visual agreement" with the disordered model (Fig. 4d vs 4f) are not testable statements.

5. **The disorder model is a single, tuned point and is not checked against independent constraints (Sec. II D; Methods III E–F; Fig. 4, Fig. 12).**
   - *(a) No scan or ensemble.* A single Gaussian strength (σ = 100 meV) is shown. The authors should provide:
     - peak height versus σ, showing the threshold;
     - averaging over disorder realizations;
     - the variance of the result.
   - *(b) Compensated pairing is unphysical.* Non-magnetic disorder is pair-breaking for d-wave. The authors compensate by raising V from 0.153 to 0.21555 (units need clarification) so that the bulk gap remains 24 meV.
     - In the real material, disorder of this strength would suppress Tc substantially (Abrikosov–Gor'kov).
     - The authors should estimate the implied normal-state scattering rate. A Born-approximation estimate with ~100 meV on-site disorder and t ≈ 0.2–0.25 eV gives Γ of order 10–20 meV.
     - They should compare it with the measured nodal MDC widths (Fig. 3d), the residual resistivity of x = 0.22 LSCO, and Tc ≈ 27 K.
   - *(c) Realism of short-range disorder.* The authors' own argument is that killing the flat band requires large-q inter-chirality scattering, hence short-range disorder. However, the dominant disorder in LSCO is from out-of-plane Sr dopants, which is smooth and forward-scattering (their Refs. 73–74). By their own argument this would be ineffective.
     - The authors should simulate dilute, extended (Yukawa-like) dopant potentials of realistic strength.
     - Otherwise the conclusion that "bulk inhomogeneities characteristic of high-Tc superconductors" remove the flat band is not established.
   - *(d) The chirality argument needs refinement.* In Fig. 1c–d, segments of opposite chirality meet at the projections of the nodes, where small-q scattering can couple them. The statement that opposite-chirality states "reside at opposite momenta" should be qualified, and the k∥ dependence of the disorder-induced broadening shown.
   - *(e) Compatibility with the (001) gap.* The authors claim this disorder is "not inconsistent" with a gap on (001). Fig. 12 shows only a spatially resolved DOS. They should present a simulated (001) antinodal EDC with the same disorder and compare its coherence-peak width with the measured (001) reference.
   - *Overall.* This concern bears directly on the abstract's central conclusion.

**B. Should address**

6. **The geometric-roughness conclusion is overstated (Abstract; Sec. II D; Fig. 4b–e).**
   - *The circularity.* The edge profile is derived from AFM with nm-scale lateral resolution. The simulated edge therefore contains long flat terraces by construction, and the result that "measured roughness is too weak to eliminate these modes" partly reflects the AFM resolution. The authors acknowledge this, but the abstract does not.
   - *Unexamined possibilities.*
     - Nanofaceting of the (110) cleave into (100)/(010) microfacets. This would remove the flat band and could locally preserve a gap.
     - Plane-to-plane variation of the edge position along c.
     - Small miscut angles relative to (110), which should be quantified from Laue or ARPES.
   - *Missing information.*
     - Define the "Edge 0.5" probabilistic roughness model, which is used in Fig. 4 but never described.
     - State when and where the AFM was performed: same cleave, post-ARPES, after air exposure, scan area and pixel size.
   - *Wording.* Rephrase the abstract to "roughness resolvable by AFM."

7. **Intrinsic alternatives to disorder are not discussed against the data.**
   - The introduction motivates the work by predicted instabilities of the flat band: TRSB splitting, phase crystals, edge ferromagnetism (Refs. 20–32).
   - A spontaneous splitting of a few meV would move weight to ±δ. Chakraborty et al. (Ref. 26) argue that the phase crystal is robust to disorder.
   - The authors should state whether their EDCs constrain a split peak (e.g. at −δ with δ ≈ 2–5 meV) and how. Otherwise "disorder as the key factor" excludes alternatives without testing them.

8. **Photoemission matrix elements and polarization are not reported for the (110) data (Methods III B).**
   - Polarization is given only for the (001) reference (LH, 65 eV).
   - The surface state's electron component and the d_{x²−y²} orbital character relative to the measurement geometry could suppress intensity in specific cuts.
   - The authors should report the polarization used, and ideally show LH/LV and a second antinodal-kz photon energy (the next Brillouin zone), so that a matrix-element null is excluded.

9. **Reconciliation with tunneling is qualitative (Sec. II E).**
   - ARPES averaging dilutes but does not eliminate signal from segments carrying the surface state.
   - The authors should estimate what fractional surface coverage, or what spectral weight per segment, would fall below their detection limit (Concern 4).
   - Planar tunnel junctions also average over large areas yet show ZBCPs. The suggestion that disorder pushes states below the top layer relies on a Bi₂Se₃ result (Ref. 76) whose relevance to Andreev states should be justified or demonstrated in the slab model.

10. **Reproducibility and spatial characterization.** The paper should report:
    - the number of successful cleaves;
    - whether the results were checked at several beam positions across the ~150 μm cleave;
    - surface ageing over the measurement time.

    Fig. 11 is helpful but lacks the cut and kz information, and reports no Tc or doping characterization for that sample.

11. **Internal inconsistencies in kz broadening and the TPP-2M input.**
    - Fig. 3 caption: Δk[110] = 0.2 Å⁻¹ from λ ≈ 5 Å.
    - Sec. II C and Methods III D: 2/λ ≈ 0.4 Å⁻¹.
    - The authors should clarify which value (FWHM or HWHM) entered which calculation.
    - TPP-2M's band-gap parameter refers to the electronic band gap. Inserting the 20 meV superconducting gap is not meaningful (for a metal, Eg ≈ 0). The effect is small, but the choice should be corrected or justified.
    - The statement that the simulation "overestimates" kz broadening (Fig. 3c) because TPP-2M underestimates λ conflicts with the later attribution to aliasing and finite-size leakage (Methods III E). The authors should settle on one explanation.

**C. Minor**

12. Fig. 4b: the x-axis "r[111] (Å⁻¹)" should be distance (Å) along [11].
13. The Fig. 4 caption labels (C, GE_AFM, GE0.5, BA) do not match the panel legends (Clean, Edge AFM, Edge 0.5, Mixed).
14. Units of V ("0.153 meV" producing a 24 meV gap) need checking; specify the hopping values used from Ref. 83.
15. Methods III C: "kF = −0.4605 Å" should be Å⁻¹.
16. "2 amt" should be atm; "40˘200 eV" should be 40–200 eV.
17. Supp. VI A: "d_k: S¹ → ℝ\{0}" should be ℝ²\{0}. Eq. (18) mixes δ and a; the normalization expression has a stray sign and labelling.
18. Calling vᵢσ_z a "mass term" is misleading in the particle–hole basis, where it is a chemical-potential (scalar potential) disorder. Please clarify.
19. Fig. 2b: give the shielding fraction and Tc onset values. Specify ZFC fields and sample masses.
20. Figs. 12–13: add DOS units and the depth (r) range explicitly. State which plane corresponds to the probed depth.
21. Specify the ion species of the FIB (the acknowledgments mention PFIB, presumably Xe) and the notch geometry.
22. The Data Availability section is empty.
23. The temperature dependence in Fig. 4g that is attributed to "extrinsic effects" should be supported by showing the gold-edge stability over the same time window.

**4. Suggestions for improvement**

1. **Orientation and doping controls.** Measure an FIB-cleaved (100) side surface and, if possible, a second doping (e.g. x ≈ 0.15–0.17 with a larger gap). This is the most direct way to validate both the surface quality and the pair-breaking interpretation.
2. **Probing-depth dependence.** Repeat antinodal EDCs at soft-X-ray energies (longer λ). Surface-confined versus bulk-like contributions to the near-E_F weight should then scale differentially, testing the "dead layer" and "pushed-below-surface" scenarios.
3. **Spatially resolved probes.** The authors' own argument predicts spatially fragmented edge states. Nano-/μ-ARPES (sub-μm spot) or in-situ STM on larger FIB-cleaved specimens would directly test it, which beam-averaged ARPES cannot.
4. **Test the disorder hypothesis experimentally.** Use a controlled disorder series (e.g. electron irradiation of LSCO, or comparison with a cleaner, more stoichiometric cuprate such as ortho-II YBCO or Hg1201). Pair it with simulations of realistic dopant potentials, so that "disorder is the key factor" becomes a falsifiable prediction rather than a fitted explanation.
