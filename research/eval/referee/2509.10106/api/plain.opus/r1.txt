# Referee Report

**Manuscript:** "Resolving the Bulk-Boundary Correspondence Paradox on Low-Symmetry Surfaces of Weyl Semimetals" (Li, Yang et al., arXiv:2509.10106)

---

## 1. Summary of the submission

The authors report ARPES measurements on a cut-and-cleaved (103) facet of the non-centrosymmetric Weyl semimetal NdAlSi, supported by DFT, semi-infinite Green's-function surface calculations, and six-layer slab calculations. They make four main claims:

1. The (103) surface presents an "apparent paradox" of the bulk-boundary correspondence (BBC). Weyl points projected from the first bulk BZ do not share the periodicity of the surface BZ (SBZ) that governs the Fermi arcs.
2. The paradox is resolved by including projections from higher-order bulk BZs. These accumulate into a superlattice commensurate with the SBZ, governed by an "LCM criterion" N_min = lcm[den(a₁), den(a₂)], with N_min = 3 for (103). A "new SBZ" one-third the size of the "theoretical SBZ" emerges as a momentum-space moiré effect and matches experiment.
3. Disorder-induced suppression of coherent surface states provides a "filter" that enables unambiguous identification of surface-bulk resonance states (SBRSs). It also distinguishes these from surface states (SSs), surface-bulk projected states (SBPSs), and bulk states (BSs).
4. Fermi arcs "from different bulk BZs" hybridize into closed surface Fermi arc loops (SFALs). This "relaxes" the equality between the Chern number on a surface loop and the enclosed projected chirality.

The experimental effort is real and potentially valuable. ARPES on a non-natural facet of a RAlSi compound is uncommon, and the photon-energy, polarization, and termination-dependent datasets are extensive. However, I find that the central theoretical framing (claims 1, 2, and 4) rests on a misidentification of the surface Brillouin zone and on several mathematically incorrect statements. Once the SBZ is defined correctly, there is no paradox to resolve. The experimental claims (3, and the arc assignment) are plausible but not yet established at the level the text asserts.

---

## 2. Major issues

### 2.1 The "paradox" stems from a misdefined SBZ; the projected reciprocal lattice *is* the surface reciprocal lattice

For any rational lattice plane (hkl), the following is a standard duality result. The surface reciprocal lattice (the dual of the 2D lattice of real-space lattice points lying in the plane) equals the orthogonal projection of the full 3D reciprocal lattice onto the plane:

$$L_s^* = P(L^*).$$

The inclusion ⊇ is immediate. If R lies in the plane, then PG·R = G·R ∈ 2πℤ.

It follows at once that the complete set of projected Weyl points, P(W + L*) = P(W) + L_s*, is automatically periodic with the true SBZ. Weyl points and Fermi arcs therefore always share the same periodicity. A mismatch can only appear if one:

- projects Weyl points from a single Wigner–Seitz cell rather than from the periodic reciprocal space, or
- compares against a 2D cell that is not the primitive SBZ.

The manuscript does both.

**The authors' own algebra demonstrates the point.** In SM Sec. 6 they define the "SBZ primitive basis" as g₁ = Pb₁, g₂ = Pb₂. However, applying P to G = hb₁ + kb₂ + lb₃ gives

$$Pb_3 = -(h/l)\,Pb_1 - (k/l)\,Pb_2.$$

The projected lattice is therefore generated by Pb₁, Pb₂ *and* Pb₃. Hence span{Pb₁, Pb₂} is a sublattice of index |l| (for coprime hkl in a primitive lattice), not the surface reciprocal lattice. For (103) the true SBZ is one-third the size of the cell spanned by Pb₁, Pb₂.

This is exactly the "new SBZ" the authors find "emerges" and agrees with experiment. The purple "theoretical SBZ" in Fig. 4 is a 3× supercell of the actual SBZ. The "emergent" green zone is simply the correct SBZ, which standard theory predicts without any moiré interpretation.

**A volume check makes this transparent.** V_BZ = A_SBZ × |G_hkl|, where G_hkl is the primitive reciprocal vector normal to the facet. The authors should compute the area of their purple cell and of the green cell and compare with V_BZ/|G_103|.

Consequently, the following are not needed to reconcile bulk and surface periodicity:

- the "LCM criterion",
- the "2π/3 phase between three nonequivalent surface unit cells", and
- the "momentum-space moiré" picture.

N_min as defined is just the index of an incorrectly chosen sublattice.

### 2.2 The relation for (a₁, a₂) is general, so the claimed metric dependence and "N_min = ∞" are incorrect

The SM states that a₁ = −h/l, a₂ = −k/l holds only for orthogonal lattices. It further claims that for hexagonal, rhombohedral, or monoclinic lattices the coefficients may be irrational, giving N_min = ∞ and "genuine metric dependence."

This is wrong. The relation Pb₃ = −(h/l)Pb₁ − (k/l)Pb₂ follows purely from linearity of P and PG = 0, for any lattice. For any facet with integer Miller indices the coefficients are rational. Irrational ratios correspond only to non-lattice planes, which are not crystal facets in the usual sense.

This undermines both the claimed "universality" and the proposed bridge to quasicrystals.

### 2.3 "Exact reconstruction only in the infinite-projection limit" is incorrect, and the 2N_min argument is mathematically flawed

The SM argues that no finite number of bulk BZs reproduces the surface spectrum and that only the infinite limit restores periodicity. In fact, one full period of k⊥ (length |G_hkl|) over the SBZ forms a prism that is a perfectly valid primitive cell of the reciprocal lattice. Integrating over it captures the complete projected spectrum exactly. "Multiple BZs" appear only because the Wigner–Seitz cell is used as the fundamental domain instead of the prism adapted to the facet.

The finite-cutoff argument in Sec. 6d is also incorrect. It requires a finite set Ω with Ω = Ω + N_min. No nonempty finite set of integers is invariant under a nonzero translation, so "Ω_min = {1, …, 2N_min}" does not satisfy the stated condition. The M_min = 6 result should be removed or rederived.

### 2.4 Crystallographic errors in the treatment of the body-centered lattice

- The SM states that NdAlSi is "body-centered cubic (bcc) in real space" with an fcc reciprocal lattice. NdAlSi is body-centered **tetragonal** (I4₁md), and its reciprocal lattice is also body-centered tetragonal.
- The stated "all-even/all-odd" indexing rule is the fcc real-space reflection condition. The I-centering condition is h + k + l = even.
- The conventional vectors b₁ = 2π/a x̂, b₃ = 2π/c ẑ used in Sec. 6 do not all belong to the true reciprocal lattice. The derivation of a₁ = −1/3 is therefore performed in a non-primitive basis. The subsequent redefinitions (g₁ ≡ 2g₁′, b₁ ≡ 2b₁′, b₃ ≡ 2b₃′) are ad hoc and hard to follow.
- The equation "3 × (2/3 g₁′) = 2 g₁" appears to mix primed and unprimed quantities.

The authors should redo the analysis with the primitive bct reciprocal vectors and explicitly derive the true (103) SBZ, including whether it is rectangular or centered-rectangular. They should then compare with the green cell extracted from experiment. I expect this to reproduce the observed periodicity directly.

### 2.5 The SFAL / "relaxed BBC" claims contradict a theorem

The SM states that, owing to hybridization, "the strict equality between the Chern number enclosed by a surface loop and the net projected chiral charge is relaxed," while "the bulk topology itself remains intact." These two statements are incompatible.

A closed loop in the SBZ defines a closed 2D manifold (a cylinder, compactified along k⊥) in the bulk BZ. Its Chern number equals the net chirality of Weyl points inside it. The net chiral count of surface-state crossings along that loop must equal this Chern number. Surface-state hybridization cannot alter this, because it is a bulk-determined invariant.

There is also a conceptual problem with "arcs originating from different bulk BZs" that "project with mismatched positions" and hybridize. The semi-infinite surface has a single set of surface eigenstates defined on the SBZ. Replicas in an extended-zone plot are the same states, not distinct arcs that can couple.

Closed surface contours and changes in arc connectivity on Weyl surfaces are well documented, for example when opposite-chirality Weyl points project onto nearly the same k∥. The authors should either:

- explain SFALs within the standard picture, or
- provide a concrete model showing new physics.

**Requested evidence:**

- For loops L1–L4 (Fig. S15g–j), explicitly count the chiral crossings, with the sign of the Fermi velocity, following the criteria of Belopolski et al. (PRL 116, 066802), which the authors cite.
- Compare these counts with the enclosed projected chiralities computed from the correctly folded Weyl-point set.

As it stands, the caption statement that the loop cuts "indicate nonzero Chern number" is unsupported by any shown counting.

### 2.6 Statements about slab versus Green's-function methods are misattributed

The claim that slab calculations "truncate bulk projections" and therefore fail specifically on low-symmetry facets is not correct. A slab built with the correct in-plane surface cell has the correct SBZ periodicity by construction.

The poor agreement of a **six-layer** slab (Fig. S9) is a thickness and convergence issue: coarse k⊥ quantization, plus top–bottom surface hybridization. It is not a property of the facet orientation.

The claim that Green's-function calculations "include infinitely many bulk BZs" should also be rephrased. They include the full k⊥ continuum, which corresponds to one reciprocal-lattice volume.

The asserted general rule (slabs suffice for high-symmetry facets; Green's functions are indispensable for low-symmetry ones) should be removed unless the authors show converged slab results, at least 20–40 layers, that still fail.

## 3. Experimental concerns

### 3.1 Surface quality and structure

The XRD θ–2θ scan (Fig. 1d) confirms the bulk orientation of the cut, not the atomic structure of the cleaved surface. Because the paper's key observable is surface periodicity, the following should be addressed:

- **Direct structural evidence.** LEED and/or STM on the measured surface should rule out reconstruction or microfaceting into natural cleavage planes, e.g., (001) or (101). Either could produce spurious periodicities or superposed spectra.
- **Characterization of the regions.** The "well-defined" versus "disordered/uneven" regions should be quantified, for example by spatial maps, roughness, or core-level linewidths.
- **Effect of unevenness.** On "uneven" regions, a distribution of local surface normals smears the k∥ mapping. This effect should be distinguished from intrinsic suppression of surface states.

### 3.2 Physical basis of the SS / SBPS / BS / SBRS taxonomy

SBPSs and BSs are the same bulk Bloch states. They differ only in how well k⊥ is resolved by the measurement, which depends on photoelectron escape depth, final-state broadening, and disorder. Presenting them as distinct state classes is misleading.

Moreover, the reported ordering seems counterintuitive. Moderate disorder yields k⊥-integrated (SBPS) spectra, while strong disorder restores k⊥-resolved bulk dispersion (Fig. 3e, Fig. S5c–d). One would naively expect stronger disorder to *increase* k⊥ broadening. A physical explanation is required.

The SBRS identification currently relies on qualitative statements ("broader", "persists across photon energies"). The following would substantiate the claim:

- quantitative linewidth (MDC) analysis,
- layer-resolved spectral weight from the Green's-function calculation, showing partial surface localization at the SBRS momenta, and
- a comparison of SBRS dispersion with the edge of the projected bulk continuum.

The claim of "direct and unambiguous identification" is too strong for the present evidence.

### 3.3 k⊥ determination

The inner potential and the photon-energy-to-k⊥ conversion are not given. The k⊥ ranges spanned by 85–205 eV should be stated relative to |G₁₀₃|.

The Fig. 3 caption asserts that "denser sampling would reveal exact repetitions." This should be demonstrated, at least in DFT. The in-plane displacement of features versus k⊥ follows trivially from the tilt of constant-k⊥ planes relative to the crystal axes, and it can be plotted quantitatively.

### 3.4 Magnetic state and Weyl-point identification

Measurements at about 8 K (Diamond) lie very close to the ordering temperature of NdAlSi (about 7.2–7.4 K). Short-range magnetic correlations and sample heating under the beam should be discussed.

The authors' earlier work (Ref. 13) attributes some Weyl fermions to ferrimagnetism. It should therefore be clarified:

- which Weyl nodes are present in the paramagnetic phase,
- their energies relative to E_F, and
- how the projected points overlaid on Fermi-level maps (Figs. 1c, 4, S16) account for nodes away from E_F.

### 3.5 Fermi-arc evidence

The arc assignment rests on a few cuts (Fig. S15) and visual comparison with Fig. S16c. The latter is obtained by subtracting SBPS from total spectra, a procedure that needs justification: are the two spectra on a common scale, and is subtraction meaningful for a hybridized resonance? Stronger evidence would include:

- constant-energy contours showing arc terminations tracking projected Weyl points, and
- chiral-crossing counting.

The authors acknowledge that SFALs are not unambiguously demonstrated. The abstract and conclusions should be adjusted accordingly.

## 4. Minor issues

- **SOC in VASP.** The Methods state that SOC was "included as a second-order perturbation," but VASP treats SOC in the noncollinear Hamiltonian, not perturbatively. Please clarify.
- **Termination count.** The Methods mention "four possible terminations," whereas the SM refers to four cleavage planes and eight terminations. The terminology for the selected termination ("Si-Al plane", "Si terminated at Al-Si layer") should be made consistent.
- **Typos.** "BCC paradox" (main text p. 6) should read BBC; "more bulk BZ projections are need" (Fig. 4 caption); "featuresreveal" (Fig. S15).
- **References.**
  - Ref. 21: "SraAl₄" should be SrAl₄.
  - Ref. 29: the first author is Sánchez-Barriga, and the year is 2016.
  - Ref. 4: please check the volume/article number.
- **Unused references.** Refs. 48–49 (DFPT, Quantum ESPRESSO) are not cited in the text.
- **Fig. 1e/f.** The text states that the flat-region map at 150 eV is surface-dominated. Since 150 eV is in the bulk-sensitive range used in Fig. 3, the evidence from Fig. S1 should be referenced explicitly where the claim is made.
- **Data availability.** The raw ARPES data and calculation inputs should be deposited in a public repository.
- **Tone.** Expressions such as "inaugurate a new frontier," "universal criterion," and the extensions to quasicrystals and non-centrosymmetric superconductors are not supported by the results. The quasicrystal analogy is also confused: cut-and-project from a higher-dimensional lattice is the relevant concept there, not BZ stacking.

## 5. Assessment and recommendation

**Strengths:**

- An experimentally demanding measurement on a rarely accessed facet.
- Extensive photon-energy, polarization, and surface-condition datasets.
- A careful survey of terminations in the surface calculations.
- Reasonable overall DFT/ARPES agreement for the Si-terminated surface.

**Weaknesses:** The central conceptual contribution does not hold.

- The "BBC paradox" arises from using span{Pb₁, Pb₂} as the SBZ. The correctly defined SBZ (the projection of the full reciprocal lattice) is exactly the "emergent" zone observed experimentally.
- Several supporting statements are mathematically incorrect, as detailed in Sec. 2:
  - irrational coefficients for lattice facets,
  - the infinite-projection requirement,
  - the invariant finite cutoff set, and
  - the "relaxed" Chern-number equality.
- The lattice type is misidentified.

**Recommendation:** major revision with substantial reframing. In its current form I cannot recommend publication. A revised manuscript could be valuable if it:

1. presents the (103) ARPES data as a characterization of a low-symmetry facet,
2. uses the standard, correctly defined SBZ throughout,
3. provides structural characterization of the surface, and
4. supports the SBRS and Fermi-arc assignments with quantitative analysis, including chiral-crossing counting.
