Review outline:

## 1. Significance and novelty

The manuscript applies operando Bragg XPCS to micron-scale VO₂ two-terminal devices. It introduces a stroboscopic scheme that correlates speckle patterns recorded only during the On phase of repeated switching cycles. The authors report four results:
- Sub-threshold currents slowly erase the pre-existing domain configuration.
- Above-threshold switching erases it within 1 s and produces a long-lived On configuration.
- The stretching exponent β changes from ~1 to ~1.5 after current is applied, which they call a "jamming transition".
- Hundreds of cycles raise the cycle-to-cycle speckle correlation, which they read as a crossover from stochastic to deterministic filament formation.

Using coherent X-rays to track whether a resistive-switching device "remembers" its nanoscale configuration across cycles is new and relevant to neuromorphic oxides. In its current form, however, the headline link between speckle correlation, jamming and electrical switching stochasticity is inferred rather than demonstrated.

## 2. Potential reasons for acceptance

- **New method.** Synchronised, cycle-resolved Bragg XPCS on a working device is new. It could become a general tool for assessing cycle-to-cycle reproducibility in phase-change and memristive materials.
- **Long-duration data.** The two-time correlation maps span hours, with clear qualitative contrasts between the three protocols in Fig. 3a–c: delayed decorrelation under sub-threshold current, abrupt decorrelation at switching, and increasing cycle-to-cycle correlation.
- **Relevant question.** Whether nanoscale configuration governs filament stochasticity matters for VO₂ neuromorphic devices. The finding connects to known ramp-reversal memory and persistent metallic domains (refs. 9–11, 45, 46).
- **Claimed reproduction.** A second, pristine device reportedly reproduces the stochastic-to-deterministic crossover (SI).

## 3. Concerns

### A. Must address

**1. Switching stochasticity is never measured electrically, and the "erase and recover stochastic switching" claim is not demonstrated.**

The title, abstract and conclusion state three things:
- Repetitive switching makes switching deterministic.
- Sub-threshold currents "erase the short-term memory … recovering stochastic switching".
- This enables train/reset operation.

The only evidence for determinism is ΔG(n, n+1) in Fig. 3i. No electrical observable of stochasticity is reported versus cycle number, such as:
- the distribution of threshold voltage or current,
- switching delay,
- On-state resistance,
- the I1/I2 multi-step structure of Fig. 5b.

There is also no experiment of the form train → sub-threshold erase → re-cycle showing that low correlation returns. The sub-threshold experiment (Fig. 3a) was done on an untrained device and was not followed by switching.

The authors should log V(t) and I(t) for every cycle of the Fig. 3c run and show the threshold/delay statistics against ΔG(n, n+1). They should also perform at least one full train–erase–retrain sequence. Without this, the neuromorphic conclusions in the abstract and in the last paragraph of the Discussion are speculative.

**2. Instrumental and thermal explanations for the rising cycle-to-cycle correlation (Fig. 3c, 3i) are not excluded.**

Low ΔG over the first ~200 cycles (~600–1000 s), followed by a rise and plateau, is also what one expects if:
- the sample, holder or substrate is settling thermally after the start of a 3 s on/off Joule-heating protocol (8 mW at 4 mA, from Fig. 5b),
- the sample position or Bragg angle is drifting during that settling,
- the beam is slowly modifying the film.

The same ~10³ s scale appears in the 0.26 mA erasure. Needed controls:
- **(a)** Correlation between consecutive Off-state frames recorded in the same run. If Off states also become more correlated, the effect is not specific to filament formation.
- **(b)** The same cycling protocol with a sub-threshold amplitude, which matches heating roughly but forms no filament.
- **(c)** A beam-on, no-bias run of the same length.
- **(d)** Tracking of Bragg peak centroid, width and integrated intensity against cycle number. This would reveal thermal expansion, tilt or drift.
- **(e)** Repetition of the cycling run after a reset, on the same and on the second device.

In addition, ΔG is shown normalised to [0, 1], which hides its absolute level. The authors should show the absolute G(n, n+1) and compare it with two references:
- the static On-state correlation at the same 6 s lag and 1 s exposure, from Fig. 3b;
- the counting-statistics noise floor.

This would show whether "stochastic" cycles are fully uncorrelated or only slightly less correlated. It would also allow the plateau to be called "deterministic" quantitatively.

**3. The jamming claim rests on β values that the fitting procedure is unlikely to constrain.**

The Methods state that a single τ is fitted for all t_age within a measurement, and the reported τ far exceeds the observation window:
- 35,000 s, 160,000 s and 81,000 s, against windows of ~3,000–10,000 s;
- the g2 decays in Fig. 3h are only a few percent of the contrast.

In the regime t ≪ τ, exp[−(t/τ)^β] ≈ 1 − (t/τ)^β, so τ, β and the contrast A are strongly degenerate. Fixing τ across t_age forces any evolution of the dynamics into β. A slowing or speeding of τ with age would therefore appear as a drift in β. The rise of β from 1 to 1.5 in Fig. 4 may be a fitting artefact rather than a change in the nature of the dynamics.

The authors should:
- refit with τ and β free for each t_age;
- report the τ–β covariance and how the error bars in Fig. 4 were obtained;
- state whether A is fixed (from the static 298 K data?) or free;
- show normalised |F(t)|² on a scale that makes the curvature visible.

The most diagnostic test is the q-dependence of the relaxation rate, available from different detector regions around the Bragg peak:
- Γ ∝ q for ballistic, stress-driven dynamics, which usually accompany compressed exponentials (Cipelletti et al.);
- Γ ∝ q² for diffusion.

Without this test, "liquid-like" versus "jammed" is a label on a fit parameter.

**4. The construction and normalisation of the correlation function, and what the speckle represents, need clarification.**

*Normalisation.* Eq. (1) normalises I(t1)I(t2) by the time-averaged ⟨I²⟩. It omits the usual pixel average over a region of interest and the symmetric normalisation ⟨I(t1)I(t2)⟩_q / (⟨I(t1)⟩_q⟨I(t2)⟩_q). With the stated normalisation, any change in total diffracted intensity changes G even if the speckle does not rearrange. Switching changes the M1/R phase fraction and hence the Bragg intensity. The authors should specify:
- the region of interest and its q-range relative to the peak;
- the normalisation actually used.

They should show that the drops in G at t = 0 in Fig. 3b and after ~1000 s in Fig. 3a survive the standard normalisation.

*Which reflection.* It should be stated whether the detected signal comes from:
- the M1 (200) reflection,
- the R (011) reflection,
- or both on the same detector, given that they are not at identical 2θ.

The authors should also show how the peak position and intensity change on switching. This would establish that the measured volume responds to filament formation and not only to heating.

*Footprint.* A 7.4 × 14.8 µm² beam at ~25° incidence illuminates an area several times the 25 µm² device, including film under and beyond the 100 nm Au electrodes, which is partly transparent at 6 keV. A sub-micron to micron-wide filament is likely a few percent of the scattering volume.

The authors should estimate:
- the fraction of scattering from the biased region;
- how much decorrelation a displaced filament of realistic width would produce, ideally with a simple speckle simulation like that in Fig. 1a, b.

Otherwise it is unclear whether high correlation means "the same filament" or merely "a dominant unchanged background".

### B. Should address

**5. The Arrhenius analysis and the comparison with the melting point are not robust.**

- The inset of Fig. 2d appears to contain very few points. The two τ values quoted (81,000 s at 298 K and 24,000 s at 340 K) alone give E_a/k ≈ 2.9 × 10³ K, so the fit may in effect be two-point.
- The 298 K value is described as "the slowest observable dynamics in our material or the experimental stability limit", so it may be instrument-limited.
- The 340 K data are at the phase transition and visibly non-stationary along the diagonal (Fig. 2b), which violates the assumptions behind a single activated rate.
- The text gives "8000 ± 3000 s" while the figure gives 81,000 ± 3000 s.
- Comparing an activation barrier to the melting temperature in kelvin has no physical basis as argued. A barrier of k × 2700 K is not "melting".

The authors should:
- measure several temperatures well below the MIT, including 313 K without current, which is also needed for concern 7;
- report the number of points and the fit uncertainties;
- discuss E_a in terms of domain-wall or nucleation barriers, or strain energies, from the VO₂ literature.

**6. The Joule-heating interpretation contradicts the paper's own numbers, and the device temperature is unknown.**

The Discussion argues that current-driven dynamics are comparable to thermally activated ones and hence probably heating-driven. Yet the 4 mA On-state τ (150,000–160,000 s) is *slower* than the 298 K no-current τ (81,000 s), even though the device dissipates ~8 mW and the base temperature is 313 K. The argument that larger currents give "larger phase space" and therefore a longer t_jam is also qualitative.

The authors should:
- estimate the local temperature, for example from the Bragg-peak shift as a lattice thermometer or from finite-element modelling;
- compare τ under current with τ at a matched stage temperature without current;
- discuss explicitly why heating would slow the dynamics. Possible reasons are pinning by the metallic filament or strain clamping.

**7. The sub-threshold "erasure" result needs a baseline and reproduction.**

There is no no-current 313 K reference for the same duration. Extrapolating the authors' Arrhenius law gives τ(313 K) ≈ 5 × 10⁴ s, comparable to the τ = 35,000 ± 2,000 s fitted with 0.26 mA (Fig. 3g). On these numbers it is not clear that the current changes the dynamics at all.

The ~1000 s delayed onset is observed once. The authors should:
- repeat the 0.26 mA step, including on the second device and at more than one amplitude;
- show the 313 K zero-current control;
- state the resistance and dissipated power at 0.26 mA. The 0.26 mA power, ~0.2 mW, should be compared with the 4 mA case.

**8. The t_jam versus current trend and the "for all currents" statement overstate Fig. 4.**

- At 0.26 mA and 1.7 mA, t_jam = 1100 ± 600 s and 1200 ± 500 s, which are indistinguishable. The "larger current, slower transition" trend therefore rests on the 4 mA point alone, at 4000 ± 3000 s.
- The 4 mA β never plateaus at 1.5 within the 7000 s window, so the statement that "for all currents a transition from β = 1 to β = 1.5 is observed" is not supported.
- The 0.26 mA case is sub-threshold, so it does not occur "after electrical switching".
- The 1.7 mA data appear nowhere else. Its relation to I1 and I2 in Fig. 5b at 313 K should be stated.
- The shear-thickening analogy should be removed, or supported with at least three or four currents and repeat runs.

**9. Sample statistics, device-to-device spread and the role of irradiation are thin.**

All electrical-switching XPCS data come from one irradiated device, and each protocol appears to have been run once. The pristine device is relegated to the SI with "equivalent results", but irradiation was introduced specifically to localise the filament (refs. 32, 7). One would expect the two devices to differ in initial stochasticity or in the number of cycles needed to "learn".

The authors should:
- show both devices side by side in the main text;
- give the number of independent cycling runs;
- report the spread in the crossover cycle number.

There is also an inconsistency to resolve. The main text says "a central 5 × 5 µm region irradiated", while the Methods say "devices were globally irradiated".

**10. The identity of the "domains" is not established.**

R_d = 2π/Q_FWHM ≈ 20 nm is obtained at room temperature, where the film is essentially all M1. In a 45 nm sputtered film the Bragg width also reflects:
- finite thickness,
- grain size,
- mosaicity,
- inhomogeneous strain.

The speckle and its dynamics may therefore be dominated by structural grains or strain domains rather than by M1/R phase domains. The authors should:
- compare R_d with the grain size from AFM or SEM and with the film thickness;
- separate in-plane and out-of-plane widths;
- temper the identification of the dynamics as "nano-domain reconfiguration".

**11. Possible X-ray beam effects are not addressed.**

A flux of 2 × 10¹⁰ ph/s at 6 keV over many hours on an oxide film can create defects or change oxygen stoichiometry, and could contribute to slow "aging" or "learning". The authors should state the dose, and either test a different flux or attenuation, or show that unexposed regions or intermittently exposed runs behave the same.

**12. The jamming and glass interpretation needs qualification and supporting signatures.**

Compressed exponents (β > 1) are generally attributed to ballistic, stress-relaxation dynamics in out-of-equilibrium solids. Classic glassy dynamics are usually stretched (β < 1). Labelling β = 1 as "liquid-like" and β = 1.5 as "jammed/glassy" requires:
- explicit references, for example Cipelletti and co-workers, and Ruta et al. on metallic glasses;
- care over terminology.

Supporting evidence would help, for example:
- the q-dependence of the rate (concern 3);
- dynamical heterogeneity, such as two-time fluctuations or χ₄-like variance;
- intermittency.

The statement that jamming in "hard matter" implies granular-like dynamics should be toned down. The domains are clamped and strain-coupled within an epitaxial film.

### C. Minor

13. **Text–figure mismatches:**
    - 8000 vs 81,000 s at 298 K (see concern 5);
    - On-state τ ≈ 150,000 ± 50,000 s in the text vs 160,000 ± 50,000 s in Fig. 3h;
    - 0.27 mA in the text vs 0.26 mA in Fig. 4 and Fig. 3;
    - "The inset (orange data)" should read Fig. 3i; there is no inset;
    - the Fig. 1 caption refers to c and a "bottom panel", but the figure has panels c–e.
14. **Definition of the age axis.** Lines perpendicular to t1 = t2 passing through t1 = t2 = t_age satisfy t1 + t2 = 2t_age, not t_age.
15. **Notation in Eq. (2).** The dummy variable τ clashes with the relaxation time τ. Eq. (3) is called a "single exponential decay" but is a stretched or compressed exponential.
16. **Cycling protocol.** The duty cycle is ambiguous ("turned on and off for 3 s"). The top axis of Fig. 3c (~1800 cycles in ~5400 s) implies a 3 s period. The authors should state the on and off times and when within the On phase the 1 s exposure starts relative to the current edge, since thermal transients matter.
17. **Overstatement in the Methods.** "At 4 mA … the measured device region is fully switched to the On state" overstates the situation, because a filament does not switch the whole region. The temperature at which Fig. 5b was measured should also be given.
18. **Unrealistic uncertainties.** Uncertainties such as ±3000 s on τ = 81,000 s, and ±100 s on 24,000 s, look unrealistically small for decays observed over a fraction of τ. State how they were derived.
19. **Abstract versus results.** The abstract's "slow nano-domain reconfiguration occurs on timescales of thousands of seconds" sits uneasily with On-state τ ~ 10⁵ s. Clarify which timescale (t_jam, the erasure onset, or τ) is meant.
20. **Indexing.** Specify the full reflection indexing and the film orientation on r-sapphire.

## 4. Suggestions for improvement

1. **Combine XPCS with simultaneous electrical statistics.** Record per-cycle threshold, delay and On-resistance during the cycling run. Add a train → sub-threshold erase → retrain sequence with matched Off-state and sub-threshold-cycling controls. This would turn the central claim from inferred to demonstrated.
2. **Exploit the q-resolution of the area detector.** Extract Γ(q) and β(q) around the Bragg peak, fitting τ freely at each t_age. This would distinguish ballistic from diffusive dynamics and support or replace the jamming interpretation.
3. **Quantify what the speckle measures.**
   - Model the illuminated footprint, including electrode-covered film.
   - Simulate the decorrelation expected from filament displacement of realistic width.
   - If possible, image the same devices in real space before and after training, using nano-diffraction or Bragg ptychography.
4. **Add temperature calibration and baselines.** Use a Bragg-peak lattice thermometer under bias, and run zero-current XPCS at 313 K and at the estimated effective device temperature. This would test the Joule-heating picture and anchor the Arrhenius analysis.
